# Periodic order of the elements: forms, ordering principles, trends, disputed placements and the nuclear frontier (state as of 23 October 2026)

**Label key.** [OBS] observing · [REC] reconstruction from observings · [CONV] convention or definition · [MOD] model construct · [SIM] simulation.

In each entry, "Observed:" names what an instrument or count registered, and "Field adds:" names the model, convention or interpretation the field layers on top.

**[unverified]** means the claim was not checked against a page opened in this session. The session's shared web-search budget ran out after the first round of eight searches, and pages not returned by those searches were blocked. Such items come from background knowledge and need checking before use.

"Prior" marks a superseded state.

## Key gathered points

- **Group 3 is still formally a convention.**
  - An IUPAC task group (chair E. Scerri, announced 2016) issued only a *provisional* report (Chem. Int., 18 Jan 2021).
  - The report called the matter a convention, but proposed Sc–Y–Lu–Lr in a 32-column table.
  - No final IUPAC recommendation was found. IUPAC's own table reportedly still uses the 15-wide "asterisk" layout [unverified].
- **Definitions do not line up.**
  - IUPAC's "lanthanoids" are 15 elements (La–Lu), but the f-block in the Lu form has 14 columns.
  - H sits in group 1 but is not an alkali metal.
  - Group 18's element-based IUPAC name is "helium or neon group".
- **The Madelung (n+ℓ) rule is an empirical regularity.**
  - Derivation attempts (Demkov–Ostrovsky 1971; Allen–Knight 2002; Belokolos 2017; Kholodenko–Kauffman 2019–20) rest on chosen effective potentials, semiclassical approximations or symmetry assumptions.
  - None of them produces the 19–20 ground-state exceptions.
  - Scerri stresses that the filling order across elements is not the ionization order within one atom: Sc is [Ar]3d¹4s², yet loses 4s first.
- **Measured superheavy chemistry reaches Z = 115 (2024).**
  - All of it is atom-at-a-time gas–solid chromatography: decay events are counted, and adsorption enthalpies are derived from them by modelling.
  - Fl (8 atoms at TASCA, 2022): two deposition zones on gold; described as highly volatile, metallic and the least reactive member of group 14.
  - Nh (14 atoms) and Mc (4 atoms), 2024: −ΔH_ads on SiO₂ ≈ 58 and 54 kJ/mol. Both are more reactive than Cn and Fl, and less reactive than Tl and Bi.
  - Measured reactivity alternates with subshell closure: Cn < Nh > Fl < Mc.
  - Og as a ~1.5 eV semiconductor and Cn as a "noble liquid" are 2019 simulations.
  - Lv, Ts and Og have no measured chemistry.
- **No new element since 2010.**
  - RIKEN's ⁵¹V+²⁴⁸Cm element-119 campaign (from 2018) reached its planned sensitivity and was "temporarily concluded" with no event reported (undated TASCA26 abstract).
  - LBNL made Lv with a ⁵⁰Ti beam (2 atoms, July 2024) and is pursuing ⁵⁰Ti+²⁴⁹Cf for element 120. The search was reported under way by Sept 2025, and an ORNL talk on the Cf target is dated 2 Sept 2026.
  - JINR planned ⁵⁰Ti+²⁴⁹Bk (element 119) and ⁵⁴Cr+²⁴⁸Cm (element 120) from May 2026. By June 2026 the 119 run had reached 480 fb sensitivity, against the ~180–200 fb said to be needed.
  - No claim of element 119 or 120 was found up to 23 Oct 2026. JINR's expected Sept–Oct 2026 results could not be checked.
- **Retracted and unconfirmed claims.**
  - LBNL's 1999 claim of element 118 was retracted in 2001 (data fabrication).
  - GSI's three 2011 signals attributed to element 120 were not confirmed and were later judged random.
- **The "end of the table" is model-dependent.**
  - Z≈137 comes from the Dirac equation with a point nucleus; Z≈170–173 comes from a finite nucleus, where the 1s level "dives" into the negative-energy continuum [unverified].
  - An SO(4,2) model sets no limit at all.
- **Several topics rest on background knowledge only [unverified]:** Kaupp's 2p anomaly; the relativistic accounts of gold's colour, mercury's low melting point and the lead–acid cell voltage; the knight's move; Biron's secondary periodicity; the Oddo–Harkins rule; magic numbers; mirror nuclei; JWP discovery criteria; Goldschmidt, HSAB and biological classes.

## 1. Which forms of the periodic system exist, and which ordering principle does each privilege?

### Takeaway
All modern forms share atomic number Z (a counted integer) as the linear order. They differ in which similarity relation they put side by side:
- chemical valence and behaviour (short form, 18-column);
- unbroken Z-continuity (32-column form, spirals);
- quantum-number architecture (Janet left-step, ADOMAH, SO(4,2) tables).

The IUPAC task group treated the choice of format as a convention for authors, and only the membership of groups as a scientific question.

### Cited Findings

*Shared ordering index*
- **[OBS→CONV] Atomic number [unverified].**
  - Observed: Moseley (1913–14) found that the square root of characteristic X-ray line frequency steps up evenly from element to element. This fixed integer positions, exposed gaps later filled (Z = 43, 61, 72, 75) and resolved atomic-weight inversions (Ar/K, Co/Ni, Te/I).
  - Field adds: Z = nuclear charge = proton count. An element is the class of atoms sharing one Z, which makes Z the membership criterion for every element.
- **[REC] Period lengths reconstructed by counting.**
  - Observed: period closures (noble gases) fall at Z = 2, 10, 18, 36, 54, 86, and period starts (alkali metals) at Z = 1, 3, 11, 19, 37, 55, 87.
  - Field adds: these follow from n+ℓ counting, with period length L_M = 2([M/2]+1)², giving 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32. Hydrogen-like 2n² degeneracy gives 2, 8, 18, 32, which fits only the first two periods — [Belokolos 2017, arXiv:1706.02535](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02535).

*Group numbering and column formats*
- **[CONV] Four numbering schemes coexist.**
  - Mendeleev's I–VIII, with group 0 added later for the noble gases.
  - US/CAS A/B labels.
  - Old-IUPAC (European) A/B labels, assigned differently from CAS.
  - IUPAC 1–18, from the 1988 IUPAC report (Fluck, Pure Appl. Chem. 60:431, doi:10.1351/pac198860030431).
  - The 14 f-block columns carry no group number — [group-names template](https://www.vigyanwiki.in/wiki/Template:Periodic_table_%28group_names%29); [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
- **[CONV] 18- versus 32-column format.** The IUPAC group-3 task group left this choice to authors and educators, and treated group composition as the scientific issue — [Chem. Int. 2016 notice](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/ci-2016-0213/html).
- **[CONV] Short form [unverified].** Mendeleev's 1871 eight-column table, with A/B subgroups, privileged valence (formulae of highest oxides and hydrides). The A/B labels above are its descendants. The medium-long (18-column) form privileges groups with the f-block footnoted; the long (32-column) form keeps Z unbroken.

*Configuration-led forms*
- **[MOD/CONV] Janet left-step table.**
  - Eight periods, each of constant n+ℓ, give period doubling: 2-2-8-8-18-18-32-32.
  - Kholodenko and Kauffman date it to 1930, six years before Madelung's formulation; other accounts date Janet's tables to 1928–29 [unverified] — [arXiv:1906.05278](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.05278v2); [Kholodenko 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.02137v1).
  - It puts He (1s²) in the s-block above Be, with the s-block at the right edge [unverified for Janet's own layout].
- **[MOD] Group-theoretic table, SO(4,2)⊗SU(2) (Kibler's "Mendeleev city").**
  - Places H with the alkali metals and He with the alkaline-earth metals; each row holds 2n² elements.
  - Presents the n+ℓ rule rather than deriving it.
  - Is a priori infinite: it predicts a new period at Z = 121–138 and a superactinide series at Z = 139–152.
  - Rumer & Fet (1971) and Barut (1972) first tied SO(4,2) to the table — [Kibler, quant-ph/0611287](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0611287).
- **[MOD] ADOMAH [unverified].** Valery Tsimmerman (2006) arranges elements in blocks by n, ℓ, m_ℓ and m_s. Its content is equivalent to Janet's table, and it can be folded into a tetrahedron. It privileges quantum numbers.

*Continuity-led, spiral and 3D forms [unverified]*
- Benfey's spiral (Theodor Benfey, 1964, ACS *Chemistry* magazine) winds Z outward from H, with the transition and inner-transition series as bulges. It privileges an unbroken Z sequence over period breaks.
- Precursors and relatives:
  - de Chancourtois's "telluric screw" (1862): atomic weight on a helix around a cylinder;
  - Hinrichs's spiral (1867);
  - Stowe's "physicist's table" (1989), built on quantum-number axes;
  - "Mendeleev flower" and Stewart's "Chemical Galaxy" (2004).
- Order- and network-theoretic treatments (Restrepo and co-workers, 2019 onward) reconstruct the system as a similarity or order structure computed from large compound databases.

*Triads*
- **[REC] Atomic-number triads (arithmetic on counted Z, done here).** Where the two period lengths involved are equal, the middle member of three vertically adjacent elements is the mean of the outer two:
  - Li(3)–Na(11)–K(19): mean 11.
  - He(2)–Ne(10)–Ar(18): mean 10.
  - H(1)–F(9)–Cl(17): mean 9.
  - Y(39)–Lu(71)–Lr(103): mean 71.
  - By contrast, Y(39)–La(57)–Ac(89) gives a mean of 64, not 57.
  - Field adds [unverified]: Döbereiner's triads (1817/1829: Ca–Sr–Ba, Cl–Br–I, Li–Na–K) used atomic weights. Scerri has used Z-triads to argue for H above F and for Lu below Y.

### Inferences
- The forms differ in which relation they make primary, not in their data:
  - chemical analogy (short and 18-column forms);
  - Z-continuity (32-column form, spirals);
  - quantum-number structure (Janet, ADOMAH, SO(4,2)).
  Only the third kind makes period doubling (paired periods of equal length) structurally visible.
- He is the sharpest test of the membership criterion for groups. By configuration (1s², s-block) it sits with the alkaline earths; by closed-shell behaviour it sits with the noble gases.
- The IUPAC split between "format" (convention) and "composition" (science) is the field's own version of stating a membership criterion for each set.

### Gaps
- Benfey, ADOMAH, Stowe, Chemical Galaxy and Restrepo details were not checked: the search budget was exhausted, and the "Alternative periodic tables" page was blocked by the session allowlist.
- No source was checked on whether any 3D or spiral form has institutional status. To my knowledge none has [unverified].

## 2. Disputed placements and their 2026 standing

### Takeaway
- **Group 3.** Sc–Y–Lu–Lr has the specialist literature and a 2021 IUPAC *provisional* report behind it. IUPAC called the matter a convention, and no final recommendation was found. The 15-wide f-block ("asterisk") layout persists in circulation.
- **Other placements remain dependent on the criterion chosen:** f-block width and lanthanoid scope, hydrogen and helium, group 12, and the metalloid list.

### Cited Findings

*Group 3*
- **[CONV] Task group (2016).**
  - On 29 Mar 2016 UCLA announced that Scerri would chair an IUPAC task group to decide between Sc–Y–La–Ac and Sc–Y–Lu–Lr — [UCLA](https://www.chemistry.ucla.edu/?p=14521).
  - Its aims were to recommend group membership and report in Pure Appl. Chem.
  - Interest had revived after the 2015 *Nature* measurement of lawrencium's first ionization energy — [Chem. Int. 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/ci-2016-0213/html).
- **[CONV] Provisional report (current formal state).** "Provisional Report on Discussions on Group 3 of the Periodic Table", Chem. Int. 43(1):31–34, 18 Jan 2021, doi:10.1515/ci-2021-0115 — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element). The IUPAC PDF linked there was not on the session allowlist.
  - It treated the question as a convention, yet suggested the Lu form.
  - Its arguments: only Sc–Y–Lu–Lr keeps Z in order, avoids splitting the d-block into two very unequal parts, and gives block widths of 2, 6, 10 and 14.
  - It noted that some relativistic-quantum specialists favour 15-wide f-blocks for superheavy elements, but argued such specialised interests should not set the general table.
  - No adoption as a formal IUPAC recommendation was found.
- **[CONV] Earlier IUPAC lean (prior).** Holden's presentation at the 1985 IUPAC General Assembly and Fluck's 1988 report favoured Lu. IUPAC publications that expand to 32 columns put Lu and Lr under Y (Leigh 1990, 2009) — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
- **[OBS→MOD] Electron configurations.**
  - Observed (via spectroscopic assignment): Sc [Ar]3d¹4s²; Y [Kr]4d¹5s²; Lu [Xe]4f¹⁴5d¹6s²; Lr [Rn]5f¹⁴7s²7p¹. For Lr, 7p lies below the expected 6d, although the 6d state is close — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element). Lu's configuration is also given in [Öhrström 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/nchem.2938).
  - Field adds: Lu's filled 4f shell is core-like, whereas La and Ac (like Th) have empty f-orbitals that become occupied in compounds. Lr's 7p¹ comes from relativistic calculations: Eliav et al. 1995 (doi:10.1103/PhysRevA.52.291), Zou & Froese Fischer 2002 (doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.88.023001) and Xu & Pyykkö 2016 (doi:10.1039/c6cp02706g), as cited in the same page.
  - [unverified] La is [Xe]5d¹6s² and Ac is [Rn]6d¹7s².
- **[OBS] Lawrencium ionization energy [value unverified].**
  - Sato et al. (Nature 2015) measured surface ionization of single atoms at JAEA: 4.96 (+0.08/−0.07) eV, the lowest of the actinides. This is consistent with a weakly bound 7p₁/₂ electron.
  - Field adds: the configuration is inferred by comparing the measured value with relativistic calculations.
  - That the measurement exists, and that it renewed the dispute: [Chem. Int. 2016](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/ci-2016-0213/html).
- **[OBS] Bulk properties** (Jensen 1982, J. Chem. Educ. 59:634, doi:10.1021/ed059p634; updated in Found. Chem. 17:23, 2015, doi:10.1007/s10698-015-9216-1) — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element):
  - Sc, Y and Lu share metallic crystal structure, oxide and chloride structures, a d-type conduction band and superconductivity behaviour; La differs on each.
  - Melting points: Sc 1814 K, Y 1799 K, Lu 1925 K.
  - Densities: 2.99, 4.47 and 9.84 g cm⁻³.
  - Atomic radii: 162, 180 and 174 pm. Lu is smaller than Y.
  - Historically, double-sulfate solubility separated an "yttrium group" (Sc, Y, Lu) from a "cerium group" (La, Ce…).
- **[MOD] Counter-arguments and replies.**
  - Lavelle (J. Chem. Educ. 85:1482, 2008, doi:10.1021/ed085p1482) kept La and Ac in the d-block because their ground states contain no f-electron.
  - Critics reply that Th (6d²7s²) is undisputedly an f-block element although it has no ground-state f-electron either.
  - Kondō (1963, doi:10.1143/PTP.29.1) and Hamilton (1965, doi:10.1119/1.1972042) tied La's superconductivity to 4f involvement. Landau & Lifshitz called the La placement wrong.
  - Bassett (1892) and Werner (1905) had already left the space below Y open for what proved to be Lu (discovered 1907).
  - Source for all of the above: [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
- **[CONV] How tables show it.**
  - Öhrström (Nature Chem. 10:372, 20 Feb 2018, doi:10.1038/nchem.2938) found many tables, IUPAC's included, vague on group 3 — [Nature Chem](https://www.nature.com/articles/nchem.2938); [full text](https://web-test.research.chalmers.se/publication/500981/file/500981_Fulltext.pdf).
  - Parsons's chapter in *Mendeleev to Oganesson* (OUP, 2018, doi:10.1093/oso/9780190668532.003.0010) reviews the debate — [OUP](https://academic.oup.com/book/40685/chapter/348381736).
  - [unverified] IUPAC's own periodic table (latest version I know of, dated 4 May 2022) shows Sc and Y with the slots below labelled 57–71 and 89–103, i.e., the asterisk form.

*f-block width; lanthanoid and actinoid definitions*
- **[CONV] 14 versus 15 columns.**
  - A blank below Y implies a 15-wide f-block, which conflicts with the 14-electron capacity of an f-subshell.
  - Hamilton placed La–Yb and Ac–No in the f-block, i.e., 14 each, as the Lu form does — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
- **[CONV] Named series.** Lanthanoids are 15 elements (La–Lu); rare-earth metals are Sc, Y plus those 15. Both are IUPAC collective names — [Öhrström 2018](https://web-test.research.chalmers.se/publication/500981/file/500981_Fulltext.pdf).
  - [unverified] The 2005 IUPAC Red Book prefers "lanthanoid"/"actinoid" (actinoids Ac–Lr, 15 elements) to the "-ide" forms, because "-ide" suggests an anion.
  - The named series (15 members) and the f-block row (14 members) are therefore different sets by definition.

*Hydrogen and helium*
- **[CONV]** H is placed in group 1 but is not an alkali metal. Group 18's element-based IUPAC name is "helium or neon group" — [template](https://www.vigyanwiki.in/wiki/Template:Periodic_table_%28group_names%29).
- **[MOD]** The SO(4,2) table places He with the alkaline-earth metals — [Kibler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0611287).
- [unverified] Placements argued for H, by criterion:
  - above Li (ns¹ configuration);
  - above F (one electron short of a closed shell; hydride ion; the H–F–Cl Z-triad);
  - above C (half-filled shell, similar electronegativity; Cronyn, J. Chem. Educ. 80:947, 2003);
  - floating outside the groups.
- [unverified] He chemistry:
  - Observed: inert under ordinary conditions.
  - Observed: Na₂He synthesized in a diamond-anvil cell above ~113 GPa (Dong et al., Nature Chem. 2017).
  - So He compounds are observed only under extreme conditions.

*Group 12 in the d block [unverified]*
- **[CONV]** The IUPAC Gold Book defines a transition element as one with an incomplete d-subshell, or one forming cations with an incomplete d-subshell. Zn, Cd and Hg (d¹⁰ in their common compounds) are d-block by position but not transition elements by this definition (Jensen, J. Chem. Educ. 80:952, 2003).
- **[OBS/MOD, contested]** Wang, Andrews, Riedel and Kaupp (Angew. 2007) claimed HgF₄, i.e., Hg(IV):
  - Observed: matrix-isolation IR bands after laser-ablated Hg reacted with F₂ in solid Ne/Ar at a few kelvin.
  - Field adds: the identification depends on quantum-chemical frequency assignments.
  - To my knowledge the claim has not been independently reproduced.

*Metalloids [unverified]*
- **[CONV]** There is no IUPAC definition. Vernon (J. Chem. Educ. 2013) surveyed 194 published lists: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb and Te are the most frequent; Po, At, Se, C and Al appear sometimes.
- **[SIM]** Condensed astatine is predicted to be monatomic and metallic (Hermann, Hoffmann & Ashcroft, PRL 2013). This has never been observed, because At has not been made in bulk.

### Inferences
- **Group 3 is a choice of criterion.** Each layout privileges a different one:
  - the Lu form: the differentiating electron, with block widths and Z-continuity;
  - the La form: textbook and historical usage;
  - the asterisk form: agnosticism.
  Once IUPAC called it a convention, no single measurement can settle it; data such as Lr's ionization energy only inform the criteria.
- **"Lanthanide" is two different sets.** The IUPAC series has 15 members; the f-block row has 14. A classification treating it as one set must name which criterion it uses.

### Gaps
- No final post-2021 IUPAC group-3 report or recommendation was found, and iupac.org could not be reached through permitted URLs to check the 2026 table.
- Whether the HgF₄ claim was later reproduced or refuted was not checked.

## 3. Periodic trends and their anomalies

### Takeaway
Radii, ionization energies (IE), electron affinities (EA) and oxidation states are measured, or derived from measurement. The explanations attached to them (shielding, radial nodes, contractions, relativistic effects) are model constructs.

Any "relativistic share" of a property is a computed counterfactual: a relativistic calculation minus a non-relativistic one. It is never observed directly.

Most specific numbers in this section come from background knowledge [unverified].

### Cited Findings

*Radii and contractions*
- **[OBS] Lanthanide contraction.**
  - Observed: Lu (Z 71) has an atomic radius of 174 pm against 180 pm for Y (Z 39), and is denser (9.84 vs 4.47 g cm⁻³) — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
  - Field adds: 4f electrons shield poorly (with a relativistic increment), so effective nuclear charge rises across the series. Near-equal Zr/Hf and Nb/Ta radii follow [unverified].
- **[OBS/MOD] d-block ("scandide") contraction [unverified].**
  - Observed: Ga has about the same atomic radius as Al, and a first IE of about 6.0 eV for both.
  - Field adds: attributed to the poorly shielding 3d¹⁰ core.

*Ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity [unverified]*
- **[OBS] First ionization energies.**
  - Observed: from spectral series limits and photoionization. He is highest (24.59 eV); Cs is lowest among stable elements (3.89 eV). There are dips at B (below Be) and O (below N).
  - Field adds: 2p lies above 2s; pairing repulsion in 2p⁴.
- **[OBS] Electron affinities.**
  - Observed: by laser photodetachment. Cl (≈3.61 eV) exceeds F (≈3.40 eV). N, the noble gases and the group-2 elements do not form stable gas-phase anions.
  - Field adds: compactness of 2p (see Kaupp below).
- **[CONV/MOD] Electronegativity** is not observed directly; each scale is a construct:
  - Pauling (1932): from bond energies.
  - Mulliken: mean of IE and EA.
  - Allred–Rochow: electrostatic.
  - Allen (1989): average valence-shell energy from spectroscopy.
  - F is highest on all of them (≈3.98 on Pauling's scale).

*Oxidation states*
- **[OBS + MOD] [unverified].** The highest established formal state is +9:
  - Observed: [IrO₄]⁺, by gas-phase IR photodissociation spectroscopy (Wang et al., Nature 2014).
  - Field adds: the structure is assigned by quantum chemistry.
- [unverified] Group oxidation states are reached across the d-block up to group 8 (OsO₄, RuO₄) and fall away after that.
- **[SIM]** Fl is predicted to disfavour +4, with only FlF₂ and FlO thermodynamically stable — as cited in [Yakushev et al. 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9453156).

*First-row (2p) anomaly — Kaupp [unverified]*
- **[MOD]** Kaupp (J. Comput. Chem. 28:320, 2007):
  - The first orbital of each angular momentum (1s, 2p, 3d, 4f) has no radial node, because there is no inner shell of the same ℓ to be orthogonal to. It is therefore unusually compact.
  - 2s and 2p have similar radial extent, so they hybridize well. This accounts for strong π-bonds, high electronegativity and small size from B to F.
  - Compact 3d accounts for peculiarities of first-row transition metals (weaker covalent bonds, a preference for high spin).
  - Compact 4f accounts for the core-like behaviour of the lanthanides.
- **[OBS]** The observations it explains:
  - N₂ and O₂ are multiply bonded, while P₄ and S₈ are singly bonded;
  - EA(F) < EA(Cl);
  - first-row elements form no hypervalent compounds.

*Relativistic effects*
- **[OBS → interpretation] Superheavy chromatography.**
  - Observed: Nh, Mc and Fl adsorb more weakly than their lighter homologues Tl, Bi and Pb.
  - Field adds: the authors attribute this to relativistic stabilization of the 7s and 7p₁/₂ orbitals and to spin–orbit splitting of 7p into 7p₁/₂ and 7p₃/₂ — [Nh/Mc 2024](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/chemistry/articles/10.3389/fchem.2024.1474820/text); [Fl 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/chemistry/articles/10.3389/fchem.2022.976635/pdf).
- **[OBS + SIM] Gold's colour [unverified].**
  - Observed: the absorption/reflectivity edge sits near 2.4 eV, so blue light is absorbed and gold looks yellow. Silver's edge is near 3.7 eV, in the UV.
  - Simulation: relativistic 6s contraction and 5d expansion narrow the 5d→6s gap; non-relativistic gold comes out silver-like (Pyykkö & Desclaux, Acc. Chem. Res. 1979; Pyykkö, Chem. Rev. 1988 and Annu. Rev. Phys. Chem. 2012).
- **[OBS + SIM] Mercury's liquidity [unverified].**
  - Observed: melting point 234.3 K.
  - Simulation: parallel-tempering Monte Carlo with relativistic versus non-relativistic interaction potentials attributes about 105 K of melting-point lowering to relativity (Calvo, Pahl, Wormit & Schwerdtfeger, Angew. 2013).
- **[OBS + SIM] Lead–acid battery [unverified].**
  - Observed: cell EMF ≈ 2.1 V.
  - Simulation: DFT calculations attribute about 1.7–1.8 V of it to relativistic effects, mainly in PbO₂ (Ahuja, Blomqvist, Larsson, Pyykkö & Zaleski-Ejgierd, PRL 106:018301, 2011). A non-relativistic, "tin-like" cell would not work.
- **[SIM] Reviews.** "Pushing the limits of the periodic table: a review on atomic relativistic electronic structure theory and calculations for the superheavy elements" (Physics Reports, 2023, doi:10.1016/j.physrep.2023.09.004) — [Nature Index topic page](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/topics/l4/relativistic-effects-in-superheavy-element-chemistry).

*Inert-pair effect [unverified]*
- **[OBS/MOD]**
  - Observed: Tl(I), Pb(II) and Bi(III) are stable relative to the group oxidation states.
  - Field adds: Sidgwick's label "inert pair" (1927). Explanations combine relativistic 6s contraction, weaker bonds for heavy atoms (the promotion energy is not repaid by two extra bonds), and d/f contractions.
- Fl's predicted dislike of +4 (above) extends the pattern.

*Diagonal relationships, knight's move, secondary periodicity, even–odd alternation [unverified]*
- **[REC] Diagonal relationships.**
  - Li–Mg: both form nitrides; similar carbonate decomposition.
  - Be–Al: amphoteric oxides; covalent halides.
  - B–Si: acidic oxides; volatile hydrides.
  - Usually explained by similar charge/radius ratio or electronegativity.
- **[REC] Knight's move** (Laing, 1989; extended by Rayner-Canham, J. Chem. Educ. 2000). In the lower right of the table, an element resembles the element one period down and two groups to the right, in a lower oxidation state. Examples: Zn²⁺/Sn²⁺, Cd²⁺/Pb²⁺, Ag⁺/Tl⁺ (similar halide solubilities and melting points).
- **[REC] Secondary periodicity** (Biron, 1915). Down the p-block groups, the period-4 members As, Se and Br resist their group oxidation state more than the elements above and below them:
  - AsCl₅ is unstable at ordinary temperatures;
  - perbromate was made only in 1968 (Appelman);
  - SeO₃ is less stable than SO₃ and TeO₃.
  - **[MOD]** Attributed to the 3d¹⁰ contraction stabilising 4s; in period 6, to 4f and relativistic effects.
- **[REC] Even–odd alternation in chemistry.** Elements of odd-numbered (old) groups favour odd oxidation states and even groups even ones, with steps of two linked to inert pairs. Nuclear even–odd effects are covered in section 5.

### Inferences
- Every "anomaly" has two layers:
  - an observed irregular step in a measured series (radius, IE, EA, oxide stability);
  - a model that turns the step into an orbital story.
  Relativistic contributions are simulation by construction, because the non-relativistic comparison atom does not exist.
- The anomalies nest into a sequence keyed to the first appearance of each orbital type, which recurs every time a new ℓ opens:
  1. nodeless "first-of-kind" orbitals (1s, 2p, 3d, 4f);
  2. the 2p first-row anomaly;
  3. the 3d (scandide) contraction and secondary periodicity;
  4. the 4f (lanthanide) contraction;
  5. relativistic sixth-row effects;
  6. the superheavy reversals (Fl, Nh, Mc).

### Gaps
- None of the Kaupp, Pyykkö, Calvo, Ahuja, Laing, Rayner-Canham, Biron or Vernon sources could be opened in this session. The numbers given are from background knowledge.

## 4. The Madelung (n+ℓ) filling rule: derived or empirical; which elements break it; filling order versus ionization order

### Takeaway
The rule is an empirical summary of observed ground-state configurations.

Derivation attempts reproduce its main shape (the period lengths). They do so by choosing an effective potential, using semiclassical approximation, or invoking symmetry. None derives it from the full many-electron (relativistic) equation, and none yields the roughly 20 exceptions.

Scerri distinguishes the order in which orbitals fill as Z grows from the order in which one atom loses electrons.

### Cited Findings
- **[REC] The rule and its history.**
  - Statement: orbitals fill in order of increasing n+ℓ; for equal n+ℓ, in order of increasing n — [Kholodenko & Kauffman](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1906.05278).
  - History: Madelung (1936, in a mathematical handbook for physicists); Klechkovskii's books (up to 1968); Hakala's 1952 formulas for where periods start and end — [Belokolos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02535).
  - Janet's constant-(n+ℓ) table (dated 1930 by these authors) predates Madelung — [Kholodenko & Kauffman](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.05278v2).
  - The same authors describe all the filling rules (Fock's n-rule, the hydrogenic (n, ℓ) rule, Madelung's) as empirical, needing explanation.
- **[OBS→MOD] What a "ground-state configuration" is [unverified].**
  - Observed: spectral lines → energy levels with known J and parity (compiled in the NIST Atomic Spectra Database, which was not accessed here).
  - Field adds: a leading-configuration label assigned by term analysis and Hartree–Fock or configuration-interaction models. Real states are mixtures of configurations.
  - For Z ≳ 100 the labels rest mainly on calculation, anchored by a few measurements (No laser spectroscopy; the Lr ionization energy).
- **[REC] Exceptions: count and identity.**
  - One source lists 19, citing Meek & Allen: Cr, Cu, Nb, Mo, Ru, Rh, Pd, Ag, La, Ce, Gd, Pt, Au, Ac, Th, Pa, U, Np, Cm. The rule puts the onset of 4f at La, but observed configurations put it at Ce — [Belokolos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02535).
  - Another gives 20 of 118 (citing Johnson 1984), adding Lr's 7p¹. Pd is 4d¹⁰5s⁰. Cu (3d¹⁰4s¹) and Au (5d¹⁰6s¹) complete the d shell early, whereas Zn, Cd and Hg are regular — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
  - Kholodenko treats Cu, Ag and Au as the "true exceptions" and Mo as anomalous — [Kholodenko 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.02137v1).
  - The count therefore varies with the data source, and with whether predicted (unmeasured) superheavy configurations are included.
- **[MOD] Löwdin's challenge.**
  - Löwdin (Int. J. Quantum Chem., 1969) asked for an ab initio derivation of the rule — [Kholodenko 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.02137v1).
  - Kibler cites Löwdin (1969) and Scerri (2006) for the view that the rule has not been deduced from first principles — [Kibler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0611287).
- **[MOD] Derivation attempts, in date order, with status.**
  - **Demkov & Ostrovsky (1971).** They reproduced the rule by quantizing coupling constants at zero energy in a "focusing" effective potential related to the fish-eye problem. Kitagawara & Barut (1983–84) and Thyssen & Ceulemans criticized the reasoning, and Ostrovsky (1981) conceded the full problem was still out of reach. Status: reproduces, does not derive — [Kholodenko](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2002.12128).
  - **Allen & Knight** (Int. J. Quantum Chem. 90). Year given as 2002 in [Belokolos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02535) and [Kholodenko 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.02137v1), but as 2000 in [Scerri's 2017 letter](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/24624/1/138.%20Responce%20to%20Marc%20Henry%20on%20Madelung%20Rule.pdf). They claimed the long-sought explanation. A 2006 commentary (Found. Chem. 8:285–292) judged it inadequate — [PhilArchive](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/SCECOA). The page shows no author; I believe it is Scerri [unverified].
  - **Ostrovsky** (Found. Chem. 3, 2001) and **Bent & Weinhold** (J. Chem. Educ., 2007) are further treatments listed in [Scerri's letter](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/24624/1/138.%20Responce%20to%20Marc%20Henry%20on%20Madelung%20Rule.pdf).
  - **Kibler (c. 2006).** SO(4,2)⊗SU(2) presents the rule, does not derive it. It credits Demkov–Ostrovsky's effective potential with a derivation of a phenomenological kind — [Kibler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/quant-ph/0611287).
  - **Belokolos (2017).** Shows O(4) symmetry for a neutral atom in mean-field approximation with a Tietz potential, V(r) = −Z/[r(1+r/R)²]. The treatment is semiclassical, non-relativistic and limited to ground states. Exceptions are not derived; Thomas–Fermi gives only an approximation; comparison with reference spectra is left for later — [Belokolos](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1706.02535).
  - **Kholodenko & Kauffman (2019) and Kholodenko (2020).** A route through Bertrand spacetimes and conformal geometry. They claim an analytic answer to Löwdin's challenge but call it non-universal. They attribute the anomalies, speculatively, to a topological difference between spin and spin^c structures, and the exceptions are not worked out. Status: outside the mainstream; no uptake was found — [arXiv:2002.12128](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2002.12128); [arXiv:2006.02137](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.02137v1).
- **[MOD] Scerri's 2017 position** — [Scerri letter, Inference 3(1), Apr 2017, doi:10.37282/991819.17.15](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/24624/1/138.%20Responce%20to%20Marc%20Henry%20on%20Madelung%20Rule.pdf).
  - He reports moving away from his earlier endorsement of Löwdin's "cannot be derived" view.
  - He argues derivability matters less for whether chemistry reduces to physics than is usually assumed.
  - Henry, replying, holds the rule to be empirical.
- **[OBS→MOD] Filling order versus ionization order.**
  - Sc is properly written [Ar]3d¹4s².
  - In K and Ca, 4s lies below 3d; from Sc onward, 3d lies below 4s.
  - Yet ionizing Sc removes a 4s electron first.
  - So the build-up sequence across elements is not the energy order inside a single atom — [Scerri letter](https://philsci-archive.pitt.edu/24624/1/138.%20Responce%20to%20Marc%20Henry%20on%20Madelung%20Rule.pdf).
  - [unverified] Schwarz (J. Chem. Educ. 2010) gives the fuller account: in transition-metal atoms the 3d orbital energy lies below 4s, but 4s stays occupied because of 3d–3d repulsion. Configuration energies and orbital energies are not the same thing.

### Inferences
- These sources support a consistent picture:
  - the n+ℓ rule is a reconstruction from observed configurations;
  - models reproduce it under chosen potentials;
  - actual configurations are computed element by element (HF/DFT/CI), not deduced from the rule.
- The Sc case shows a sequence that is not reversed symmetrically. Building up adds 4s before 3d as Z grows; stripping the same atom removes 4s first.

### Gaps
- The exception list was not cross-checked against NIST ASD, which was not reachable.
- Derivation attempts from 2021–2026 were not searched.

## 5. The nuclear frontier that organizes the elements

### Takeaway
Nuclear stability decides which Z values exist long enough to have chemistry.

The magic numbers come from observation. The superheavy closures (Z = 114/120/126, N = 184) and the "island of stability" are model predictions, and known nuclides still fall several neutrons short of N = 184.

No element has been added since 2010. As of 23 Oct 2026, the sources found report no candidate event for element 119 or 120 from RIKEN, LBNL or JINR.

The "end of the table" depends on the model used.

### Cited Findings

*Magic numbers and the island of stability*
- [unverified] **[OBS→MOD] Magic numbers.**
  - Observed: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126 show up as jumps in separation energies, as abundance peaks, as large numbers of stable isotopes and as high first-excited states.
  - Field adds: the shell model with spin–orbit coupling (Mayer; Haxel, Jensen & Suess, 1949).
- **[MOD] Magic beams.** Doubly magic ⁴⁸Ca favours neutron evaporation over fission in fusion — [INTDS2026 abstract](https://indico2.riken.jp/event/5355/contributions/28965/). ⁵⁰Ti has a magic neutron number — [Oxford Scientist, 17 May 2026](https://oxsci.org/beyond-oganesson-the-race-to-create-element-119/).
- **[MOD] The island.**
  - Predicted near Z = 114 or 120 with N = 184. Predicted α half-lives for element-119 isotopes span 10⁻¹¹ to 10⁹ s — [Oxford Scientist](https://oxsci.org/beyond-oganesson-the-race-to-create-element-119/).
  - N = 184 would stabilise neutron-rich element-120 isotopes — [Isotopes of unbinilium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotopes_of_unbinilium).

Macroscopic–microscopic models favour Z = 114; self-consistent mean-field models favour Z = 120 or 126. *Even–odd and abundance patterns*
- [unverified] **[OBS] Oddo–Harkins rule** (Oddo 1914; Harkins 1917).
  - Solar-system abundances (from CI chondrites and the solar photosphere) show even-Z elements more abundant than their odd-Z neighbours, giving a sawtooth curve.
  - Odd-Z elements have at most two stable isotopes; Sn (Z = 50, magic) has ten.
  - Only four stable odd–odd nuclides exist (²H, ⁶Li, ¹⁰B, ¹⁴N), and Tc and Pm have no stable isotope.
  - α-decay of ²⁰⁹Bi was observed (T½ ≈ 1.9×10¹⁹ yr; de Marcillac et al., Nature 2003), which leaves Pb as the heaviest element with stable isotopes.
- [unverified] **[MOD] Explanations.**
  - Pairing energy.
  - Nucleosynthesis: Big Bang H and He; depletion of Li, Be and B, which are replenished by cosmic-ray spallation; the iron peak at maximum binding energy per nucleon; s- and r-process peaks at N = 50, 82 and 126.
- **[OBS] Crustal abundances.** Y ≈ 30 ppm, Sc 16 ppm, Lu ≈ 0.5 ppm — [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element). Lu is more abundant in the crust than Ag — [Öhrström 2018](https://web-test.research.chalmers.se/publication/500981/file/500981_Fulltext.pdf). [unverified] By mass the crust is dominated by O, then Si, Al and Fe.

*Mirror nuclei and isospin [unverified]*
- **[OBS]** Mirror pairs (Z and N swapped: ³H/³He, ¹³C/¹³N, ¹⁵N/¹⁵O) have near-identical level schemes; their mass differences are mostly Coulomb energy.
- **[MOD]** Isospin (Heisenberg 1932; named "isotopic spin" by Wigner 1937) treats proton and neutron as two states of one nucleon. The symmetry is approximate, and isobaric multiplets follow a quadratic mass equation.

*Discovery criteria, and the record*
- [unverified] **[CONV] Discovery criteria.**

  - Neither limit has been observed; searches for spontaneous positron emission in heavy-ion collisions were inconclusive.
  - Nuclear fission instability may end the table before either electronic limit.

## 6. Superheavy-element chemistry: predicted versus measured

### Takeaway
Measured chemistry currently ends at moscovium (Z = 115, published Sept 2024). All such experiments are atom-at-a-time gas–solid thermochromatography:
- what is counted is decay events at detector positions with a known surface and temperature;
- adsorption enthalpies are inferred by Monte Carlo modelling of transport;
- "metallic", "noble" and "more or less reactive" are interpretations relative to lighter homologues.

Bulk properties of Cn, Fl and Og (liquid ranges, band gaps) are simulations only.

### Cited Findings
- **Background [unverified].**

  - GSI's list of results includes nobelium laser spectroscopy (2016) and fermium (2024) — [Mainz/GSI release](https://press.uni-mainz.de/pushing-the-boundaries-of-chemistry-properties-of-heaviest-element-studied-so-far-measured-at-gsi-fair/). [unverified] These are Laatiaoui et al., Nature 2016, and charge radii of fermium isotopes in Nature 2024.
  - [unverified] LBNL reported in 2025 the direct mass identification of nobelium-containing molecules using FIONA.
- **Cn (112).**
  - [unverified] **Measured.** Eichler et al. (Nature 2007): two ²⁸³Cn atoms adsorbed on gold-covered detectors at low temperature, giving −ΔH_ads(Au) ≈ 52 kJ/mol. Interpreted as volatile but metal-like, binding to gold more weakly than Hg.

  - **[SIM]** "Copernicium: A Relativistic Noble Liquid" (Mewes et al., Angew. 2019, doi:10.1002/anie.201906966) — [Nature Index](https://www.nature.com/nature-index/topics/l4/relativistic-effects-in-superheavy-element-chemistry). Pitzer had predicted noble-gas-like Cn and Fl in 1975.

- **Nh (113) — measured 2024.**

  - **Derived.** −ΔH_ads(SiO₂) ≈ 58 kJ/mol (68% c.i.).
  - **Interpretation.** Nh binds more weakly than Tl but is more reactive than closed-shell Cn, in agreement with relativistic calculations. Before this work there were only first hints of Nh chemistry.
- **Fl (114) — three experimental states.**
  - **Prior state 1: Eichler et al. 2010.** Three atoms, read as noble-gas-like; −ΔH_ads(Au) = 34 (+54/−11) kJ/mol (95% c.i.).
  - **Prior state 2: 2009 GSI run, published by Yakushev et al. 2014.** Two atoms on room-temperature gold, read as metallic; −ΔH_ads > 48 kJ/mol.
  - **Current state: Yakushev et al., Front.     - Conclusions: Fl is highly volatile, the least reactive member of group 14, less reactive than Hg and more than Rn. The press release calls it the most volatile metal and says the results reconcile the two earlier readings.

### Inferences
- **Alternation with subshell closure.** The measured order of reactivity, Cn < Nh > Fl < Mc, follows closed-shell (Cn, 7s²6d¹⁰), single p electron (Nh), quasi-closed 7p₁/₂² (Fl), then 7p₃/₂¹ (Mc). This is a reconstruction from observing (relative adsorption) joined to a model (spin–orbit subshells).
- **"Metal" means different things.** In the GSI experiments it means chemisorption-type bonding to gold, distinct from physisorption. In the Florez/Schwerdtfeger simulations it is a band-structure property, and there Fl comes out semiconductor-like. The labels belong to different predicates, so they are not necessarily in conflict.
- **The Fl record shows a claim settled by reconciliation, not refutation.** Two single-run readings (noble-like in 2010, metallic in 2014) were both re-observed as two deposition zones in 2022, leaving the mechanism open.

- The primary source for Cn (Nature 2007) was not opened.
- No 2025–2026 experimental results for Lv or heavier elements, or for Cn/Fl re-measurements, were found.

## 7. Element families and classification schemes (with membership criteria)

### Takeaway
Classification schemes differ in where their membership criterion comes from:
- table position or configuration (groups, blocks);
- an IUPAC naming convention (lanthanoids, rare-earth metals);
- bulk physical behaviour (metal, non-metal, metalloid);
- observed partitioning between phases (Goldschmidt);
- physiological essentiality (biological classes);
- measured complex stabilities (Ahrland–Chatt–Davies class a/b, later HSAB).

Only the first two are formally fixed by IUPAC. Most of the rest are conventions with fuzzy borders that depend on conditions.

### Cited Findings
- **[CONV] Group names** — [template](https://www.vigyanwiki.in/wiki/Template:Periodic_table_%28group_names%29).
  - Membership is by column in the IUPAC 1–18 table.
  - Element-based IUPAC names: lithium group, beryllium group, … helium or neon group (group 18).
  - Trivial names: alkali metals (group 1 without H), alkaline-earth metals (2), triels (13), tetrels (14), pnictogens (15), chalcogens (16), halogens (17), noble gases (18).
  - The template marks only the element-based names as IUPAC-recommended. [unverified] The 2005 Red Book also accepts several collective names (alkali metals, alkaline-earth metals, pnictogens, chalcogens, halogens, noble gases).
  - The template's group-3 entry follows the 1988 and 2021 reports (Sc, Y, Lu, Lr).
- **[CONV] Series names.**
  - Lanthanoids: 15 elements (La–Lu).
  - Rare-earth metals: 17 elements (Sc, Y and the lanthanoids). Criterion: chemical closeness and co-occurrence in minerals. Lu, for instance, occurs at about 0.003% in monazite.
  - Sources: [Öhrström 2018](https://web-test.research.chalmers.se/publication/500981/file/500981_Fulltext.pdf); [Group 3 element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_3_element).
  - [unverified] Further names and their criteria:
    - actinoids (Ac–Lr);
    - transition elements: an incomplete d-subshell, which excludes group 12;
    - platinum-group metals (Ru, Rh, Pd, Os, Ir, Pt);
    - "coinage metals" (Cu, Ag, Au), not an IUPAC term.
- **[OBS→CONV] Metal / non-metal / metalloid [unverified].**
  - Observed: electrical conductivity and its temperature coefficient (it falls on heating in metals and rises in semiconductors); band gaps; lustre; malleability.
  - Field adds criteria such as the Herzfeld and Mott criteria (Edwards & Sienko).
  - "Metalloid" has no IUPAC definition; lists vary (see section 2).
  - Status is phase- and pressure-dependent: I and Xe become metallic under high pressure.
- **[REC] Goldschmidt geochemical classes [unverified].**
  - Observed: how elements partition between metal, sulfide and silicate phases in meteorites and in smelting (slag, matte, metal).
  - Classes (Goldschmidt, 1920s–1930s): lithophile (silicate/oxide), siderophile (iron metal), chalcophile (sulfide), atmophile (gas).
  - Membership changes with oxygen fugacity, temperature and pressure, and some elements fall into several classes (Fe, for example).
- **[REC/CONV] Biological element classes [unverified].**
  - Bulk elements: H, C, N, O, P, S.
  - Macrominerals: Na, K, Mg, Ca, Cl.
  - Essential trace elements: Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Zn, Mo, Se, I, with disputed members (Cr, V, Ni, B, Si).
  - Criterion: a deficiency that causes impairment reversible by supplementation (Fraústo da Silva & Williams 1991).
  - Reopened memberships: chromium essentiality questioned by EFSA (2014); lanthanides essential for some methylotrophic bacteria (XoxF methanol dehydrogenase; Pol et al. 2014); W enzymes in some prokaryotes; a Cd carbonic anhydrase in a marine diatom.
- **[OBS→MOD] Ahrland–Chatt–Davies classes and HSAB [unverified].**
  - Observed: stability constants of metal complexes in water across the halide and donor-atom series (Ahrland, Chatt & Davies, Q. Rev. Chem. Soc. 12:265, 1958).
  - Class (a) acceptors bind first-row donors most strongly: N ≫ P, O ≫ S, F⁻ > Cl⁻ > Br⁻ > I⁻.
  - Class (b) acceptors reverse these orders. They cluster around Cu(I), Ag, Au, Hg, Pd and Pt near the end of the d-block, with a borderline zone between the classes.
  - Field adds: Pearson's hard/soft generalization (JACS 85:3533, 1963) and the Parr–Pearson absolute hardness η = (I − A)/2 (1983), which is a model construct.

### Inferences
- Each scheme states its membership criterion differently, ordered roughly by how firmly fixed it is:
  1. definitional, from a count or a configuration (groups, blocks, IUPAC series);
  2. from measured behaviour under stated conditions (metal/non-metal, Goldschmidt, class a/b);
  3. from physiological judgement, which gets reopened (biological classes).
- Fuzzy or condition-dependent membership (metalloids, borderline acids, chalcophile Fe) is the rule rather than the exception outside the IUPAC-named sets.
