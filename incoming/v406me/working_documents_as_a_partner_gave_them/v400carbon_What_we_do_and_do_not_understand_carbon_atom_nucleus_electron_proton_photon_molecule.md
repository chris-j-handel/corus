## Atom

### A · Science entry

**The observability of atomic orbitals and the structure of the atom · number at receiving**

**Conditions prior.** The statement holds across readings. The terms have determinate reference. The accounts hold their truth value while tested. A procedure repeated returns the same outcome independently of what precedes it. Nothing prior enters.

**Statement.** Do atomic orbitals, the one-electron functions in which chemistry and spectroscopy place an atom's electrons, refer to anything observable in a many-electron atom, and what does an instrument record when it images an atom? In the field's own posing, a 1999 report in Nature claimed the first direct imaging of d-orbitals in cuprite, its editorial saying "the classic textbook shape of electron orbitals has now been directly observed"; a reply concluded that "atomic orbitals have not in fact been observed", and the dispute has continued in the philosophy of chemistry since.

**The field's account of its hardness.** That orbitals are genuine wavefunctions only in one-electron systems and serve in many-electron atoms as basis sets, coordinate systems for expanding a wavefunction; that textbook statements placing particular electrons in particular orbitals violate the indistinguishability of electrons; that what X-ray scattering and scanning probes measure is a total charge density, which is an observable, while an orbital is a state and not an observable; and that orbitals nonetheless classify spectra, configurations and the periodic table with great success, so that the case divorces a theory's success from whether its central terms refer.

**Accounts.** Orbitals are unobservable in principle: mathematical constructs, not referring, their pictures the squared basis functions of an expansion.

Orbitals are observable in a second, chemists' sense: the charge distribution of a particular electronic state, reconstructible from measured data.

The dispute is verbal: orbitals are states, trivially not observables, and the images are of densities the states predict.

**Specifications.** Single atoms imaged and moved one at a time with scanning probes since the 1980s — the field's record. What a scanning tunnelling or atomic-force microscope records is the response of a three-dimensional charge density to the probe, at a resolution of about 0.6 Å — the field's record. The hydrogen 2S–6P transition frequency is 730,690,248,610.79(48) kHz, a test of the Standard Model to 0.7 parts per trillion — measured. Covalent, van der Waals and ionic radii of one element differ by tens of percent — the field's record. Whole atoms, and molecules of up to 2,000 atoms, interfere with themselves through two paths — measured.

**Specifications withdrawn.**

**Common requirement.**

**Conserving relation.** One account returns every measured density, spectrum and transition strength of a many-electron atom from one wavefunction, with no electron assigned to an orbital.

**Conserving reach.** The atom as spectroscopy, scattering and scanning probes observe it.

**Reach.** The statement quantifies over one-electron functions in a many-electron atom. A total density, a spectrum, a current or a force is what is measured. The orbitals are not among the measured quantities; they are the coordinates in which the measured quantities are expanded, and an atom's radius is a convention fitted to the coupling asked of it.

**Unreachability.** An observable has an operator and an orbital does not; in a many-electron atom an orbital is not even a state but a basis function; the measured density is one function of all the electrons together, and nothing in it is any electron's.

**Persistence.** A measurement whose outcome depends on orbitals and not on the density alone, which no account proposes; or agreement on a meaning of "orbital" under which the dispute dissolves.
