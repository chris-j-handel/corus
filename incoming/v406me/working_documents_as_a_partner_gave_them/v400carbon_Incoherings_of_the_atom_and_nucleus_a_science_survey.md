## Purpose and how to read

Science's picture of the atom and nucleus works extremely well, yet it still holds dozens of places where measurements, theories or models do not fit together. This survey collects those places from current research (as of November 2026), as a science-first base for the later work of natural naming and explaining at corus.me.

An **incohering** here is any place where our understanding does not hold together:

- **Tension**: two careful measurements of the same quantity disagree by more than their stated errors (given in σ, standard deviations; 3σ is a hint, 5σ is the usual discovery bar).
- **Theory gap**: a measurement and the best calculation disagree, or no calculation from first principles exists.
- **Model conflict**: several models each work in their own domain but contradict each other underneath.
- **Foundational**: a question the current framework cannot pose or answer inside itself.

Each entry carries a status: **Open** (no accepted explanation), **Tension** (live disagreement), **Fading** (recent work narrowing it), or **Resolved** (kept because how it dissolved is itself a lesson). Observations and numbers are reported as the field reports them; no reframing is attempted yet.

## 1. Size and shape: how big is a nucleus?

The size of the smallest nuclei was the field's most famous incohering for 15 years, and it is now mostly closed: the proton is small (about 0.841 fm). What remains open is why different probes once disagreed, and how neutrons spread out in heavy nuclei.

**1.1 Proton radius puzzle — Fading to Resolved.** Muonic hydrogen (a muon in place of the electron) gave 0.8418 ± 0.0007 fm, against a long-accepted 0.876–0.88 fm from electron scattering and ordinary hydrogen, a gap of more than 5σ ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/a/physicists-confirm-proton-radius-is-smaller-than-we-thought/)). In 2026 a Garching measurement of the hydrogen 2S–6P line gave 0.8406 ± 0.0015 fm, matching the muonic value, and its authors called it the final nail in the puzzle ([Maisenbacher et al., Nature 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14980)).

- Still loose: CODATA 2022 found the electronic-only value 2.8σ above the full value and said the puzzle was not yet solved ([review, arXiv 2604.15546](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.15546)).
- Still loose: the PRad and Mainz A1 scattering experiments disagree on the proton's form factor shape, which PRad-II aims to settle ([PRad-II, CIPANP 2025](https://agenda.hep.wisc.edu/event/2257/contributions/34193/attachments/10480/13488/Hague-PRad-II-CIPANP2025_final.pdf)).
- Lesson: the old large value came from many independent groups that all agreed with each other, and were all off.

**1.2 Deuteron radius puzzle — Tension.** Muonic deuterium gave a deuteron radius 7.5σ smaller than CODATA-2010 and 3.5σ smaller than ordinary-deuterium spectroscopy ([Pohl et al., Science 2016](https://isidore.co/misc/Physics%20papers%20and%20books/Zotero/storage/ZEGX7NCK/669.full.html)). A separate gap of about 2σ remains between the calculated nuclear-structure correction and the value the experiment implies ([Hernandez et al., PLB 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.01199)). If the proton puzzle is resolved, this one should follow, but that has not been shown cleanly.

**1.3 Helium-3 vs helium-4 size difference — Fading.** Muonic helium and ordinary helium gave squared-radius differences 3.6σ apart. Adding a missed hyperfine-mixing term in the ordinary-atom theory cut the gap to about 1.7σ ([Qi et al., PRR 2025](https://doaj.org/article/b67e1e90200747d0867dc14c790f64dc)), or 1.2σ by the Amsterdam team's count ([Physics World 2025](https://physicsworld.com/a/conflicting-measurements-of-heliums-charge-radius-may-be-reconciled-by-new-calculations/)). The helion radius is now 1.97007 ± 0.00097 fm ([Mainz press release](https://www.alphagalileo.org/en-gb/Item-Display/ItemId/258874)).

**1.4 Proton mass radius smaller than its charge radius — Open.** Jefferson Lab used J/ψ photoproduction to probe how the proton's gluons carry its mass. Every model they tried gave a mass radius well below the charge radius ([Duran et al., Nature 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.05212v4)). So a proton has no single size: its charge, its mass and its gluon "scalar" cloud each spread differently, and the extraction is model-dependent.

**1.5 Neutron skin: PREX-II vs CREX — Tension.** Parity-violating electron scattering measured how far neutrons extend beyond protons. Lead-208 gave a thick skin of about 0.28 fm, pointing to a stiff symmetry energy; calcium-48 gave a thin 0.12 fm, pointing to a soft one ([review, arXiv 2609.15856](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.15856)). No standard model reproduced both within 1σ ([arXiv 2511.15385](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15385)). 2025–2026 papers reconcile them by strengthening the isovector spin-orbit force or loosening the usual skin–symmetry-energy link ([Papakonstantinou 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22722)). The planned MREX experiment at Mainz should settle the lead value.

## 2. Inside the nucleon: what a proton is made of

The textbook proton is "two up quarks and one down quark." Measurements show that picture fails on mass, spin and contents: the three quarks supply about 1% of the mass and roughly a quarter to a third of the spin.

**2.1 Where the proton's mass comes from — Open (no single answer).** The up and down quark masses are a few MeV each, about 1% of the proton's 938 MeV ([Ji & Liu, arXiv 2302.11600](https://arxiv.org/html/2302.11600v3)). The rest is energy of the strong interaction itself, but how to split it depends on the chosen bookkeeping. One lattice decomposition gives quark masses 9%, quark energy 31%, gluon field energy 37% and the trace anomaly 23% ([Yang et al., PRL 2018](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/1808.08677)); another way of counting attributes about 91% to the trace anomaly ([arXiv 2302.11600](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2302.11600)). The incohering: "how much of the proton is gluon" has no frame-free answer.

**2.2 Proton spin puzzle — Open.** Experiments in the 1980s found quark spins carry only about a quarter of the proton's spin ([APS Physics](https://physics.aps.org/articles/v7/s78)). Gluon spins are now known to point the same way as the proton's ([DOE/PHENIX 2024](https://www.energy.gov/node/4837803)). A 2025 lattice calculation puts the total gluon helicity at 46 ± 9% of the spin ([Zhao et al., arXiv 2512.24315](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.24315v1)). The remainder must come from orbital motion of quarks and gluons, which is barely measured. The Electron-Ion Collider at Brookhaven is built largely to close this ledger.

**2.3 More anti-down than anti-up quarks — Open.** Naively, gluons should split equally into up and down quark–antiquark pairs. Fermilab's SeaQuest found about 1.4 down antiquarks for every up antiquark ([SeaQuest, Nature 2021](https://quantamagazine.org/protons-antimatter-revealed-by-decades-old-experiment-20210224/)), a result with no agreed explanation ([UConn summary](https://puckett.physics.uconn.edu/?p=506)). Pion-cloud models fit it: the proton spends part of its time as a neutron plus a pion.

**2.4 Intrinsic charm — Tension (disputed).** NNPDF reported charm quarks as a built-in part of the proton, under 1% of its momentum, at about 3σ ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/?p=102592)). Other groups argue the evidence depends on how "intrinsic" is defined and on scheme choices ([Guzzi et al., PLB 2023](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2211.01387v2)). A quark heavier than the proton sitting inside the proton is itself a sign that "made of" is the wrong verb.

**2.5 Confinement and the mass gap — Foundational.** No free quark or gluon has ever been seen, and QCD has no proof of why. Showing that the underlying Yang–Mills theory exists mathematically and has a mass gap is one of the Clay Institute's seven Millennium Prize problems, still unsolved. Lattice QCD computes hadron masses well, but it computes; it does not explain.

## 3. The neutron: how long it lives, and why it is so round

The free neutron sits at the center of two of the sharpest incoherings: its lifetime depends on how you measure it, and its charge is far more perfectly balanced than theory requires.

**3.1 Neutron lifetime: beam vs bottle — Tension (about 5σ).** Neutrons held in a bottle and counted as they disappear live 877.8 ± 0.3 s on average. Neutrons in a beam, counted by the protons they produce, live 888.1 ± 2.0 s ([CERN Courier, Jan 2026](https://cerncourier.com/a/the-beam-bottle-debate-at-psi/)). The bottle counts survivors; the beam counts decay products, so a missing decay channel would show up exactly this way.

- Proposed explanations: an unknown systematic in one method; a long-lived excited neutron state present only in fresh beams ([Physics World on Koch & Hummel](https://physicsworld.com/?p=117907)); protons that capture an electron and escape detection as hydrogen. Exotic decay channels are now largely excluded ([CERN Courier](https://cerncourier.com/a/the-beam-bottle-debate-at-psi/)).
- Next decisive tests: J-PARC's method detects the decay electrons in a time-projection chamber instead of protons; new results are due from LANL, NIST, J-PARC and PSI over the next few years.

**3.2 Neutron electric dipole moment and the strong CP problem — Foundational.** QCD allows a term (θ) that would give the neutron a measurable electric dipole moment. None is seen: the best limit is |dₙ| < 1.8 × 10⁻²⁶ e·cm ([nEDM, PRL 2020](https://psi.ch/en/nedm)), which forces θ below about 10⁻¹⁰ ([n2EDM overview](https://indico.cern.ch/event/1382917/contributions/6028566/attachments/2925594/5135730/2024_09_11_SPS2024_n2EDM_overview_WTC.pdf)). Nothing in the theory explains why a free parameter should be so close to zero. The leading proposal, the axion, has not been observed ([n2EDM design paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.08730)). PSI's n2EDM aims for a tenfold better limit and was preparing to start measuring in late 2025.

## 4. The weak force and neutrinos at the nucleus

Nuclei are the laboratory for the weak force, and three of its numbers refuse to close: quark-mixing probabilities that do not add to one, neutrinos that go missing near gallium, and an unexplained bump in reactor spectra.

**4.1 Cabibbo angle anomaly — Tension (2–3σ).** The first row of the quark-mixing (CKM) matrix must sum to exactly 1. Using V\_ud from superallowed nuclear 0⁺→0⁺ decays and V\_us from kaon decays, the sum falls short by 2–3σ ([Gorchtein et al., PRR 2025](https://journals.aps.org/prresearch/pdf/10.1103/z8g6-9j25)); one fit gives 0.9985 ([arXiv 2201.09949](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.09949)). The different V\_us determinations also disagree with each other at about 3σ ([Crivellin et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.06862v2)).

- The nuclear link: a 2025 re-treatment of finite nuclear size in aluminium-26m decay alone reduced the deficit by 1σ. The anomaly partly lives in how well we model the nucleus, not only in the weak force.

**4.2 Gallium anomaly — Open (over 4σ).** Intense radioactive neutrino sources placed in gallium produce about 20% fewer captures than predicted: observed/predicted = 0.80 ± 0.05 across GALLEX, SAGE and BEST ([Gonzalez-Garcia et al., PLB 2025](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2411.16840)). Cross-section checks do not explain it, and a sterile neutrino explanation is disfavored by solar and KamLAND data. A 2% error in chromium-51 decay branching ratios would be enough ([Brdar et al., JHEP 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2303.05528)). It has only ever been seen in gallium.

**4.3 Reactor 5 MeV bump — Open.** Reactor antineutrino spectra show a roughly 10% local excess near 5 MeV. The older 6% flux deficit now looks like a bias in nuclear data, but STEREO confirms the bump ([El Atmani, STEREO](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.12193v1)). A 2026 first-principles calculation of first-forbidden beta decays produces an enhancement near 5 MeV that may partly explain it ([Xu et al., arXiv 2603.22699](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22699)).

**4.4 Quenching of g\_A — Fading.** For 50 years nuclear beta decays ran about 25% slower than calculated, as if the weak coupling g\_A shrank inside nuclei. Ab initio work traced most of this to the weak force acting on pairs of nucleons plus nuclear correlations ([Gysbers et al., Nature Physics 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.00047)). Whether the same applies to neutrinoless double beta decay is still unsettled ([review, arXiv 2111.15543](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.15543)).

**4.5 Neutrinoless double beta decay — Open.** If it occurs, the neutrino is its own antiparticle. KamLAND-Zen's full dataset gives a half-life above 3.8 × 10²⁶ years. The matching neutrino-mass limit spans 28–122 meV, a fourfold spread set mainly by disagreeing nuclear matrix element calculations ([KamLAND-Zen, PRL 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11438v2)). Nuclear theory, not the detector, is now the bottleneck for reading this result.

## 7. The nuclear force and how nuclei are built

There is still no derivation of the force that holds a nucleus together from the theory of quarks and gluons, and the effective forces we use break down at the edges: very neutron-rich nuclei, four free neutrons, quarks inside bound nucleons.

**7.1 The nuclear force has no first-principles form — Open.** Today's best approach, chiral effective field theory, builds two-, three- and sometimes four-nucleon forces from symmetry and fits their constants to data ([Machleidt, arXiv 1901.01473](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1901.01473)). Three-nucleon forces are unavoidable, which already says a nucleus is not a sum of pairs. Calculations fixed to light nuclei tend to give medium-mass nuclei radii that are too small and binding that is too strong. Getting nuclear matter and medium-mass nuclei right at the same time remains an outstanding problem ([Machleidt, arXiv 2307.06416](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.06416)).

- Example: earlier ab initio work put the calcium neutron drip line near calcium-60, while a 2025 interaction puts it at calcium-71. The authors conclude current interactions lack precision at best and may hold a significant flaw at worst ([arXiv 2512.11723](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.11723v1)).

**7.2 Magic numbers that stop being magic — Open.** Shell-model "magic" counts (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) explain why some nuclei are extra stable. Oxygen-28, with 8 protons and 20 neutrons, should be doubly magic. In 2023 it was seen for the first time and found unbound, falling apart into oxygen-24 and four neutrons ([Kondo et al., Nature 2023, via DOE](https://www.energy.gov/node/4839327)). Far from stability the shell structure itself shifts, so the magic numbers are local, not universal.

**7.3 The tetraneutron — Tension (experiment vs theory).** In 2022 a RIKEN experiment saw four neutrons briefly held together as a resonance, at 5σ ([Iowa State](https://research.iastate.edu/?p=9420)), with energy about 2.4 MeV and width 1.8 MeV ([Iowa State](https://research.iastate.edu/2022/06/24/theoretical-calculations-predicted-by-iowa-state-researcher-now-confirmed-tetraneutron-an-exotic-state-of-matter/)). The data do not fit modern nuclear-force theories ([Advanced Science News](https://www.advancedsciencenews.com/tetraneutron-finally-found/)). A 2025 review notes that combining all recent signals implies a bound four-neutron state, in conflict with nearly all published theory ([arXiv 2506.11623](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.11623)).

**7.4 The EMC effect — Open after 40 years.** Quarks inside a nucleon bound in iron are distributed differently from quarks in a free nucleon. Two camps explain it: short-lived high-momentum nucleon pairs (short-range correlations) or a whole-nucleus mean field ([Physics World](https://physicsworld.com/?p=77901)). A 2026 review still finds no consensus ([arXiv 2601.11867](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.11867)). The incohering: a nucleon is not the same object inside a nucleus as outside it.

**7.5 Where fission fragments get their spin — Fading.** A nucleus with zero spin splits into fragments that each spin with 6–7 units of angular momentum, a puzzle for over 40 years. A 2021 experiment found the two fragments' spins uncorrelated, so the spin is generated after the split, likened to an elastic band snapping ([Wilson et al., Nature 2021](https://docta.ucm.es/rest/api/core/item/c3853f94-adf8-451f-bf11-7a2dcefbd317/export/refworks)). This overturned the prior consensus that spin arose before scission.

## 5. Hints of something new inside atoms and nuclei

Two families of signals sit right at the boundary between "new force" and "nucleus we have not modeled well enough": the X17 pairs from excited light nuclei, and curved King plots from isotope shifts.

**5.1 X17 — Tension (experiments disagree).** Since 2015 the ATOMKI group in Hungary has reported excess electron–positron pairs at large angles from excited beryllium-8, then helium-4 and carbon-12, first claimed at 6.8σ and fitting a new boson of about 17 MeV ([CERN Courier, Sept 2025](https://cerncourier.com/a/mixed-signals-from-x17/)). A Vietnamese group reported a replication in 2024.

- MEG II at PSI repeated the beryllium reaction and found no significant signal, but its result is still within 1.5σ of ATOMKI's, so it disfavors without excluding ([arXiv 2504.11439](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11439v2)).
- PADME at Frascati looked for X17 directly in electron–positron collisions and saw a modest excess peaked at 16.90 MeV ([arXiv 2504.11439](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.11439v2)).
- The PADME excess sits in significant tension with electron g-2 limits ([arXiv 2504.14014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.14014)). Neither a nuclear-physics explanation of ATOMKI's angles nor a consistent new-particle model is accepted.

**5.2 Curved King plots — Open.** Plotting the isotope shifts of one atomic transition against another should give a straight line if the nucleus only changes its charge radius and mass. In ytterbium, the curvature is large and mainly traced to the deformed nucleus's higher charge moments ([Door et al., arXiv 2403.07792](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.07792)). Calcium, with a light spherical nucleus, was expected to stay straight; instead it shows a nonlinearity of about 900σ. Nuclear polarization is the only known effect that might be large enough to explain it ([Wilzewski et al., PRL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.10277v1)). Mercury now shows a 4.9σ curve too ([Groh et al. 2025](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2510.18514)). The two-parameter description of how a nucleus shifts an atom's light is failing, and it is not yet clear what replaces it.

## 6. Precision atomic physics: where the numbers stop agreeing

The theory of how electrons bind to a nucleus (bound-state QED) now matches hydrogen to 0.7 parts per trillion, yet the constants it runs on still disagree between laboratories.

**6.1 The fine-structure constant: caesium vs rubidium — Tension (5.4–5.5σ).** α sets the strength of every atomic bond. Berkeley's caesium recoil gave 1/α = 137.035999046(27); Paris's rubidium gave 137.035999206(11), 5.5σ apart ([arXiv 2504.14014](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.14014)). Both experiments use the same physics, which makes the gap stranger ([Scientific American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/physicists-achieve-best-ever-measurement-of-fine-structure-constant/)). Electron g-2 then sits 2.3σ above theory using the rubidium value; with caesium the sign flips ([Jegerlehner, arXiv 2601.08101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.08101)). The most basic number of atomic physics is uncertain at the tenth digit.

**6.2 Positronium fine structure — Fading.** Positronium (an electron bound to a positron) has no nucleus, so it tests binding with no nuclear unknowns. A 2020 microwave measurement of one n = 2 interval disagreed with QED by 4.2σ ([Gurung et al., PRA 2021](https://core.ac.uk/download/417940725.pdf)). Follow-up work showed that interfering line shapes can shift apparent frequencies and explain the discrepancy ([UCL, PRA 2023](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10168858/1/PhysRevA.107.042810.pdf)). The older ground-state hyperfine mismatch of 2.5–3σ is still listed as unresolved in reviews ([Karshenboim](https://arxiv.org/pdf/hep-ph/0310099)).

**6.3 Hydrogen and the Rydberg constant — Resolved for now.** With the 2026 2S–6P result, hydrogen and muonic hydrogen agree and test the Standard Model to 0.7 ppt ([Maisenbacher et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14980)). The puzzle's earlier form, that the Rydberg constant might be the misfit, is now closed by consistency.

**6.4 Muon g-2 — Resolved, but the incohering moved.** Fermilab's final result reached 127 ppb in June 2025 ([Fermilab via BNL](https://www.bnl.gov/newsroom/news.php?a=222457)). The 2025 Theory Initiative adopted lattice QCD for the strong-force part, and that prediction agrees with experiment ([KEK](https://www2.kek.jp/ipns/en/news/7427/)). The disagreement now sits between two ways of computing the same quantity: in a 2026 Nature analysis, lattice differed from the KLOE-based data-driven value by 6.2σ and from BaBar by 3.5σ ([ScienceBlog summary](https://scienceblog.com/?p=594562)). The muon is fine; our measurements of how electrons and positrons make pions are not.

## 8. Nuclei at the extremes: first minutes, dense stars, heaviest elements

Outside the laboratory, nuclear physics must explain the universe's first light elements and the cores of neutron stars, and in both places the standard account does not close.

**8.1 The cosmological lithium problem — Open.** Big Bang nucleosynthesis correctly predicts hydrogen, deuterium and helium, but predicts 3–4 times more lithium-7 than old stars show, a 4–5σ gap ([Fields, arXiv 1203.3551](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1203.3551)). Twenty years of re-measuring the reactions that destroy beryllium-7 have not closed it ([IFIN-HH project, 2025](https://www.nipne.ro/proiecte/pn4/10-projects.html)); a 2026 measurement of ⁷Be(d,p) again found limited impact ([Indico 2026](https://indico.global/event/16335/contributions/155099/)). The options are stellar depletion, a missed nuclear rate, or new physics.

**8.2 The hyperon puzzle — Open.** At two to three times normal nuclear density, hyperons (baryons containing strange quarks) should appear in neutron-star cores. They soften matter so much that standard calculations cannot support stars even near 1.4 solar masses, yet stars of about 2 solar masses are observed ([Blaschke et al., arXiv 1803.01836](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1803.01836)). Three-body repulsion or an early shift to quark matter can rescue the mass ([EPJ A highlight](https://www.epj.org/epja-news/1828-epja-highlight-towards-the-solution-of-the-hyperon-puzzle)), but which one nature uses is unknown. A 2025 ab initio paper still calls it a central open problem ([Tong et al., via Emergent Mind](https://www.emergentmind.com/open-problems/hyperon-puzzle-neutron-stars)).

**8.3 The island of stability — Open (unreached).** Theory predicts a region of superheavy nuclei that live much longer than their neighbors, but it has never been reached. In 2024 Berkeley made two atoms of livermorium (element 116) with a titanium-50 beam, opening a path to element 120, which is expected to be 10–20 times harder still ([DOE, 2025](https://www.energy.gov/node/4850554)). Where the heaviest possible nucleus lies is unknown.

**8.4 The thorium-229 nuclear clock — Frontier.** Thorium-229 has the lowest-energy nuclear excitation known, about 8.4 eV, reachable by an ultraviolet laser ([APS Physics, 2024](https://physics.aps.org/articles/pdf/10.1103/Physics.17.71)), while most nuclear transitions need X-rays far beyond current lasers ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/node/1859356)). Its frequency was laser-measured in 2024, and two groups reported working nuclear clocks in Nature on 7 October 2026 ([The Debrief](https://thedebrief.org/scientists-achieve-major-breakthrough-with-worlds-first-working-nuclear-clocks/)). This is less an incohering than a new instrument: a nuclear transition sensitive enough to test whether constants such as α drift over time.

## 9. Conceptual incoherings: where the words themselves strain

Behind the numerical tensions sit a few places where science's own vocabulary for the atom does not hold together. These matter most for the later naming work, because they are not settled by a better measurement.

**9.1 The measurement problem — Foundational.** Quantum theory evolves states smoothly, yet every measurement gives one definite outcome, and the theory does not say how. In Nature's 2025 survey of 1,101 researchers, 45% said there is a boundary between quantum and classical objects, 45% said there is none, and 10% were unsure ([Scientific American](https://scientificamerican.com/article/physicists-divided-on-what-quantum-mechanics-says-about-reality)). Copenhagen remains the most popular reading, about 15% favor many worlds, and support for information-only (epistemic) views is rising ([The Debrief](https://thedebrief.org/scientists-are-still-perplexed-by-quantum-physics-and-cant-agree-on-its-true-meaning-landmark-survey-finds/)). Every statement about an electron "in" an atom inherits this gap.

**9.2 Two incompatible pictures of one nucleus — Foundational.** The liquid-drop model treats the nucleus as a strongly interacting fluid and explains fission; the shell model treats it as independent nucleons in orbits and explains magic numbers and decays. Taken as descriptions of what the nucleus is, they contradict each other. One philosophical reading resolves this by treating both as consistent descriptions of processes rather than things ([Pen, PhilPapers](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/PENMOT)). After 70 years no single comprehensive theory covers all nuclear phenomena ([arXiv 0811.0435](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/0811.0435)).

**9.3 "Made of" fails inside the nucleon.** Three quarks give about 1% of the proton's mass (2.1). A charm quark heavier than the proton may be part of it (2.4). A nucleon is measurably different inside a nucleus than outside (7.4). The parts-and-whole language of chemistry does not carry down to this scale.

**9.4 "Size" depends on what you ask.** The proton has a charge radius, a smaller mass radius and a larger gluon "scalar" radius (1.4); a lead nucleus has different proton and neutron radii (1.5). Even the long-running radius puzzle came down partly to which probe was used. Size here is a relation between object and probe, not a single property.

**9.5 Orbits that are not orbits.** Popular pictures still show electrons circling the nucleus as in the Bohr model, while quantum mechanics describes waves that only show particle-like features when measured ([Ars Technica via Harvard TagTeam](https://tagteam.harvard.edu/hub_feeds/1804/feed_items/18035982)). The image most people hold of the atom is one physics abandoned a century ago.

**9.6 A "force" that is not fundamental.** The force between nucleons is a residual, short-range effect of quark–gluon interactions, described with effective two- and three-body terms (7.1) rather than derived. What is called "the nuclear force" is a bookkeeping device fitted to data.

## 10. How incoherings dissolve: lessons from the ones that closed

Every atomic or nuclear anomaly that has closed in the past decade closed through a missing piece of known physics or an unrecognized measurement effect; none has yet closed by confirming a new particle or force.

| Incohering | How it dissolved | Kind of resolution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Proton radius (1.1) | Better hydrogen spectroscopy agreed with muonic hydrogen; the many older agreeing values were all biased high | Measurement systematics |
| Muon g-2 (6.4) | Lattice QCD replaced data-driven input; the gap moved into the e⁺e⁻ → pions data | Theory input; tension relocated |
| Helium radius difference (1.3) | A hyperfine-mixing term had been left out of ordinary-helium theory | Missing known physics |
| g\_A quenching (4.4) | Weak force acting on nucleon pairs plus correlations had been ignored | Missing known physics |
| Reactor flux deficit (4.3) | Traced to bias in the nuclear data used for predictions ([STEREO](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.12193v1)) | Input data bias |
| Positronium interval (6.2) | Interfering line shapes shifted the apparent frequency | Measurement systematics |
| Fission spin (7.5) | Data showed spin forms after the split, overturning the prior consensus | Wrong mechanism assumed |

Two cautions for reading live incoherings. First, agreement between many groups is not proof: physical constants have historically drifted by several error bars before settling ([Quanta Magazine](https://www.quantamagazine.org/proton-radius-puzzle-deepens-with-new-measurement-20160811)). Second, a closed incohering often reopens one level down, as muon g-2 shows.
