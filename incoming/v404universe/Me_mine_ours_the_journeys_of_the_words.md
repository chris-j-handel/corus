# Me, mine, ours: the journeys of the words

**No word here has authority. No living file and no gathering file is changed by this page.** A page of the session v404universe. Each row is a field's own word, set beside the living files for a reader to test. The living files were first read for it through summaries, so each section number and wording wants checking against the file.

---

People said I, you, mine and ours long before anyone asked what a self is. "Not mine, not I, not myself" is about 2,500 years old. "The self" as a subject of philosophy in English is about 330.

## Reading used

This page works from natural intelligence as stated in this session. The opening sentence, "The universe is the set of now-existing things", is the one observing. Everything after it is co-sequential naming and explaining, all or none, and "the universe is an existing thing" does not enter.

[Me](https://corus.me/Exhibit_THIRTY-ONE_Me_v402mgr.md) already names me, self and other. It already carries Hegel, James, Mead, Parfit, Metzinger, Zahavi, Abhidharma, Ubuntu and Burnet. It gives no naming for I, myself, mine, yours or ours.

So this page traces those words and their neighbours in the fields' own records: where each first occurs, where it turns, and where it becomes a public debate. Every row is a field's word, offered for the expedition to test. A shared word is a lead and no more.

One flag for the set: the published v402mgr opening sentence still reads "the set of all now-existing things".

## The words

The everyday words carry the oldest record. Most began as a saying, a sameness or a relation, and became things later.

| Word | Earliest sense | The turn |
|---|---|---|
| I | Indo-European \*eǵ-: the speaker, in the act of speaking. | Benveniste, 1958: "I" names whoever is saying "I" now, and swaps with "you" at every turn of talk. |
| me, my, mine | A different root, \*me-. Ego and me, aham and mām, ich and mich are unrelated pairs. | The one acting and the one acted on, known or owned were never one word. "Mine" comes from "me", not from "I". |
| [self](https://en.wiktionary.com/wiki/self) | Old English self, seolf: "same", "the very one". Ic selfa is "I, the same". | An emphasis became a noun. "The self" as a subject of philosophy in English dates to Locke, 1694. |
| [myself](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/myself) | Old English me self: "me, the same". | Reshaped to "my self" about 1500, as if self were a thing owned. |
| own | Old English āgen, from āgan, to have. "Owe" and "ought" are the same verb. | To own and to owe were one word. |
| person | Latin persona: an actor's mask, then a role, then standing in law. | [Boethius](https://logoslibrary.org/boethius/eutyches/3.html), about 512: an individual substance of a rational nature. |
| individual | Latin individuum, "undivided". Boethius gives it as his word for Greek atomon. | The atom's name became the person's. |
| identity | Latin idem, "the same": sameness. | Applied to persons by Locke, 1694. |
| proper, property | Latin proprius, "one's own". | What is proper to me becomes my property. |
| common, immune | Both from Latin [munus](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/528577?ref=rc): a duty, an office, a gift owed. Communis shares the duty; immunis is exempt from it. | Medicine's "immune" began as "owing nothing to the common". |
| ahaṃkāra, mamakāra | Sanskrit: "I-making" and "mine-making". | I and mine are named as doings. |
| wu 吾, wo 我 | Classical Chinese has two words for I. Wo is the I set against others. | [Zhuangzi](https://api.philpapers.org/rec/SHAILM): "wu has lost wo". |
| we | Many languages have two: we-with-you and we-without-you. English has one. | "Ours" hides whether you are in it. |
| my, of two kinds | Many languages mark "my hand, my mother" differently from "my canoe". | What cannot be given away, and what can. |
| I as a kin word | Vietnamese has no fixed "I"; a speaker names themself by relation to the hearer. Japanese watakushi means "private". | The I changes with the other. |

Three of these bear directly on the set. Self first meant same. I and me were never one word. And to own was to owe.

## I, the one saying

The record moves from I as a saying, to I as a thing found inside, and back to I as a saying.

| When | Where | The saying | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. 700 BCE | [Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.1](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/the-brihadaranyaka-upanishad/d/doc117939.html) | The first being looks round, sees only itself and says "I am". From that, the text says, came the name "I". | The oldest account of the word's origin: I as a first saying |
| c. 500 BCE | Delphi; Heraclitus | "Know yourself." "I searched myself." | I becomes something to inquire into |
| c. 400 CE | Augustine, Confessions | He has become a question to himself. | The first long inward book in the first person |
| c. 1020 | Avicenna | The flying man: someone floating with no sensation would still affirm "I am". | I without body or world |
| 1641 | Descartes | "I am, I exist" is certain each time it is said or thought. | I as philosophy's first certainty |
| 1781 to 1787 | Kant | The "I think" goes with every experience and is never itself an object of one. | I as a form, not a thing found |
| c. 1790 | Lichtenberg | One should say "it thinks", as one says "it lightens". | The I as a habit of grammar; Nietzsche repeats it in 1886 |
| 1794 | Fichte | The I posits itself, and against itself a not-I. | I and not-I as a made pair |
| 1958 | Benveniste | I and you exist only in the turn of speaking, and they reverse. | I as coupled with you |
| 1975, 1979 | Anscombe; Perry | "I" cannot be swapped for any name or description. | The I resists becoming a thing among things |

Lichtenberg's "it thinks" and the Sanskrit "I-making" are the nearest priors to the set's -ing naming of a self.

## Me and myself, the same one

The oldest question about me is how I stay the same while changing. The answers run from flow, to memory, to a bundle, to a model the body keeps.

| When | Who | What they held |
|---|---|---|
| c. 500 BCE | Heraclitus | A river stays the same river by flowing. |
| c. 480 BCE | Epicharmus | A debtor argues he is no longer the man who borrowed. |
| 1694 | Locke | A person is the same self as far back as their consciousness reaches. "Person" is a word of law and blame. |
| 1739 | Hume | Looking inward he finds perceptions and never a self. |
| 1890 | James | The I knows and the Me is known. The Me is all a person can call theirs. |
| 1934 | Mead | The me is the attitudes of others, taken in. |
| 1949 | [Burnet and Fenner](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2014/entries/biology-self/) | "Self" enters immunology: the body's own is tolerated, the not-self attacked. |
| 1984 | Parfit | Sameness of person is not what matters. Continuity is. |
| 1998 | Botvinick and Cohen | The rubber hand: the sense that "this hand is mine" shifts within minutes. |

James, Mead, Parfit and Burnet already stand in Me. The rows before 1890 are new to it, and they show the question is as old as the first page's parting.

## Mine and yours

"Mine" has been argued in public for at least 2,400 years, and the argument has barely changed shape. This is the side of me that Me does not yet name.

| When | Who | What was said |
|---|---|---|
| c. 375 BCE | [Plato, Republic 462](https://www.contextus.org/Republic.5.462) | The best-run city is the one where most people say "mine" and "not mine" of the same things. |
| c. 340 BCE | [Aristotle, Politics II.3](https://classicalwisdom.com/greek_books/politics-by-aristotle-book-ii) | "All say mine" is two different sayings, and what is common to the most people gets the least care. |
| c. 300 BCE to 120 CE | [Stoics; Hierocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oikei%C3%B4sis) | Oikeiōsis: a creature first takes itself as its own, then widens that in circles to kin, city and humankind. |
| c. 200 CE | [Pirkei Avot 5:10](https://www.sefaria.org.il/English_Explanation_of_Pirkei_Avot.5.10) | Four characters, sorted by how they say mine and yours. "Mine is mine, yours is yours" is called average by some and Sodom's way by others. |
| 2nd to 6th c. CE | Roman law | Mine and thine; "to each their own"; things of no one; things common to all. |
| c. 530 | Rule of Benedict 33 | No monk is to call anything their own. |
| 1323 | Franciscan poverty dispute | Can one use a thing without owning it? The pope says no. From the quarrel comes "a right" as something a person has. |
| 1646 | [Richard Overton](https://oll.libertyfund.org/page/lt078) | Each one "has a self-propriety, else could he not be himself". |
| 1651 | Hobbes | Before law there is no mine and thine. |
| 1689 | Locke | Each has a property in their own person, and labour makes things mine. |
| 1755 | Rousseau | The first person to fence land and say "this is mine" founded civil society. |
| 1840 to 1844 | Proudhon, Stirner, Marx | Property as theft; the unique one and its own; work made "not mine". |
| 1890 | James | Between "me" and "mine" the line is hard to draw. |
| 1925, 1992 | Mauss; Weiner | A gift keeps something of its giver. Some things are kept by being given. |

Oikeiōsis is the closest ancient prior to a self's own widening. It names "making one's own" as a going-on, starting from the creature and reaching all.

## Ours, and not ours

"Ours" is debated wherever a people decides who is inside it and what no one may fence. The record alternates between holding in common and enclosing.

| When | Who | What was said or done |
|---|---|---|
| 4th c. BCE | [Yang Zhu, Mozi, Mencius](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9780203953624-20/egoism-chinese-ethics) | "For me" against "inclusive care". Mencius rejects both and stands between. |
| c. 90 CE | Acts 4:32 | No one called anything their own; they held all in common. |
| 533 CE | Justinian's Institutes | Air, running water and the sea are common to all. |
| 1235 to 1850s | English enclosure | Common fields fenced into private ones, by statute. |
| 1649 | Winstanley | The earth as a common treasury. |
| 1807 | Hegel | The I that is We, and the We that is I. |
| 1893 | Durkheim | A collective consciousness, more than the sum of its members. |
| 1968, 1990 | Hardin; Ostrom | The "tragedy of the commons"; then field evidence that users often govern commons well. |
| 1969 | Mbiti | I am because we are. |
| 1992 | Mabo v Queensland | Australia's High Court ends terra nullius, "land of no one". |
| 2017 | [Te Awa Tupua Act](https://ecojurisprudence.org/initiatives/te-awa-tupua-act-2017/) | "I am the river and the river is me" enters statute, and the Whanganui River becomes a legal person. |

Hegel and Ubuntu already stand in Me 2.1. The Whanganui saying is the nearest living statement of a self whose me includes an other that is not human.

## Not I, not mine, not myself

The negations are as old as the affirmations, and the Buddhist formula states all three at once. In most traditions they are a practice of release. In medicine they are a finding.

| When | Where | The negation |
|---|---|---|
| c. 700 BCE | Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad | The self is told by negation: "not this, not this". |
| 5th c. BCE | [Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta](https://encyclopediaofbuddhism.org/wiki/Anattalakkhana_Sutta) | Of body, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness: "This is not mine, this I am not, this is not my self." |
| 5th c. BCE | Na Tumhaka Sutta | Let go of what is not yours. |
| 4th to 2nd c. BCE | Zhuangzi | "Now I have lost me." |
| c. 200 BCE to 200 CE | Bhagavad Gītā 2.71 | The one at peace is without "mine" and without I-making. |
| c. 55 CE | Paul, Galatians 2:20 | It is no longer I who live. |
| 9th to 13th c. | Sufism; Rumi | Fanāʾ, the passing away of self. In Rumi's story the door opens to the one who answers "you", not "I". |
| 1794 | Fichte | The not-I is what the I sets against itself. |
| 1923 | Freud | The I and "the it" (das Es): what in me is not I. |
| 1949 | Burnet and Fenner | Not-self in the body: what the immune system does not treat as its own. |
| Clinical | Somatoparaphrenia; depersonalization | A patient denies that a limb is theirs. A self feels unreal to itself. |

The Buddhist formula denies exactly three things: a mine, an I and a myself. Me 2.4 sets a self by relation beside a self by exclusion. Whether the formula belongs with either is for the expedition to test.

## Only I can

"Only I can" enters the record as a moral duty, becomes an inner certainty, and ends as law and clinical rule. Philosophy since Wittgenstein has pushed back on the "only".

| When | Who | What only I can do |
|---|---|---|
| 5th c. BCE | Dhammapada 165 | Purify myself. No one purifies another. |
| c. 441 BCE | Sophocles, Antigone | Judge what I owe, against the city's law. |
| c. 200 BCE to 200 CE | Bhagavad Gītā 3.35 | Do my own duty. Better mine done poorly than another's done well. |
| c. 30 BCE to 10 CE | Hillel, Avot 1:14 | Be for myself, and not only for myself, and now. |
| c. 110 CE | Epictetus | Choose. Only my choosing is mine; body, goods and repute are not. |
| 1522 | Luther | Believe, as each must do their own dying. |
| 1784 | Kant | Use my own understanding. |
| 1914, 1947 | Schloendorff; Nuremberg Code | Consent to what is done to my body. |
| 1927 | Heidegger | Exist as "in each case mine". No one can take over another's dying. |
| 1953 | Wittgenstein | Know my pain? He examines the sentence and refuses it: partly false, partly without sense. |
| 1968 | McCaffery | Say what my pain is. Pain is what the person in pain says it is. |
| 1974 | Nagel | Have this point of view: what it is like to be this one. |

Me 3.1 names uniquenessing: only one possibly same-existing. These rows are where the fields meet that "only", and where they dispute it.

## Where the journeys cross and meet

Ten crossings stand out, and the first joins this page to the universe page: the atom of 430 BCE became the individual of 512 CE.

| Crossing | When | What met |
|---|---|---|
| Atom meets person | c. 512 | Boethius calls the person an "individual", his word for the uncuttable atom. The particle of the first page becomes the self of this one. |
| Same meets self | Old English to 1694 | "Self" meant "same". Locke makes sameness of self a matter of memory and of law. |
| Me meets mine | 1646 to 1890 | Overton and Locke ground liberty in owning oneself. James then finds me and mine barely separable. |
| Common meets immune | Rome to 1949 | A word for exemption from shared duty becomes medicine's word for the body defending its "self". |
| I meets we | 1807 to 1934 | Hegel, then Mead: no I without recognition by others. Both stand in Me. |
| Mask meets river | Rome to 2017 | "Person" runs from mask, to legal standing, to soul, to corporation, to a river. |
| Not-self meets no self | 5th c. BCE; 1739; 1984 | The Buddha, Hume and Parfit reach neighbouring conclusions. Parfit cites the Buddha. A route from Buddhism to Hume has been proposed and is unproven. |
| Flying man meets cogito | c. 1020; 1641 | Avicenna's argument circulated in Latin for centuries before Descartes. Direct borrowing is debated. |
| Mine meets ours | c. 375 BCE; 1968; 1990 | Plato against Aristotle is replayed as Hardin against Ostrom. |
| "For me" meets "care for all" | 4th c. BCE; 1851 | Yang Zhu against Mozi. Europe names the same pair "egoism" and "altruism" only when Comte coins the second word. |

[Marcel Mauss](https://anthroencyclopedia.com/node/1741) drew the first such map in 1938. He traced "person" from role and mask, through Roman law and Christian moral standing, to the modern self of Kant and Fichte, and held that the modern self is a recent and local form.

## Where it is debated now

Each of these words is in open public dispute today, and each dispute continues one of the journeys above.

| The word | The live debate | The journey it continues |
|---|---|---|
| I, you, they | Pronouns: singular "they", old in English and argued over since the 2010s. Quakers said "thee" to everyone in the 1650s for the same reason. | I, the one saying |
| My body | Consent, bodily autonomy, and tissue. In Moore v. Regents (1990) a patient was held to have no property in his removed cells. | Only I can; mine and yours |
| My data | Privacy began as "the right to be let alone" (1890). It now covers data protection and Indigenous data sovereignty. | Mine and yours |
| Whose words | Language models are trained on the writing of very many people, and who may call the result theirs is unsettled. This document was drafted by one. | Ours, and not ours |
| Ours | Atmosphere, oceans, orbit and the genome as commons. | Ours, and not ours |
| Person | Rivers, animals and corporations, and now AI systems. Me 2.3 already places trained models among non-living others. | Mask meets river |
| Self | The microbiome and holobiont; independent and interdependent selves (Markus and Kitayama, 1991). Me 3.4 holds the first. | Me and myself |

In each child the order repeats in small. Shared attention with another comes at about nine months, self-recognition at about eighteen, and "mine" at about two years. The between comes before the I, and the I before the mine.
