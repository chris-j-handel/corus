## Scientific explaining from material to date

### Carbon incorporation and the event being dated

The scientific account distinguishes stable carbon-12 and carbon-13 from radioactive carbon-14. Carbon-14 changes to nitrogen-14 through beta-minus decay: an electron and an antineutrino are emitted in that account. Atmospheric production supplies carbon-14; carbon dioxide enters plants, and feeding transfers carbon into animals. Carbon-14 already undergoes radioactive change during living. Continuing intake and replacement affect the composition that remains. Stopping replacement does not initiate decay. [S1]

A particular tissue or compound can stop replacing carbon while the organism remains alive. An inner tree ring is a useful example. Conversely, burial does not guarantee isolation: roots, groundwater, microbes or conservation materials can introduce other carbon. Dating therefore concerns the selected material and its incorporation history, rather than a universal instant called death. [S2, S3]

Carbon dating is not generally DNA dating. Laboratories select such fractions as wood cellulose, bone collagen, charcoal, or shell carbonate. DNA can be isolated for particular radiocarbon studies, but a date for bone collagen is not a measurement of DNA strands. [S4, S5]

### Preparation is part of the observing

Laboratories separate the intended material and use pretreatments appropriate to it. Standards and blanks accompany preparation. In a common AMS route, the selected carbon is converted to purified carbon dioxide and then graphite. Some AMS instruments also accept gas.

This is a decisive relationship for the proposed explaining: the original object’s surface, its molecular arrangement and any original DNA sequence are not retained as the shape interrogated by the AMS detector. The analysis follows the carbon through preparation, with contamination and fractionation controlled. [S4, S6]

A date may be affected if the selected fraction is inappropriate, if preparation leaves foreign carbon, or if preparation introduces it. It is therefore necessary to preserve the fraction selected, carbon yield, blank contribution and reference measurements with the reported result. More counting cannot repair a wrong sample association. [S4, S7]

### Three different observing methods

| Method | Instrument response | Scientific interpretation | Relationship any alternative explaining must retain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accelerator mass spectrometry | Currents in abundant-carbon collectors and individual events in the rare-carbon detector, after controlled beam separation | Relative abundances of carbon isotopes | Different carbon assignments remain distinguishable after separation, acceleration and removal of molecular interference. |
| Decay counting | Electrical pulses in gas counters or light signals in scintillation systems | Radioactive activity, corrected for efficiency and background | Emission-associated signals occur in prepared material; the account must specify their origin and relation to carbon abundance. |
| Optical spectroscopy | The loss of light in a gas-filled optical cavity varies with wavelength | Absorption associated with particular carbon-dioxide isotopic forms | An explaining must also reach spectral distinctions in gas, not only the AMS beam or a weathered solid surface. |

Penn State describes its AMS isotope separation and detectors. Australian National University describes gas and scintillation counting. NIST researchers report optical measurements in gas-phase carbon dioxide below one fraction modern. These methods have different apparatus and corrections; they do not constitute one unqualified claim that every technique yields an identical result for every sample. [S8, S1, S9]

One concrete optical observing uses gas at 220 K and 0.9 kPa. The recorded light-loss curves are fitted to obtain decay times; wavelength-dependent absorption then yields an inferred carbon-14 proportion. The reported combined uncertainty corresponds to 0.11 fraction modern. The optical light-decay interval is an instrument response, not carbon-14's thousands-of-years half-life. These two uses of “decay” must remain separate. [S9]

### Measuring a ratio

The central AMS comparison is the abundance assigned to carbon-14 relative to stable carbon, standardized and corrected. Carbon-13 measurements help address isotope fractionation: natural processes and preparation can favor one isotope relative to another. The resulting age is not inferred from the remaining mass of wood or the depth of a worn surface. [S10]

A hypothetical calculation makes the difference exact:

- Before nonselective removal: 10 units of a rare component for 1,000 units of a common component.
- After removing half of each: 5 for 500.
- The ratio remains 1 to 100.

These numbers illustrate arithmetic, not natural isotope abundance. For any positive remaining fraction f:

**(f × rare amount) / (f × common amount) = rare amount / common amount.**

Thus nonselective loss alone cannot change the ratio used for dating. Selective loss, mixing and transformation can change it, but each supplies an additional relation that must be specified and checked.

### Inferring a long half-life without watching thousands of years

A long half-life can be inferred by combining an independently determined number of radioactive atoms, N, with their measured activity, A, under the exponential-decay model:

**A = λN; half-life = ln(2) × N / A.**

Manov and Curtiss combined mass-spectrographic abundance with gas counting. Varying the counter's recovery interval from 350 to 1,300 microseconds changed recorded rates from 208.7 to 173.2 counts per second; their selected correction gave 225.1 and 223.5. They compared correction formulas and selected the one giving the least systematic dependence on that interval. Their inferred half-life, 5,370 ± 200 years, is historical, not the modern value. Converting abundance and corrected activity into half-life assumes the decay relation; this was not observation through a whole half-life. [S11]

Caswell, Brabant and Schwebel subsequently used an ionization-chamber energy-emission method and an average beta energy to infer activity. They reported approximately nine percent disagreement with the earlier gas-counting assignment, independent of the shared isotope-abundance determination. Their paper suggested multiple pulses in the gas counter as a possible explanation, without establishing it. Its historical half-life estimate was 5,900 ± 250 years. These records demonstrate a specific disagreement between measurement routes, not a measured change in carbon's half-life. [S40]

This gives the expedition an observing before the final accounting: apparatus participation changes which pulses are registered, and additional operations support the inference from those pulses to a rate assigned to the prepared material. A correction yielding a nearly constant result across settings is a consistency check conditional on its assumptions; it is not a direct observing of an unchanging entity. Disagreement with another route keeps those assumptions open to investigation.

The natural explaining can follow the investigators' distinguishing, revising and using their revised preparation alongside the detector's participation. These historical papers do not identify that participation with the proposed carbon bump connector. The particular joining still needed is from the proposed co-sequencing to the recorded dependence on apparatus settings and to the activity remaining after corrections. The counter's technical term “resolving time” names an electronic recovery interval and must not be silently equated with the expedition's resolving.

Approximately 5,730 years is the familiar physical half-life used in introductory explanations. Conventional radiocarbon ages deliberately retain the historical 5,568-year value for consistency, and use 1950 as “present.” With normalized fraction modern F, the conventional age is approximately **−8033 ln(F)**. Half the reference proportion therefore corresponds to about 5,568 conventional radiocarbon years, not automatically 5,568 calendar years. [S12]

### Calendar calibration and archaeological association

Calibration compares radiocarbon measurements with independently dated material. Annual tree-ring patterns supply a principal calendar connection. Other dated archives extend the record. IntCal20 reaches 55,000 calendar years before 1950; its fully atmospheric tree-ring record reaches approximately 13,900 years before 1950. The older part combines archives with their own age and carbon-reservoir uncertainties. [S13]

The calibration relation has bends and plateaus. One measured value can correspond to several calendar intervals. A narrow analytical uncertainty can therefore coexist with a broad or divided calendar range. Reported statistical ranges depend on the calibration data and, where used, chronological assumptions. [S12]

A sample date and an event date remain distinct. Charcoal in a hearth can come from old inner wood; an object and nearby charcoal can have different histories. Short-lived samples and independently supported associations can narrow this concern. [S3]

The practical limit is commonly around 50,000 years and depends on material, contamination and laboratory background. A calibration curve extending to 55,000 years does not mean every sample can be reliably dated that far back. [S2, S13]

- carbon-14 changes as −(λ + k) times its abundance;
