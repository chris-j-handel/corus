## Genome duplication

1. Two goatsbeard species, *Tragopogon*, arose in eastern Washington within a century of their parents' arrival from Europe in the 1920s, each with twenty-four chromosomes, twice the parents'; from the first generation they lose and silence gene copies, and which copies go is repeatable and predicted (Pamela and Douglas Soltis, in Carrie Arnold, "Genome Duplication Is a Radical Evolutionary Gamble", Quanta Magazine, 2 September 2026, read whole).
2. Diploid snowflake yeast under selection for larger multicellular size became tetraploid within fifty days, their cells larger and longer, tetraploidy continuing across nearly five thousand generations and conserved aneuploidy following (Tong and colleagues, Nature 639:691, 2025, at its abstract).
3. The New Zealand mud snail *Potamopyrgus antipodarum* carries a whole-genome duplication after its split from *P. kaitunuparaoa*, its data best fit at a tetraploid model, 3,040 genes at two copies where relatives carry one (Jalinsky and colleagues, Genome Biology and Evolution 17(11), 2025, at its abstract); the duplication is under a million years old, about 60% of doubled genes already lost, and asexual females are triploid or tetraploid (the Quanta article).
4. In yeast, extra copies of each gene hinder adapting to nystatin (Otto's group, cited at the Quanta article, not read).
5. Genome duplication and loss of the gene ACE2, whose product separates daughter cells after division, give multicellular yeast (Oud and colleagues, PNAS 2013, at its title).
6. 132 ancient duplications across 470 flowering plants are reported clustering at the end-Cretaceous extinction, global cooling and the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (Chen and colleagues, Cell 2026, at a news account); a reply names five conceptual and methodological issues against the association (Marcussen and Meseguer, bioRxiv 2026, at its abstract).

## Twinning, conceiving and pregnancy
