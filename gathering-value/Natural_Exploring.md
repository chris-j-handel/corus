### 6.1 Co-competencing

- Two crews join through rock by each other's sharing. The Siloam inscription, from Hezekiah's tunnel in Jerusalem, gives an account of quarrymen digging toward each other from two ends, with three cubits still to cut a man's voice calling to his fellow through a fissure or overlap in the rock, axe striking axe as they break through, and water then going from the spring to the pool for 1,200 cubits, the rock 100 cubits over the stonecutters' heads. The tunnel of Eupalinos on Samos, of the sixth century BC, 1,036 m long, goes from two ends toward a joining under a hill over 200 m high, its two straight shafts each aiming toward that joining and a zigzag close to it: one reading is a turn by design, each shaft turning and the two crossing; some scholars read the tunnel's winding as errors and their correcting; and others read each crew as likely hearing the other's hammering through the rock and turning with it; its vertical offset at the joining given as 4 cm by Hermann Kienast and as 60 cm elsewhere. Each crew goes on from its own side, and a voice or a hammering through the rock, a surface opening between the two crews across, orients the last cubits: co-competencing, a fixing between the crews loosening two-way, the tunnel of rock its artifact and its joining in neither crew alone. *(possible exploring)*

### 6.7 A school, a flock, a voyaging people, an orienting surface in no self alone

- In starling flocks, a field's reconstruction of each bird's place and velocity in three dimensions finds the birds' velocity changes going together over a distance growing with the flock's size, the going together having no length of its own: a change in one bird goes with each other bird's, whatever the flock's size, each bird's reach of perceiving wider than its direct interacting, and a flock's response to a disturbance stronger with it. A flock's orienting is a surface in no bird alone, each bird with its six or seven nearest and its changing going with the whole. *(possible exploring)*
- A message reaches a far person through a society, each self orienting from its own side. In Travers and Milgram's letters, 296 people in Nebraska and Boston start letters toward a person working in Boston, and 64 reach him, with a mean of 5.2 people between; 3 people, each the last before the target, are in 48 percent of the chains reaching him. In Dodds, Muhamad and Watts' email chains, 61,168 senders in 166 countries, in 24,163 chains toward 18 people in 13 countries, bring 384 chains to their target in a mean of about 4 steps, a field's estimate of the median about 5 to 7 with the chains that end in its reckoning, 5 within one country and 7 across; each sender has only its own others, choosing by a place first and by a work after the third step; under 0.3 percent of senders not sending on say they find no one to send to, a lack of interest or incentive, not a difficulty, the main reason a chain ends, its authors suggest; and chains reaching their target send to hubs, a field's people with many ties, 1.6 percent against 8.2 percent in chains that end. In Kleinberg's model of a two-dimensional lattice, each holder sending the message on to its contact closest to the target finds a short path only as the lattice's long ties fall off with distance as the inverse square, its power the lattice's own dimension. A self in a society has no map of the society's ties: each sender aims from the is-not of the message's place to a person over its horizon, one parity at a time, closer or not, and a path in no self alone uncovers. *(possible exploring)*
- 26 olive baboons of one troop at Mpala in Kenya wear GPS collars, 25 giving their places once a second through each day's light over the first 14 days, 16 to 25 on a day: as some baboons go out ahead in a heading, the others follow more readily as more of them go out in one heading, and with a split among them a baboon follows less readily, a deciding coming later, its authors suggest; with two headings parting under about 90 degrees a follower goes between them, and over it with one of them, more often the heading more baboons go, and with two equal parts either about equally; a baboon's rank is-not going with others following it, and the highest-ranking male is-not the one others follow most. ### 6.9 Two priors, an elders' going and a kind's artifact

- Mau Piailug, a master navigator from Satawal in Micronesia, navigates Hōkūleʻa from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti in 1976 with no modern instruments, the atoll of Mataiva coming after some 30 days at sea. Over two Hawaiian winters he teaches Nainoa Thompson with sand diagrams, string, shells and the feel of ocean swells at sea, and with a star compass, Hawaiian star names in a frame of the horizon; an astronomer, Will Kyselka, studies the stars' going with Thompson in the Bishop Museum's planetarium over hundreds of hours. In spring 1980 Thompson navigates Hōkūleʻa to Tahiti and back, the first Hawaiian to practise wayfinding on long ocean voyages from about the 14th century, at which voyaging between Hawaiʻi and Tahiti stops. Long voyaging by wayfinding comes again through a master navigator of Satawal: one people's going passing across to another, as a song passes between whole societies, and along to a young self who opens with it as his prior, in no self alone. *(possible exploring)*
- In the 1993 drought at Tarangire in Tanzania, a drought of its size coming last in 1958 to 1961, 16 of 81 elephant calves in 21 family groups of three clans die over nine months, 20 percent, against an average of 2 percent a year over the 12 years before; most of two clans' groups go out of the park, their oldest matriarchs 45 and 38 years old, and the clan going on in the park, its oldest matriarch 33 and none of it 5 years old or more in the 1958 to 1961 drought, has 11 of the 16 deaths, at least 63 percent of the deaths coming inside the park; its authors speculate that the groups going out go for food and water outside the park, perhaps with their oldest matriarchs' experience of the earlier drought.
