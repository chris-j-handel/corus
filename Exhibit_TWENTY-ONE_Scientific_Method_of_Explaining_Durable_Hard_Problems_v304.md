# 1 The form and its criteria

## 1.1 Aim

This document does not propose answers. It does not favour a position in any problem it states. It sets out, for each problem, why the problem is hard given the way the field sets it up — and it does so by exhibiting the field's own record rather than by argument.

The intended reading is top to bottom, one entry at a time. At the end of an entry a reader who followed the science in it should be able to say: I understand the problem, I understand both answers, I have seen every route the field has taken, and I have seen where each one ends. Any way through would have to be a way nobody has found, and nothing in the setup shows where it would be.

That is a demonstration of hardness, not a proof of any answer. It is checkable in the ordinary way: every route is one the field took and recorded, and any route omitted is a defect in the entry that anyone holding the field's literature can correct.

## 1.2 What a durable problem is

**Durable problem.** A problem P is durable within a framework F when four criteria hold:

- **(i)** P is stated as two positions, mutually exclusive within F, each with holders who defend it.
- **(ii)** The argument for each is valid from premises F supplies.
- **(iii)** No instrument definable within F discriminates the two.
- **(iv)** (iii) follows from the statement of P together with F, without appeal to the record of attempts.

Criterion (iv) is the substantive one. It is what makes this a demonstration rather than a report that some problems have proved difficult, and it is what the form below exhibits.

Three other things are not durable problems and are distinguished from them throughout.

**Open problem.** No answer currently established, and an instrument discriminating the candidates is definable within F. Open problems close as instruments arrive. Entry 2.139 is the collection's worked example of one that took a century to close and is not durable.

**Ill-posed problem.** Underdetermined, inconsistent, or equivocal in statement, and remedied by restatement. Durable problems are well-posed; that is what makes them durable rather than confused.

**Resource-limited difficulty.** A computation or search too large to complete. The test: would an unlimited instrument within the observation domain discriminate the positions? If yes, the problem is resource-limited and not durable.

## 1.3 Why refinement does not discriminate

This is the load-bearing step and it is a quantifier argument rather than a claim about instruments.

An instrument returns values over a domain D. A statement may quantify over a domain D\* obtained from D by closure, by external framing, or by attribution across an interaction. Where D\* is not a subset of D, no finite family of readings over D determines a value over D\*.

Refinement increases resolution within D. It does not extend D to D\*.

**Result.** For a problem whose statement quantifies over a domain the field's instruments do not range over, no refinement of measurement within that domain discriminates the positions — however far the refinement is carried.

This is why the record shows what it shows: measurement running finer reads the same, data past hand-reading returns the same, computation at unprecedented rates returns the same. That record is what the result predicts, and it was predictable before the effort was expended.

The two lines of the form marked *what the question is about* and *what the field's instruments reach* state D\* and D for each entry. The line after them answers whether they are the same, and that single binary is the whole inference.

## 1.4 The form

```
The question                        in the field's own words

  One answer given
  The other answer given

Origin conditions                   the field's own published setup

What both answers require

  What the question is about
  What the field's instruments reach

Whether these are the same

Routes taken, and where each ends

  What has been settled so far
  What would settle the rest

What the field records              present only where the field records
                                    something about the question itself
```

**Origin conditions are not additions to a field's account.** They are the field's own founding commitments, published and uncontroversial, restated here at the problem. A field working within them has no reason to restate them at every question, and does not. This document restates them because a route's ending is legible only against the condition it was attempting to get around. Each origin condition is quotable from the field's own founding or methodological literature; one that cannot be is a defect in the entry.

## 1.5 The logic of the form

Three forkings and two joinings, alternating.

**Fork.** The question parts into two answers, each stated as its holders give it.

**Join.** What both answers require. The two disagree, so what they share is not what they disagree about — it is what makes the disagreement statable at all. The test is requirement: if either argument runs without it, it does not belong here; if either fails without it, it does, whatever kind of thing it is.

**Fork.** What the question is about, and what the field's instruments reach.

**Join.** Whether these are the same. This is the only inference in the document and it is binary. Same, and the problem is open: refinement will reach it. Different, and refinement will not, however far it is carried.

**Fork.** What has been settled, and what remains. Both lines carry content in most entries: a great deal is often settled without the question being settled, and that is stronger evidence of hardness than an empty line.

The second of these lines states **the form a discovery would take** — not what is missing, but what shape an answer would have if one arrived. A design returning an attribution invariant across environments. A measure that continues to track after being made a target. A criterion of function agreed before the measurement is taken. Stated this way the line is a specification: it says what would be recognised as the answer, so that a result arriving can be checked against it rather than argued about. Where the answer is proved to be unavailable, the line says so and names the proof.

This is also where the file records progress. A result arriving moves content from the second line to the first, and the entry stays intact around the move.

Between the last join and the last fork stands the routes line, which is what carries a reader to the end.

## 1.6 Routes

A route is a way the field has tried to get from the setup to an answer. Each is recorded with where it ends, and the ending is the field's own — a null result, a proved equivalence, a stated limitation, an acknowledged circularity, a bound that narrows without closing.

Eight endings recur across the collection. They are distinguished because they are not the same kind of ending, and because a reader assessing an entry should be able to see which kind each route reached.

**1. Ends by measuring more of what was already reached.** The route returns readings from the instruments' own domain, and the question is not about that domain. This answers the question a reader is most likely to ask — why has more data not settled this. In every entry where it appears, the additional measurement is real and the field regards it as progress.

**2. Ends by empirical equivalence.** The route reproduces every prediction of its rival, so no measurement distinguishes them. These routes are not failures; they are successes that leave the question untouched by construction.

**3. Ends by explaining something adjacent.** The route succeeds at a related question, and the field states in its own words that this is not the question. This ending most often looks like a solution from outside a field and is not treated as one inside it.

**4. Ends by narrowing without closing.** The route makes real predictions, searches return null, parameter space shrinks, and the route stays open. Nothing here forecasts that such a route fails.

**5. Ends at a proved limitation on the method.** The field establishes what any method of a given kind can deliver, and the limit is a theorem. These are the strongest single items of evidence in the collection, because the field established the limit rather than encountered it. Examples across unrelated fields: the relativization, natural proofs and algebrization barriers; the parity problem in sieve theory, which blocks two number theory entries at the same place; Gibbard–Satterthwaite following Arrow; Rice's theorem; the causal-discovery equivalence class; the multitask distortion theorem; Tao's averaged Navier–Stokes construction; higher-order vagueness; Tarski's undefinability theorem.

**6. Ends because the field's instruments disagree on the same object.** Two accepted measurement paradigms applied to one phenomenon return incompatible results, and the field has not reconciled them. Examples: the three criteria of genomic function returning different fractions; the two imaging paradigms in phantom limb returning opposite directions; convergent network effects against agent-specific signatures in anesthesia.

**7. Ends by dissolving the rivalry through accounting equivalence.** Two apparently rival accounts are proved to give the same predictions under general conditions, so they are two bookkeepings of one process rather than two hypotheses. The dispute is not settled; it is shown to have been about the accounting. Example: inclusive fitness and multilevel selection.

**8. Ends by absorbing the rival phenomenon at the cost of testability.** The account is extended to accommodate what the rival predicted, and the extension removes the difference that could have been tested. The field states the cost. Examples: the moving-setpoint model in homeostasis; continuum framings in niche-neutral theory.

A route still open is recorded as open.

**Closure.** Each entry ends its routes block with a closure line naming which of the eight endings its routes reached. A reader can scan the closure lines alone and see the shape of each entry's difficulty without reading every route. Every entry carries one. The three entries that do not fill the form carry a closure line stating why the endings do not apply to them.

## 1.7 Where a form fails to fill

Three lines can fail to fill, and each failure is a reading rather than a gap.

**No second answer given.** Criterion (i) fails: not a pair. Entries 2.139 and 2.142 record five problems this way, entered and marked rather than removed.
**Nothing both answers require.** Not one question.
**The two domains are the same.** Criterion (iii) fails: the problem is open rather than durable. No entry currently carries this failure.

An entry that does not fill carries a shape of its own, and the same one each time: the question, which line fails and why, what is recorded, and what would make it fill. Three entries carry it.

A failure to fill is recorded at the entry, not at the door. Nothing considered has been left out of this document: every candidate that arrived is entered, and where the form fails to fill, the failure and its reason are written at the entry. Whether the criteria would exclude anything is therefore untested here, and stating them is not the same as exercising them.

## 1.8 What this document does not claim

No claim about the truth of any position. Where a position is a conjecture, its truth value is untouched.

No claim that any measurement, prediction, or engineering result is incorrect.

No claim that a route which has not closed will close.

No forecast about problems not yet stated.

## 1.9 Falsification

**F1.** An entry whose question is about the same thing its field's instruments reach — the join line answered wrongly.
**F2.** A route taken by the field and not listed, which does not end where the entry says routes end.
**F3.** An entry in which one answer's argument is shown invalid from the field's own premises. If general, the collection reduces to ordinary error diagnosis.
**F4.** An entry settled by refinement within the instruments' domain.
**F5.** An origin condition stated in an entry that the field does not hold, or that cannot be found in the field's own founding or methodological literature. Origin conditions are recall, not attribution: a condition a field would decline to recognise as its own is a defect in the entry.
**F6.** A route ending stated more strongly or more weakly than the field's own record supports. Overstating and understating fail the same standard, and understating is the harder of the two to detect.

F2 is the one that matters most and it is the one anyone can run, since it requires only the field's literature. F5 and F6 are runnable by a specialist in a single field on a single entry.

## 1.10 A living file, and the nye marks

Entries are added as problems arrive and revised as fields report further routes.

Where a route record is stated from general knowledge rather than from a field's documented record, the entry carries the mark **nye**, which names the specific claim or route that would need checking. A nye is not a hedge and not a general caution: a mark that does not name something checkable is a defect. The marks stay visible until the check is run, and they concentrate in fields whose records move quickly.

## 2.1 The measurement problem

**The question.** The equation governing a quantum system describes it as evolving into a combination of possibilities. Every measurement finds one. What accounts for the one?

**One answer given.** Nothing further is needed. The evolution is linear, deterministic, and confirmed to extraordinary precision; nothing in the formalism marks a selection; outcomes are relative to a branch or to a record.

**The other answer given.** Something is missing. Measurements return one result, once, and any account on which all results occur owes a derivation of the observed frequencies.

**Origin conditions.** That a quantum system is completely described by its state; that the state evolves by the linear equation; that measurement is an interaction between systems described by the same theory.

**What both answers require.** That the outcome is fixed by a specifiable term — the state and its evolution, or the state together with a selection rule.

**What the question is about.** The selection at the interaction.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Outcomes after selection.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Refine the measurement.* Returns more outcomes, each one outcome. Ends by measuring more of what was already reached.
- *Dynamical collapse models.* Predict deviations from standard quantum mechanics, which is a genuine test. Interferometry, gravitational-wave detector noise, and spontaneous X-ray emission searches have constrained the parameter space and found nothing. Narrows without closing; still open.
- *Hidden variables, de Broglie–Bohm.* Reproduces every prediction exactly. Ends by empirical equivalence. Bell's theorem requires any such theory to be nonlocal.
- *Decoherence.* Accounts for why superpositions become unobservable. Ends by explaining something adjacent; its own principal authors state that it does not by itself select an outcome.
- *Many worlds.* Removes the need for a selection. The derivations of the probability rule from branch structure are contested within the field on grounds of circularity.
- *Epistemic readings of the state.* Treat the state as information about outcomes. Leaves open what occurs.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 2, 3 and 4. Refinement returns more post-selection outcomes; de Broglie–Bohm is empirically equivalent; decoherence explains an adjacent question by its own authors' statement; collapse models narrow and remain open.

**What has been settled so far.** Local hidden variables are excluded, by Bell and by loophole-free experiments. Collapse parameters are bounded over a substantial range. Decoherence timescales are measured and confirmed.
**What would settle the rest.** A measured deviation from linear evolution at any scale, or a derivation of the probability rule from branch structure that survives the circularity objection.

**What the field records.** That decoherence does not by itself resolve the measurement problem is stated by the authors who developed it.


## 2.2 P versus NP

**The question.** For every problem whose proposed solutions can be checked quickly, can a solution also be found quickly?

**One answer given.** No; the classes are distinct. No polynomial-time algorithm has been found for any NP-complete problem despite concentrated effort, and the known barriers to proving collapse are themselves theorems. The absence is structured.

**The other answer given.** Not established. No proof of separation exists; barrier results constrain techniques rather than the object; complexity theory contains collapses that were expected to be separations.

**Origin conditions.** That problems sort into classes by the resources a machine needs to solve them; that reductions between problems compose, which is what makes a class rather than a list; that polynomial time is the feasibility class, selected partly for being closed under composition.

**What both answers require.** That finding and checking are two properties of one class of objects, comparable by reduction, where reductions compose. The composing is what makes these classes rather than lists.

**What the question is about.** All algorithms.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Finitely many algorithms, and proof techniques of known kinds.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Find a fast algorithm.* Sixty years, none for any complete problem. Ends by measuring more of what was reached.
- *Diagonalization.* Baker, Gill and Solovay: relativizes, and oracles exist making the answer go either way. Ends as a proved limitation on the technique.
- *Circuit lower bounds.* Razborov and Rudich: any proof that is natural, in a precise sense their paper defines, would break the pseudorandom generators the field believes exist. Ends as a proved limitation.
- *Algebraic methods.* Aaronson and Wigderson: algebrizes, and the barrier extends. Ends as a proved limitation.
- *Geometric complexity theory.* A programme designed to evade the barriers, requiring representation-theoretic results not yet obtained. Open, and its own architects describe the timescale as long.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5, with 5 dominant. Three proved barriers, each closing a class of techniques, and the search for an algorithm returning more of what was reached.

**What has been settled so far.** Three barrier results, each proved, each closing a class of techniques. Completeness is established for thousands of problems, so the question is one question.
**What would settle the rest.** A proof either way.

**What the field records.** That polynomial time is selected as the feasibility class partly for being closed under composition, and that the closure property is named robustness and offered as a reason for the choice.


## 2.3 The explanatory gap

**The question.** A complete physical description of a brain is available. Does it account for what it is like to be that brain?

**One answer given.** Yes, or it will. Every intervention on the substrate alters experience predictably; no experience is observed without a substrate; the appearance of a gap is a limitation of current description.

**The other answer given.** No, and the argument is not evidential. A complete third-person description is compatible with more than one first-person assignment. This is shown by argument, so it does not weaken as neuroscience advances.

**Origin conditions.** That the physical facts about a system are exhaustively statable in third-person terms; that if experience is physical, it is fixed by those facts.

**What both answers require.** That there are two descriptions, one third-person accessible and one not, and that determination runs from one to the other.

**What the question is about.** The first-person assignment.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Third-person description.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Neural correlates.* Identified for many contents, increasingly precisely. Ends by explaining something adjacent; a correlate is agreed by both sides to be a correlate.
- *Global workspace and higher-order theories.* Account for reportability, access, and integration. Ends by explaining something adjacent; both sides agree these are the accessible functions.
- *Integrated information.* Proposes a measure, and makes some predictions. Its assignments to simple systems are disputed within the field, and the measure is not computable for systems of interesting size.
- *Denying the second description.* Illusionism holds that there is nothing further to account for. Its critics hold that the appearance to be explained away is the thing in question.
- *Waiting for neuroscience.* The deductive argument does not weaken with data, so accumulation does not address it.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 3. Correlates and access theories address the accessible functions, which both sides agree is a different question; the deductive argument is unaffected by accumulation.

**What has been settled so far.** A great deal about access, report, integration and their neural bases. The accessible functions are the ones on which the field has advanced, which both sides accept.
**What would settle the rest.** An instrument reading the first-person assignment from the third-person description, or a demonstration that the compatibility argument equivocates.

**What the field records.** That the problem is distinguished from the problems of access and report, and that progress on the latter is not progress on the former, is the field's own standing distinction.


## 2.4 The problem of induction

**The question.** Does the fact that a regularity has held so far give reason to expect it to hold next time?

**One answer given.** Yes. Inductive practice is indispensable and uniformly successful, with no competitor holding any record. A justification that cannot be given non-circularly may still be a justification.

**The other answer given.** No justification has been given. Any argument from past to future requires a premise that the future resembles the past, supported only inductively.

**Origin conditions.** That observation supports generalisation; that the support is a relation between statements about observed instances and statements about unobserved ones.

**What both answers require.** That observed and unobserved instances are two collections, with the question being whether a relation established over one transfers to the other.

**What the question is about.** Unobserved instances.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observed instances.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Inductive justification of induction.* Circular by construction, as both sides agree.
- *Probabilistic reconstruction.* Bayesian conditionalisation yields convergence results, given a prior. The prior is not derived, and the convergence theorems assume the very regularity in question. Ends by relocating the premise.
- *Pragmatic vindication.* Reichenbach: if any method works, induction works. Establishes a conditional, not the antecedent, as he states.
- *Falsificationism.* Popper: no induction is needed, only conjecture and refutation. Its critics hold that choosing among unrefuted theories reintroduces it, and the field has not settled this.
- *Grue.* Goodman shows the problem recurs at predicate selection, so a solution at instances would not reach it.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Probabilistic reconstruction relocates the premise; Reichenbach's vindication establishes a conditional and not its antecedent; the circle is agreed by both sides.

**What has been settled so far.** That the circle is real and has not been broken; that probabilistic reconstructions require a prior; that the problem recurs at predicate level.
**What would settle the rest.** A derivation of the uniformity premise from premises not themselves inductive.


## 2.5 Dark matter

**The question.** Galaxies rotate faster at their edges than the visible mass accounts for. Is there unseen mass, or is the law of gravity different at those scales?

**One answer given.** Unseen mass. The same discrepancy appears in clusters, in lensing, and in the microwave background, and one cold component fits all with few parameters. The Bullet Cluster shows lensing mass separated from the visible gas.

**The other answer given.** Modified dynamics. A single acceleration scale predicts rotation curves with high accuracy, including the tight relation between visible mass and rotation speed, which a distribution of unseen mass does not require. Direct searches have returned nothing at the predicted couplings.

**Origin conditions.** That gravity acts as general relativity states at all scales; that luminous matter traces total matter by a determinable factor; that the observed dynamics is produced by mass distribution under that law.

**What both answers require.** That the discrepancy is produced either by an unobserved substance or by an incorrect law, and that these exhaust the possibilities.

**What the question is about.** Whether the mass present or the law in force accounts for the discrepancy, the two being separately specifiable.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Luminous inference and dynamical inference, each checking the other and neither reaching the quantity alone.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Direct detection.* Xenon-based detectors have improved sensitivity by orders of magnitude and returned null, approaching the neutrino floor. Narrows without closing; open.
- *Collider production.* No excess. Narrows without closing.
- *Indirect detection.* No confirmed signal. Narrows without closing.
- *Relativistic modified theories.* TeVeS and successors; the gravitational-wave speed measurement of 2017 excluded a class of them. Some remain; cluster-scale fits remain difficult for all.
- *Cosmological structure.* Cold dark matter reproduces large-scale structure; modified dynamics has no equivalent success at that scale, which its proponents acknowledge.

**Closure.** Ending 4 throughout, with one closure. Baryonic candidates are excluded outright; both remaining programmes narrow without closing.

**What has been settled so far.** Baryonic candidates are excluded by microlensing and nucleosynthesis. A large class of relativistic modifications is excluded by the wave-speed measurement. Large regions of the detection parameter space are excluded.
**What would settle the rest.** A direct detection with a measured cross-section, or a relativistic theory reproducing cluster and cosmological observations without additional mass.


## 2.6 Heritability and the determination of traits

**The question.** For a trait that varies across people, is the variation produced by genetic differences or by environmental differences?

**One answer given.** Genetic differences dominate. Twin and adoption designs return substantial heritability for nearly every measured trait, replicated across cohorts, decades and countries, and molecular data recover polygenic signal in the same direction.

**The other answer given.** The question as posed has no answer. Heritability is a ratio specific to a population and its range of environments, not a property of a trait. Change the range and the ratio changes; environmental interventions produce large trait changes no genetic account predicts.

**Origin conditions.** That a trait's variation in a population decomposes into components attributable to sources; that a design holding one source fixed estimates the other's contribution.

**What both answers require.** That the outcome is to be attributed to a determining source, and that a design holding one source fixed while apportioning variance to the other bears on that attribution.

**What the question is about.** An attribution invariant across environments and genetic compositions.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Conditional quantities specific to the range sampled.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Larger twin samples.* Estimates stabilise. Ends by measuring more of what was reached: a more precise ratio for the sampled range.
- *Genome-wide association at scale.* Recovers increasing but still partial variance. The gap is itself an entry in this collection.
- *Adoption and migration designs.* Vary the environmental range and the estimates move, which is what the second answer predicts.
- *Gene-environment interaction models.* Estimate interaction terms, which are design-dependent and typically underpowered; the field states this.
- *Within-family designs.* Remove population stratification and reduce estimated effects, showing part of the earlier estimate was not what it was taken to be.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. Larger samples return a more precise conditional; Lewontin's distinction is a proved limitation on what the design returns.

**What has been settled so far.** That heritability is population- and environment-specific is established and not disputed within quantitative genetics. Within-family designs have quantified part of the inflation.
**What would settle the rest.** A design returning an attribution invariant across environmental ranges and genetic compositions.

**What the field records.** Lewontin's distinction between an analysis of variance and an analysis of causes states inside the field that a share apportioned to one factor while another is held fixed says nothing about either factor at other levels.


## 2.7 Simultaneity and the present

**The question.** Is there a single present moment shared by everything, or is "now" only relative to an observer's motion?

**One answer given.** There is a present. Change is the most evident feature of experience, and an account on which all times are equally real must reconstruct change as a relation among tenseless facts, which its critics take to be no reconstruction of change.

**The other answer given.** There is no shared present. The relativity of simultaneity is established and precisely confirmed; a universal present requires a privileged slicing, and no measurement identifies one.

**Origin conditions.** That the laws are the same in every inertial frame; that no measurement identifies a preferred frame; that simultaneity is defined by a signalling convention within a frame.

**What both answers require.** That the question is which description is correct, and that one of them is.

**What the question is about.** Which slicing, if any, is the real one.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Measurements taken in a frame.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Look for a preferred frame.* The microwave background rest frame exists and is measurable, and is not dynamically privileged; nothing in the laws refers to it. Ends by explaining something adjacent.
- *Neo-Lorentzian reconstruction.* Reproduces every prediction of relativity with a privileged frame added. Ends by empirical equivalence.
- *Presentism with a preferred foliation.* Consistent, and requires a foliation no measurement identifies.
- *Tenseless reconstruction of passage.* Accounts for the temporal structure of experience within a block. Its critics hold that it accounts for the appearance rather than the passage; the dispute is unsettled.
- *Quantum gravity.* Some programmes reintroduce a preferred slicing. None is established.

**Closure.** Endings 2 and 3. Neo-Lorentzian reconstruction is empirically equivalent; the microwave background frame is an adjacent finding.

**What has been settled so far.** The relativity of simultaneity, to high precision. The absence of any dynamically privileged frame in the tested laws.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurable privileged foliation, or a reconstruction of passage satisfying the objection on its own terms.


## 2.8 Aggregation of preferences

**The question.** Can individual rankings of options be combined into a single group ranking that is fair by stated conditions?

**One answer given.** No. Arrow's theorem: with unrestricted domain, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and no dictator, no such rule exists. The proof is complete.

**The other answer given.** Yes, routinely. Groups combine preferences continually and the results are used. The independence condition excludes intensity information actual procedures use; rules admitting cardinal comparability lie outside the theorem's domain.

**Origin conditions.** That individual preferences are orderings; that aggregation is a function from profiles of orderings to a social ordering; that the function must satisfy conditions stated in advance.

**What both answers require.** That aggregation is a function from profiles of individual orderings to a social ordering, and that the dispute is over which conditions it must satisfy.

**What the question is about.** All rules satisfying the stated conditions.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Particular procedures and their outcomes.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Weaken independence.* Admits Borda and scoring rules, which become manipulable; Gibbard–Satterthwaite proves that any non-dictatorial rule over three or more options is manipulable. Ends at a second impossibility.
- *Restrict the domain.* Single-peaked preferences admit majority rule, by Black's theorem. Ends by holding only where the restriction holds, which is an empirical matter case by case.
- *Cardinal utility.* Escapes the theorem; requires interpersonal comparison, which the field has not established a basis for.
- *Probabilistic rules.* Escape some conditions; Gibbard's random dictatorship result constrains them.
- *Judgment aggregation.* The same structure recurs for propositions, with its own impossibility results.

**Closure.** Ending 5 throughout. Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, Black and Gibbard's randomisation result are four proved limitations on classes of rules.

**What has been settled so far.** Arrow, Gibbard–Satterthwaite, Black, Gibbard's randomisation result, and the doctrinal paradox in judgment aggregation. Each is proved.
**What would settle the rest.** Within the conditions, nothing: the results are proved. Outside them, the question is empirical about which procedures satisfy which conditions.

**What the field records.** That independence of irrelevant alternatives requires the social ranking of a pair to depend on individual rankings of that pair and nothing else, and that the proof proceeds by altering one individual's ranking with all others held fixed.


## 2.9 The halting problem

**The question.** Is there a general procedure that, given any program and input, decides whether the program eventually stops?

**One answer given.** No, and this is proved. Turing's construction derives a contradiction from the assumption. Rice's theorem extends it: no non-trivial property of a program's behaviour is decidable from the program.

**The other answer given.** The result is accepted; its reach is disputed. Verification succeeds on programs people write, termination checkers handle large classes, and undecidable cases are constructed rather than encountered.

**Origin conditions.** That a computation is a finite description operating on finite input; that properties of the computation are to be established from the description rather than by running it.

**What both answers require.** That a property of an execution can be established without executing it.

**What the question is about.** All programs and inputs.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Finite descriptions, examined finitely.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Restrict the language.* Total languages guarantee termination and lose expressiveness exactly in proportion. A known and exact trade.
- *Termination checkers.* Size-change and ranking-function methods succeed widely and are incomplete by construction, which their authors state.
- *Bounded checking.* Answers for a bounded run, which is not the question.
- *Interactive proof.* Shifts the burden to a person supplying the invariant, which the theorem does not forbid and does not scale automatically.
- *Probabilistic or average-case decidability.* Results exist for restricted distributions; the general statement is unaffected.

**Closure.** Ending 5, with the trade stated exactly. Turing and Rice are proved; the language restriction's expressiveness cost is exact and known.

**What has been settled so far.** The theorem itself, Rice's generalisation — which is what makes this one closure rather than a list of separate undecidable properties — and the exact expressiveness cost of total languages. Termination checking succeeds on large classes of programs written in practice, with incompleteness by construction.
**What would settle the rest.** Nothing: the result is proved. The disputed content is scope, settled case by case.

**What the field records.** That the statement contains no subject matter — it concerns procedures and their execution and nothing else.


## 2.10 Free will and determinism

**The question.** If every event is fixed by prior events and physical law, can a person act otherwise than they do?

**One answer given.** No. A person is a physical system, and physical systems evolve by law from prior states. Indeterminacy does not help: a randomly produced action is no more the person's own.

**The other answer given.** Persons act otherwise in the sense that matters. Deliberation and reasons are features of how a system's own states produce its actions, unaffected by whether those states were produced. The question conflates being caused with being compelled.

**Origin conditions.** That every physical event is fixed by prior physical events and law, or is not; that a person is a physical system to which the same description applies.

**What both answers require.** That the action has a determining source, and that locating it settles the question.

**What the question is about.** What would have happened under conditions that did not obtain.
**What the field's instruments reach.** What did happen.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Neuroscience of decision.* Libet and successors find preparatory activity preceding reported intention. The interpretation is contested within the field, and later work shows the signal is partly an artefact of averaging. Ends by explaining something adjacent.
- *Quantum indeterminacy.* Supplies randomness, which both sides agree is not agency.
- *Compatibilist analysis.* Redefines the ability in dispute. Its critics hold that the redefinition changes the subject; unsettled.
- *Frankfurt cases.* Argue that alternative possibilities are not required for responsibility. Widely discussed, not settled.
- *Experimental philosophy.* Reports what people judge, which is not the question.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 3. Timing experiments address an adjacent question and their interpretation was revised; indeterminism supplies randomness, which both sides reject as agency.

**What has been settled so far.** That indeterminism alone supplies no agency; that the readiness-potential interpretation has been substantially revised by later work on averaging artefacts, which is documented and is not uniformly accepted as settling the interpretation.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurement distinguishing an action with an alternative available from one without, on the same occasion.


## 2.11 Sorites

**The question.** One grain of sand is not a heap. Adding one grain to something that is not a heap does not make it a heap. Therefore no number of grains makes a heap. Which step fails?

**One answer given.** The second premise fails somewhere. There is a number at which one grain makes the difference, even if nobody can say which; vagueness is ignorance of a sharp boundary rather than absence of one.

**The other answer given.** The premise fails nowhere. There is no such number, and the predicate has no sharp extension. The argument shows classical logic does not apply to vague predicates.

**Origin conditions.** That a predicate has an extension; that classical logic applies to statements formed with it; that the induction step preserves truth.

**What both answers require.** That the predicate divides cases into those it applies to and those it does not, so that a boundary either exists or is absent.

**What the question is about.** A boundary in the predicate's application.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Uses of the predicate, which vary continuously between speakers and occasions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Epistemicism.* Posits a sharp boundary nobody can know. Consistent, and requires an unknowable fact; its critics hold that positing one to save the logic is the cost, not the solution.
- *Degrees of truth.* Assigns intermediate values. Higher-order vagueness recurs: the boundary between fully true and partly true is itself vague, and the move repeats without terminating.
- *Supervaluation.* Truth on all precisifications. Preserves classical tautologies and again faces higher-order vagueness, which its proponents acknowledge.
- *Contextualism.* The boundary shifts with context. Accounts for the variation and leaves the question of any single context's boundary.
- *Nihilism about the predicate.* Denies heaps exist. Both sides regard the cost as prohibitive.

**Closure.** Endings 5 and 8. Higher-order vagueness recurs against two families; epistemicism absorbs the phenomenon by positing an unknowable boundary.

**What has been settled so far.** That higher-order vagueness recurs against degree-theoretic and supervaluationist treatments, and that epistemicism posits an unknowable boundary. Each is stated by the accounts themselves; whether every possible treatment faces one or the other is not established.
**What would settle the rest.** Exhibition of the boundary, or a demonstration that the predicate's use requires none.

**What the field records.** That the argument contains no material of any kind: sand is incidental, and the same argument runs for any predicate admitting small increments.


## 2.12 Goodhart's law

**The question.** A statistical regularity used as a target for control ceases to hold. Why does it break exactly when it is relied on?

**One answer given.** It was never a mechanism, only a correlation obtained where nobody optimised against it. Applying pressure changes those conditions. The lesson is not to use correlations as targets.

**The other answer given.** It was a mechanism, and pressure alters the mechanism. Those measured respond to being measured, and the response is part of the system. No care in selecting the measure avoids this, because the response is to being measured at all.

**Origin conditions.** That a statistical relation estimated over observed instances holds over instances not yet occurring; that acting on the relation does not alter it.

**What both answers require.** That a relation estimated over past instances can be asserted over instances not yet occurring, and acted on there.

**What the question is about.** Instances occurring after the assertion.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Instances occurring before it.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Pick a better measure.* Each replacement is gamed in turn; the record across public administration, education and health is consistent.
- *Use multiple measures.* Distributes the response and does not remove it; composite indices are gamed at the weights.
- *Keep the measure secret.* Works while secret; the field records that secrecy is unstable in institutions and forecloses accountability, which was the purpose.
- *Measure outcomes rather than proxies.* Outcomes are also responded to, more slowly. Delays rather than removes.
- *Mechanism design.* Incentive-compatible schemes exist for restricted settings and require knowledge of preferences the setting does not supply; Gibbard–Satterthwaite bounds the general case.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. Each replacement measure is gamed in turn; Gibbard–Satterthwaite bounds the general mechanism-design case.

**What has been settled so far.** That the effect is general across domains; that composite measures relocate rather than remove it; that incentive-compatible mechanisms exist only under restrictions. The same result is proved inside contract theory at entry 2.129 and recurs at entry 2.119.
**What would settle the rest.** A measure that continues to track after being made a target, in a system whose members can detect it.


## 2.13 The Lucas critique

**The question.** Relations estimated from economic data change when policy acts on them. Can any estimated relation be used for policy?

**One answer given.** Not as estimated. The relations aggregate decisions made under the regime in force during estimation; a new policy changes the decisions. Policy evaluation requires parameters invariant to policy, and estimated relations are not those.

**The other answer given.** In practice, with care. Some relations are stable over relevant horizons, and the programmes built to supply invariant parameters have not supplied them. A critique no available method satisfies is a counsel of perfection.

**Origin conditions.** That an economy is described by parameters estimable from data; that some of those parameters are invariant to the policy regime, which is what policy evaluation requires.

**What both answers require.** That there exist, or could exist, parameters describing the system that are invariant to the policies acting on it.

**What the question is about.** Behaviour under policies not yet enacted.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Behaviour under policies enacted.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Deep parameters.* Microfoundations in preferences and technology, held to be policy-invariant. The invariance is assumed rather than tested, and estimates of the parameters shift across samples; the field states this.
- *Natural experiments.* Identify effects of enacted changes. Bear on regimes that occurred, which is the estimation problem again one step out.
- *Structural vector autoregression.* Requires identifying restrictions that are themselves not estimated.
- *Reduced-form forecasting.* Abandons policy evaluation and forecasts; performs adequately until a regime change, which is the critique restated.
- *Robustness to model uncertainty.* Designs policies performing acceptably across a set of models. Requires specifying the set.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 8. Natural experiments return the estimation problem one step out; the microfoundations programme assumes the invariance it was to supply.

**What has been settled so far.** That the critique is correct as stated is not disputed. That the microfoundations programme has not produced demonstrably invariant parameters is stated within the field.
**What would settle the rest.** Parameters demonstrated invariant across a policy change not used in their estimation.


## 2.14 The replication crisis

**The question.** A large fraction of published findings fail to reproduce when the study is repeated. Are the original findings wrong?

**One answer given.** Many are. Publication incentives, analytic flexibility and small samples enrich the literature in false positives, and direct replication is the corrective. Failure rates are measured and mechanisms identified.

**The other answer given.** Failure to replicate does not establish error. Effects are conditional on populations, settings and procedures that differ between original and replication, and a null replication is one study with its own error rate.

**Origin conditions.** That a finding obtained in one sample estimates a population effect; that an independent sample from the same population estimates the same effect.

**What both answers require.** That a finding obtained in one study is assertable at studies not yet run, and that the second bears on the first.

**What the question is about.** Studies not run.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Each study's own sample.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Large multi-site replication.* Reproducibility projects returned rates well below expectation. The interpretation of a failed replication remains the disputed point.
- *Preregistration.* Removes analytic flexibility, and preregistered studies report lower effect sizes, which is evidence about the mechanism. Does not settle the status of the earlier literature.
- *Raising thresholds.* Reduces false positives and raises false negatives by a computable amount. A known trade.
- *Meta-analysis.* Pools studies and inherits the publication bias it corrects for by models that are themselves assumptions.
- *Many-analysts studies.* Give the same data to independent teams and find divergent results, which quantifies analytic flexibility and does not adjudicate any single finding.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Replication returns more studies; the interpretation of a failed replication is where the field's instruments disagree on the same finding.

**What has been settled so far.** Replication rates measured in large coordinated projects across several fields, with substantial fractions failing to reproduce; the reduction in reported effect sizes under preregistration; the magnitude of analyst-driven variation in many-analysts studies.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion, agreed in advance, under which a replication outcome determines the status of the original.


## 2.15 Levinthal's paradox

**The question.** A polypeptide chain has more possible conformations than could be sampled in the age of the universe, and it reaches its native fold in seconds. How?

**One answer given.** Folding is not a search. Random or exhaustive sampling would require times exceeding the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude, and the arithmetic is not disputed.

**The other answer given.** Folding is a search on a structured landscape. Funnelled energy landscapes, local structure formation and nucleation reduce the accessible volume enormously; the paradox becomes an empirical question about landscape shape.

**Origin conditions.** That a protein's conformations form a space defined by its degrees of freedom; that the native state is the global minimum of a free energy defined over that space.

**What both answers require.** That there is a conformational space, enumerable in principle, over which folding is defined; the dispute concerns the measure on it.

**What the question is about.** The native state of an arbitrary sequence, in advance of folding it.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Solved structures and simulated trajectories.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Molecular dynamics from first principles.* Reaches microseconds to milliseconds for small proteins with special-purpose hardware. Ends by measuring more of what was reached; general sequences remain out of range.
- *Energy landscape theory.* Accounts for how fast folding is possible. Its authors state it does not supply the native state for a given sequence.
- *Homology modelling.* Predicts structures from solved relatives. Refinement within the observed domain by construction.
- *Deep learning from the structure database.* Predicts many structures at experimental accuracy — a major result, and one obtained by learning correlations across solved structures. Whether it constitutes a solution to the folding question or a very good interpolation over the database is disputed within the field, and its failures on orphan sequences and novel folds are the evidence in that dispute.
- *De novo design.* Designs sequences that fold as intended, which is the inverse problem and succeeds; the field distinguishes the two directions.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. Simulation reaches further timescales; landscape theory answers an adjacent question by its authors' statement; learned prediction is refinement within the observed domain, disputed as such.

**What has been settled so far.** That folding is not uniform sampling; that funnelled landscapes make the observed rates possible; that structure prediction from evolutionary information reaches experimental accuracy for a wide range of natural proteins, with measured degradation on sequences lacking deep alignments.
**What would settle the rest.** A general method returning native states from sequences with no reference to previously solved structures.


## 2.16 The origin of life

**The question.** Living systems come from living systems. How did the first one arise from chemistry that was not living?

**One answer given.** Genetic material first. Replication with variation makes everything else possible, so a self-copying molecule came first and metabolism was recruited around it. Ribozymes are demonstrated, and an RNA world couples information and catalysis in one molecule.

**The other answer given.** Metabolism first. A self-copying molecule of the required length is prohibitively improbable without a chemistry already running to build and concentrate its parts. Autocatalytic cycles at mineral interfaces supply that chemistry, and heredity arises within it.

**Origin conditions.** That living systems and non-living chemistry are distinguishable by criteria stated in advance; that the transition between them occurred as an event with prebiotic conditions before it.

**What both answers require.** That there is a threshold separating non-living chemistry from living, and that the question is which capacity crossed it first.

**What the question is about.** A transition, with prebiotic chemistry before it and inheritance after.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Present chemistry, and preserved traces from later than the event.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Prebiotic synthesis.* Amino acids, nucleobases and activated nucleotides have been made under plausible conditions, some in one pot. Ends short of a self-maintaining system; the field states the gap.
- *In vitro evolution of ribozymes.* Produces polymerases that copy substantial lengths, not yet themselves. Narrows without closing; open.
- *Autocatalytic set theory.* Proves that such sets form above a diversity threshold in model chemistries. The models are not the chemistry of the early Earth, which their authors state.
- *Hydrothermal and mineral surface chemistry.* Produces gradients and organics. Establishes plausibility of conditions, not the transition.
- *Search for traces.* Isotopic and morphological evidence is contested at the relevant age, and the earliest rocks are metamorphosed.
- *Panspermia.* Relocates the event without addressing it, as its proponents acknowledge.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. Prebiotic synthesis and ribozyme evolution narrow without closing; autocatalytic set theory establishes a result about model chemistries.

**What has been settled so far.** That the building blocks form abiotically under plausible conditions; that RNA can be both information and catalyst; that autocatalytic sets form above a threshold in model chemistries.
**What would settle the rest.** A demonstrated route from plausible prebiotic conditions to a self-maintaining, heritably varying system, in one continuous sequence.


## 2.17 Aging

**The question.** Do organisms age because damage accumulates, or because aging is produced by the organism's own regulation?

**One answer given.** Accumulation. Damage occurs continuously, repair is imperfect, and the failure to repair is what aging is. Selection weakens with age, so no programme is needed and none would be maintained.

**The other answer given.** Regulation. Aging shows species-typical timing, regulated pathways alter lifespan reproducibly across taxa, and single-gene interventions produce large coordinated changes.

**Origin conditions.** That aging is a change in an organism's state over time with a determinable cause; that damage and regulation are separable sources of that change.

**What both answers require.** That aging has a determining source, and that identifying it as damage or as regulation settles the question.

**What the question is about.** Which of two coupled processes drives the other.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The two together, in every organism measured.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Single-gene lifespan mutants.* Extend lifespan severalfold in nematodes, less in mice. Shows regulation modulates rate; both sides accept this and read it differently.
- *Caloric restriction.* Extends lifespan across taxa, with primate results mixed and diet-dependent. Ends contested.
- *Damage-clearance interventions.* Senolytics and repair-directed approaches produce measurable improvements in some tissues. Ends by explaining something adjacent: rate, not the driver.
- *Comparative biology.* Negligible-senescence species exist. Shows aging is not compulsory and does not part the two accounts.
- *Epigenetic clocks.* Predict age accurately from methylation. Whether the clock reads damage or a programme is exactly the dispute, restated in a new instrument.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Interventions establish modulation of rate rather than the driver; epigenetic clocks restate the dispute in a new instrument.

**What has been settled so far.** That lifespan is modulable by single genes and by diet across taxa; that several species show no detectable increase in mortality with age over the measured range, with the claim contested for some of them; that damage clearance improves function in specific tissues.
**What would settle the rest.** An organism accumulating damage at normal rates with no aging phenotype, or one with the regulatory pathways removed and no change in damage or lifespan.


## 2.18 The glass transition

**The question.** A cooling liquid becomes rigid over a narrow range with no change in its structure. Is there a phase transition there?

**One answer given.** Yes, an underlying one. Relaxation times grow by fourteen orders of magnitude over a modest temperature range, and theories positing an ideal transition below the accessible range fit that growth.

**The other answer given.** No. Nothing identifying a phase transition is observed: no diverging static correlation length, no thermodynamic discontinuity. The glass temperature depends on cooling rate, which a genuine transition would not.

**Origin conditions.** That phases are distinguished by order parameters and thermodynamic discontinuities; that a transition is identified by those signatures; that measurements are taken on equilibrated systems.

**What both answers require.** That rigidity is either a phase transition or it is not, and that the structural signatures determine which.

**What the question is about.** Behaviour at temperatures where equilibration exceeds any experiment's duration.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Equilibrated states above that range.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Cool more slowly.* Equilibration time grows faster than any experiment can be extended. Ends by construction.
- *Vapour-deposited ultrastable glasses.* Reach states equivalent to millennia of cooling. A genuine advance, and still above the proposed ideal temperature.
- *Search for a growing correlation length.* Dynamic heterogeneity is measured and grows modestly; no diverging static length is found.
- *Random first-order transition theory.* Predicts an ideal transition; the prediction is at a temperature not reachable.
- *Dynamical facilitation theory.* Reproduces the slowdown with no transition at any temperature. Both accounts fit the accessible data, which is the dispute.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 2 and 6. Slower cooling is bounded by construction; two theories fit the accessible range and diverge only outside it.

**What has been settled so far.** Dynamic heterogeneity, measured. The cooling-rate dependence of the glass temperature. The equivalence of ultrastable glasses to very slow cooling.
**What would settle the rest.** A diverging static correlation length in the accessible range, or a demonstration that the observed growth is reproduced with no transition at any temperature.


## 2.19 Efficient markets

**The question.** Do asset prices reflect all available information?

**One answer given.** Substantially yes. Prices respond to news within moments, active managers do not outperform indexes net of costs over sustained periods, and anomalies shrink after publication.

**The other answer given.** No. Persistent deviations — momentum, value, volatility clustering, bubbles with identifiable dynamics — exceed what risk adjustment explains, and the behavioural mechanisms producing them are demonstrated experimentally.

**Origin conditions.** That an asset's price reflects expected future payoffs discounted by a model of required return; that tests of price behaviour are conducted against such a model.

**What both answers require.** That prices either do or do not incorporate information, and that returns data can distinguish these.

**What the question is about.** Efficiency alone.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The conjunction of efficiency and a model of expected returns.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Test an anomaly.* Any rejection is a rejection of the joint hypothesis; the field names this and does not dispute it.
- *Add risk factors.* Each anomaly can be absorbed by a factor, and the factor zoo is documented in the field's own literature as the consequence.
- *Event studies.* Measure response speed to news, which both sides accept and read differently.
- *Experimental markets.* Produce bubbles reliably under controlled conditions. Ends by explaining something adjacent: laboratory conditions, disputed as to transfer.
- *Post-publication decay of anomalies.* Consistent with efficiency arriving, and with data mining; both readings fit.

**Closure.** Ending 5, stated by the field. The joint hypothesis problem makes every test a test of a conjunction, which is the field's own statement of the limitation.

**What has been settled so far.** The joint hypothesis problem, stated and accepted in the field. Post-publication decay, measured. The reliability of laboratory bubbles.
**What would settle the rest.** A test of efficiency independent of any asset pricing model. The field states that none is available.


## 2.20 The tragedy of the commons

**The question.** When a resource is open to all and each user's gain is private while the cost is shared, is depletion inevitable?

**One answer given.** Yes, absent assigned rights or an external authority. Each user's individually rational choice is to take more, the aggregate exceeds renewal, and depletion follows from the payoff structure.

**The other answer given.** No. Long-lived commons are documented, governed neither by private property nor external authority, using locally devised rules with monitoring and graduated sanctions. The outcome depends on institutional arrangements the payoff model omits.

**Origin conditions.** That each user acts to maximise its own return; that the resource has a renewal rate; that the outcome follows from the payoff structure facing individual users.

**What both answers require.** That the outcome is determined by the payoffs facing individual users, so that arrangements either alter the payoffs or are irrelevant.

**What the question is about.** Outcomes across all arrangements.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Cases, each with one arrangement.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Formal modelling.* Yields depletion under the stated payoffs, by construction.
- *Repeated-game folk theorems.* Establish that cooperation is sustainable in equilibrium, and equally that defection is; the multiplicity is the result.
- *Field studies.* Ostrom's cases establish that durable self-governance exists, with design principles extracted. Whether the principles are predictive or descriptive of survivors is disputed and is a selection question.
- *Laboratory commons games.* Show communication and sanctioning raise cooperation substantially. Transfer to field settings is contested.
- *Privatisation and state control.* Both work in some cases and fail in others, which is the field's own summary.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 5 and 8. Folk theorems give multiplicity rather than prediction; design principles extracted from survivors face a selection objection.

**What has been settled so far.** That durable commons exist without private property or external authority. That communication and sanctioning raise cooperation in the laboratory. That folk theorems give multiplicity rather than prediction.
**What would settle the rest.** A specification of the conditions under which local governance holds and fails, tested prospectively rather than extracted from survivors.


## 2.21 The arrow of time

**The question.** The laws of physics are symmetric in time. The world is not. Why?

**One answer given.** The past was low in entropy. The asymmetry is in the initial condition, not the laws, and everything asymmetric follows from it plus statistics.

**The other answer given.** Granting the initial condition is the problem. A state of that specificity is overwhelmingly improbable under any standard measure; naming it an initial condition relocates the puzzle.

**Origin conditions.** That the fundamental laws are time-symmetric; that thermodynamic behaviour follows from those laws together with a specification of the boundary conditions.

**What both answers require.** That the asymmetry lies either in the laws or in the boundary condition, and that assigning it settles the matter.

**What the question is about.** The initial condition of the universe.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Its consequences.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Statistical mechanics.* Derives the second law given the past hypothesis. Ends by requiring what is in question.
- *Inflation.* Accounts for homogeneity; whether it accounts for low entropy is disputed within cosmology, since the inflationary state is itself special.
- *CPT violation in weak decays.* Real and measured, and too small and too specific to produce the thermodynamic arrow, which the field states.
- *Cosmological accounts of the initial state.* Hartle–Hawking and successors propose a condition; none is established, and each contains a stipulation at the same place.
- *Two-time boundary conditions.* Consistent constructions exist and are not favoured by evidence.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Statistical mechanics derives the second law given what is in question; cosmological proposals relocate the stipulation.

**What has been settled so far.** That the derivation of the second law requires a low-entropy past. That measured CPT violation is insufficient in magnitude.
**What would settle the rest.** A dynamical account producing the low-entropy past from something not itself stipulated, or a demonstration that none is needed.


## 2.22 The frame problem

**The question.** When something changes, most other things do not. How can a system determine what is relevant to update without checking everything?

**One answer given.** By explicit machinery. Default assumptions, persistence axioms and circumscription formalise what stays the same unless stated otherwise, and working systems use them.

**The other answer given.** The technical answers address the narrow logical version and leave the general one. Relevance depends on the whole context including what was not anticipated, and no enumeration of defaults reaches this.

**Origin conditions.** That a system's knowledge is a set of represented propositions; that action changes some and leaves others; that what changes must be derivable from a specification made in advance.

**What both answers require.** That relevance can be settled by a procedure specifiable in advance of the situation it applies to.

**What the question is about.** Situations not anticipated.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Specifications made in advance.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Circumscription and default logics.* Solve the logical formulation; their authors distinguish this from the general problem.
- *Successor state axioms.* Solve the representational version efficiently, for a specified set of fluents.
- *Probabilistic state estimation.* Handles uncertainty over a specified state space; the space is specified.
- *Learned world models.* Acquire relevance from data and generalise within the distribution sampled. Out-of-distribution behaviour is the open case, which the field measures and does not solve.
- *Embodied approaches.* Argue relevance is fixed by the task and the body rather than represented. Reframes rather than settles, as its proponents accept.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 3. Formalisms solve the logical and representational versions, distinguished by their authors from the general one.

**What has been settled so far.** The logical and representational versions, with working formalisms. The measured gap between within-distribution and out-of-distribution generalisation.
**What would settle the rest.** A system determining relevance in situations its designers did not anticipate, without enumeration.


## 2.23 Simpson's paradox

**The question.** An association between two variables can reverse when the data are divided into subgroups. Which direction is correct?

**One answer given.** The subgroup reading. The aggregate mixes populations differing in ways relevant to the outcome; conditioning on the relevant variable gives the effect.

**The other answer given.** Neither, in general. Which reading answers a question depends on the causal structure, and identical numbers support opposite conclusions under different structures.

**Origin conditions.** That an association between variables is estimated from data; that a correct estimate requires conditioning on the variables relevant to the outcome.

**What both answers require.** That there is a correct reading to be identified.

**What the question is about.** The causal structure.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The data, which are identical under both structures.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Condition on everything available.* Can introduce collider bias, which is proved; more conditioning is not monotonically better.
- *Graphical criteria.* The back-door criterion determines which sets to condition on, given the graph. The graph is an input.
- *Randomisation.* Settles it where feasible, by removing the structure in question. Not available for many questions, which is the residual case.
- *Sensitivity analysis.* Bounds the conclusion over a range of assumed structures. Returns a range, which is what it is for.
- *Causal discovery algorithms.* Recover structures up to an equivalence class from data. The equivalence class is the limit, and it is proved.

**Closure.** Ending 5 throughout. The back-door criterion, collider bias, and the equivalence-class limit of causal discovery are three proved limitations.

**What has been settled so far.** The back-door criterion, collider bias, and the equivalence-class limit of discovery from observational data, all proved.
**What would settle the rest.** Determination of the causal structure by means independent of the data exhibiting the reversal.


## 2.24 Externalities

**The question.** Production imposes costs on people outside the transaction. Can those costs be brought into the price?

**One answer given.** Yes, by pricing them. Assign the right or levy the charge and the cost enters the calculation; magnitudes are estimable and the mechanisms work where tried.

**The other answer given.** Not in general. Which effects count as external is set by where the transaction's boundary is drawn, and the boundary is chosen rather than found. Internalising some prices those and leaves the rest.

**Origin conditions.** That a transaction has parties, and effects falling on parties to it and on others; that the boundary between the two is determinable.

**What both answers require.** That there is a boundary distinguishing effects internal to a transaction from effects external to it.

**What the question is about.** All effects of a production.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Effects inside a drawn boundary.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Pigouvian taxation.* Prices the identified effect, and requires the magnitude, which is estimated under assumptions about the boundary.
- *Coasean bargaining.* Establishes that allocation is efficient under zero transaction costs regardless of assignment. Transaction costs are not zero, which Coase states.
- *Tradable permits.* Work well for a bounded pollutant with measurable emissions. The boundedness is the condition.
- *Full-cost accounting.* Extends the boundary; each extension reveals further effects at the new edge.
- *Contingent valuation.* Prices non-market effects by survey, and the estimates are sensitive to elicitation format by amounts the field measures.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Coase establishes a result under a condition that does not obtain; each boundary extension reveals effects at the new edge.

**What has been settled so far.** The Coase theorem and its transaction-cost condition. The performance of permit markets for bounded pollutants. The elicitation-sensitivity of survey valuation.
**What would settle the rest.** A principled determination of the boundary that does not appeal to what is convenient to measure.


## 2.25 Species boundaries

**The question.** Where does one species end and another begin?

**One answer given.** At reproductive isolation. Populations not exchanging genes under natural conditions are distinct species, and the criterion is testable.

**The other answer given.** No single criterion works. Ring species, asexual lineages, hybridising taxa and pervasive introgression defeat it, and species concepts number in the dozens, chosen for the question at hand.

**Origin conditions.** That organisms sort into species; that a criterion identifies the sorting; that the criterion applies across the relevant lineages.

**What both answers require.** That populations divide into kinds, and that a criterion identifies the division.

**What the question is about.** A boundary holding across all lineages, sexual and asexual, extant and extinct.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Populations varying continuously in every relevant respect.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Biological species concept.* Works for sexual, sympatric, extant populations. Undefined for asexual and fossil lineages, which its author acknowledged.
- *Phylogenetic concepts.* Apply universally and multiply species counts by large factors depending on the resolution chosen.
- *Genomic thresholds.* Set a percentage divergence. The threshold is stipulated and differs by taxon.
- *Integrative taxonomy.* Combines criteria and returns agreement where they agree, which is where the question was not hard.
- *Species as a pragmatic unit.* Abandons the boundary; conservation law and biodiversity accounting require one, which the field notes.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Concept choice changes species counts by measured factors, which is the field's instruments disagreeing on the same populations.

**What has been settled so far.** That introgression is documented between taxa standardly treated as distinct species, including in humans and hominins; that concept choice changes species counts by large and measured factors.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion applying without exception across sexual and asexual, extant and extinct lineages.


## 2.26 The Collatz conjecture

**The question.** Take any positive integer. Halve it if even; triple it and add one if odd. Repeat. Does every starting number reach 1?

**One answer given.** Yes. Every number tested reaches 1, past 10²⁰, and heuristics from the average multiplicative effect predict decrease over long runs.

**The other answer given.** No proof exists and the heuristics are not evidence of the required kind. No quantity is known to decrease monotonically along trajectories, so no induction runs.

**Origin conditions.** That the map is defined on all positive integers; that a question about the behaviour of all trajectories is well posed; that the iterate composes into a single reaching.

**What both answers require.** That the iterate composes into a single reaching, so a question can be posed about all starting values at once.

**What the question is about.** All positive integers.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Finitely many, and the structure of the map.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Computation.* Verified past 10²⁰. Ends by measuring more of what was reached.
- *Density results.* Terras and successors, and Tao's result that almost all orbits attain almost bounded values. A genuine advance and explicitly not all.
- *Cycle exclusion.* Non-trivial cycles are excluded below very large lengths. Bounds, not exclusion.
- *Generalised Collatz maps.* Conway proved a class of them undecidable, which suggests the difficulty is structural.
- *Reformulation as a stopping-time problem.* Restates it; the same obstacle appears at the new form.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. Computation returns more of the same domain; Conway's undecidability for generalised maps is a proved structural limitation.

**What has been settled so far.** Verification to 10²⁰; almost-all results in the density sense; cycle bounds; undecidability of a generalised class.
**What would settle the rest.** A proof, or a counterexample: an unknown cycle or a divergent trajectory.


## 2.27 The neural code

**The question.** Neurons signal with discrete spikes. Is the information carried by how many spikes occur, or by when they occur?

**One answer given.** Rate. Rates correlate reliably with stimuli and behaviour across systems, decoding from rates succeeds, and the system is noisy enough that fine timing would not survive transmission.

**The other answer given.** Timing. Millisecond-precision timing carries information rates discard, is reproducible across trials in several systems, and is read by coincidence-sensitive mechanisms downstream.

**Origin conditions.** That neurons carry information about stimuli in their spiking; that the code is identified by which feature of the spiking carries the information; that a window over which to define a rate is available.

**What both answers require.** That there is a code, and that identifying its variable settles the question.

**What the question is about.** What the downstream system uses.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Spike trains, analysed over a window the analyst chooses.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Information-theoretic decoding.* Quantifies information in rate and in timing, both non-zero. Returns amounts, not the used variable.
- *Optogenetic timing manipulation.* Alters timing with rate controlled, with behavioural effects in specific systems. The strongest route, and system-specific so far.
- *Downstream biophysics.* Coincidence detection is demonstrated in auditory brainstem. Establishes timing is usable there; generalisation is the open part.
- *Population decoding.* Shifts the question to population states, where the same rate-versus-timing choice recurs at the population level.
- *Metabolic argument.* Precise timing is expensive, so it should be used where it pays. A constraint, not a determination.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Decoding returns amounts rather than the used variable; the rate window is set by the analyst, which is where the instruments differ.

**What has been settled so far.** Timing precision measured in several systems; coincidence detection demonstrated; both variables carry information in the technical sense.
**What would settle the rest.** Manipulation of timing with rate held constant, or the converse, with measured behavioural consequence, generalised beyond single systems.


## 2.28 Missing heritability

**The question.** Twin studies attribute a large share of variance in a trait to genetics. Genome studies find variants accounting for much less. Where is the rest?

**One answer given.** In variants not yet found. Effects are small and numerous, rare variants are undersampled, and larger studies recover more of the gap steadily.

**The other answer given.** In the estimate. Twin-based heritability assumes equal environments and no gene-environment interaction; where these fail the estimate is inflated and there is less to find.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a single quantity, the genetic contribution to variance, that both twin designs and molecular designs estimate; that the designs' assumptions hold in the populations sampled.

**What both answers require.** That a single quantity — the genetic share of variance — exists and is estimated by both methods.

**What the question is about.** A variance component defined for a population and environment range.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Two designs with different assumptions, each returning its own estimate.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Larger association studies.* Recover increasing variance; the gap narrows and has not closed.
- *Whole-genome sequencing for rare variants.* Recovers some; less than projected for most traits.
- *SNP-based heritability.* Returns values between the two, which is consistent with both answers.
- *Within-family designs.* Reduce estimates substantially, showing part of the earlier figure was population structure rather than direct genetic effect. This supports the second answer for a measured portion.
- *Interaction modelling.* Underpowered at available sample sizes, which the field states.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Both designs return estimates and they do not agree, which is the disagreement the entry is about.

**What has been settled so far.** That within-family estimates are lower by measured amounts; that SNP-based estimates fall between; that rare variants supply less than projected.
**What would settle the rest.** Convergence of the two estimates on one design, or identification of the assumption whose failure accounts for the gap.


## 2.29 Is and ought

**The question.** Can a statement about what ought to be done follow from statements about what is the case?

**One answer given.** No. A conclusion containing an evaluative term cannot be validly derived from premises containing none, since validity preserves only what the premises contain. The point is logical rather than substantive, holds whatever the facts turn out to be, and is what makes moral criticism of any factual arrangement possible at all: if ought followed from is, whatever is the case would be what ought to be.

**The other answer given.** The gap is bridged by the meanings of terms. Institutional and functional facts carry evaluative content, and inferences from them to obligations are ordinary. The prohibition assumes a separation of vocabularies language does not observe.

**Origin conditions.** That statements sort into descriptive and evaluative; that validity is a relation between premises and conclusion preserving content in the premises.

**What both answers require.** That descriptive and evaluative statements are two kinds, and that the question is whether one entails the other.

**What the question is about.** All possible derivations.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Exhibited derivations, one at a time.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Exhibit a derivation.* Searle's promising argument. Its critics locate an evaluative premise in the institutional description; unsettled.
- *Naturalise the evaluative terms.* Defines goodness in natural terms; Moore's open question argument is pressed against each definition, and the dispute is old and live.
- *Deny the evaluative side has truth values.* Expressivism. Faces the embedding problem, which its own proponents work on and have not closed.
- *Thick concepts.* Terms carrying description and evaluation together. Both sides claim these; whether they can be factored is the dispute restated.
- *Formalise the prohibition.* Proofs exist for restricted logical languages, and their scope is exactly the restriction.

**Closure.** Endings 5 and 8. The prohibition is proved for restricted languages; expressivism absorbs the phenomenon and inherits the embedding problem.

**What has been settled so far.** The prohibition is proved for restricted formal languages. The embedding problem is a live open problem in expressivism, by its own account.
**What would settle the rest.** A valid derivation whose premises both sides agree are purely descriptive.


## 2.30 Placebo effects

**The question.** Patients given an inert substance sometimes improve. Is the improvement produced by the administration?

**One answer given.** Yes, and measurably. Open-label trials show improvement without deception, ritual dose-response is documented, and imaging shows endogenous opioid release in placebo analgesia.

**The other answer given.** Much of what is called a placebo effect is not an effect. Regression to the mean, natural history and reporting bias account for a large share, and trials with no-treatment arms find the difference small outside pain and subjective outcomes.

**Origin conditions.** That a trial's arms differ only in the substance administered; that the difference between arms estimates the substance's effect.

**What both answers require.** That the inert substance's administration either does or does not produce the improvement, and that a comparison isolates which.

**What the question is about.** What the administration contributes.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Arms of a trial, each including a context that cannot be removed while the trial runs.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Three-arm trials with no-treatment control.* Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche: small effects except for pain and subjective outcomes. The strongest route, and its own limitation is that no-treatment is itself a context.
- *Open-label placebo.* Improvement without deception, replicated. Narrows the mechanism; the control problem persists.
- *Neuroimaging and antagonist blockade.* Naloxone blocks placebo analgesia, which establishes a mechanism for that outcome. Specific to that outcome.
- *Conditioning paradigms.* Produce measurable immune and endocrine responses in animals, where reporting bias is absent. The strongest evidence for a real effect, in a different setting.
- *Nocebo studies.* Symmetric findings, same methodological position.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 3. Three-arm trials narrow the estimate; the animal conditioning result establishes the effect in a different setting.

**What has been settled so far.** Naloxone blockade of placebo analgesia. Conditioned immunosuppression in animals. The small size of placebo-versus-nothing differences for objective outcomes.
**What would settle the rest.** A design separating administration from context, or a demonstration that no such separation is definable.


## 2.31 Turbulence and the Navier–Stokes equations

**The question.** Do smooth solutions of the equations of fluid motion in three dimensions remain smooth for all time, or can they develop a singularity?

**One answer given.** They remain smooth. No singularity has been observed in any physical fluid or reliable simulation, and the equations describe fluids accurately across the range where they are tested.

**The other answer given.** Not established, and blow-up is plausible. The scaling of the nonlinear term against dissipation is supercritical in three dimensions, which is exactly the regime where the available methods fail; two dimensions is settled precisely because it is not supercritical.

**Origin conditions.** That the Navier-Stokes equations describe fluid motion at all scales of the flow; that a solution means a function satisfying them for all time from given initial data.

**What both answers require.** That the equations continue to describe the fluid at every scale, so that a question about all time and all initial data is well posed.

**What the question is about.** All initial data, for all time.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Finite simulations at finite resolution, and specific classes of initial data.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Energy methods.* Give global weak solutions, by Leray. Uniqueness and smoothness of weak solutions are open, which is the same question.
- *Partial regularity.* Caffarelli–Kohn–Nirenberg bound the singular set to parabolic Hausdorff dimension zero. A strong constraint that does not exclude.
- *Numerical search for blow-up.* Candidate scenarios have been proposed and repeatedly resolved as resolution improved; recent computer-assisted work has established blow-up for related equations and boundary cases, not for the free three-dimensional problem.
- *Modified and averaged equations.* Tao constructed an averaged Navier–Stokes with blow-up, showing that any proof of regularity must use structure the averaged version discards.
- *Two-dimensional case.* Settled, and its methods do not carry to three.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 4 and 5. Numerical search returns finite intervals; Tao's averaged construction proves a class of arguments cannot work.

**What has been settled so far.** Global weak existence; partial regularity; the two-dimensional case; and Tao's blow-up for an averaged system, which proves that any regularity argument must use structure the averaging discards, and so rules out a class of approaches.
**What would settle the rest.** A proof of global regularity, or an explicit blow-up solution.


## 2.32 Black hole information

**The question.** Matter falls into a black hole and the hole evaporates into radiation. Is the information about what fell in preserved?

**One answer given.** Yes. Quantum evolution is unitary, and abandoning unitarity would break the framework everywhere it is tested. Recent calculations of the entropy curve reproduce the expected turnover.

**The other answer given.** The semiclassical calculation says no. Hawking's derivation gives thermal radiation independent of what formed the hole, and the derivation uses only physics that is well established at the horizon, where curvature is low.

**Origin conditions.** That quantum evolution is unitary; that semiclassical gravity applies where curvature is low, including at the horizon of a large black hole; that both hold together in one consistent theory.

**What both answers require.** That the question has one answer determinable from a consistent theory covering the horizon and the interior together.

**What the question is about.** The interior, and the correlations between interior and exterior.
**What the field's instruments reach.** No black hole evaporation has been observed. All routes are theoretical.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Semiclassical calculation.* Gives information loss. Its own domain of validity at late times is disputed.
- *Holographic duality.* In settings where the duality is established, evolution is unitary in the dual description. Whether the setting applies to astrophysical holes is the open part.
- *Island formulas and the entropy curve.* Reproduce the expected turnover using gravitational path integral techniques. What they imply about the interior is actively disputed within the field.
- *Firewalls.* AMPS showed that unitarity, effective field theory at the horizon and no drama cannot all hold. A proved incompatibility, and no consensus on which to give up.
- *Remnants and baby universes.* Consistent constructions with acknowledged costs.
- *Observation.* Evaporation timescales for astrophysical holes exceed the age of the universe by enormous factors.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 4 and 5. Holography answers in settings whose applicability is open; AMPS proves three accepted premises incompatible.

**What has been settled so far.** Hawking radiation as a theoretical result; the AMPS incompatibility; the entropy curve in the settings where the island calculation applies.
**What would settle the rest.** Observation of an evaporating hole, which is unavailable; or a theory covering horizon and interior together whose predictions are checkable elsewhere.


## 2.144 Structure and agency

**The question.** Social outcomes are produced by people acting and by the structures they act within. Which explains which?

**One answer given.** Structure. Regularities in outcomes persist across changes of personnel, patterned by class, institution and position; individuals act within constraints they did not choose and mostly reproduce them, and explanation at the level of individual choice misses what is doing the work.

**The other answer given.** Agency. Structures have no existence apart from what people do; they are reproduced only by being enacted, and treating them as causes reifies a pattern into a thing. Explanation must run through the actions that constitute the regularity.

**Origin conditions.** That social outcomes have causes; that the causes are located either in properties of persons or in properties of arrangements; that the two levels are distinguishable in measurement.

**What both answers require.** That structure and action are two things with a determination relation between them.

**What the question is about.** Which level does the explanatory work.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Outcomes in which both levels are present, and cannot be varied independently.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Structuration.* Giddens's duality of structure: structure is both medium and outcome of action. Widely adopted and criticised as redescribing the relation rather than analysing it, since it makes the two inseparable by definition.
- *Critical realism.* Archer's morphogenetic approach separates them analytically over time, structure preceding action and being elaborated by it. Requires a temporal separation its critics hold is stipulated rather than found.
- *Methodological individualism.* Derives regularities from individual action. Faces the aggregation problem — the same regularity follows from many individual-level specifications — and the emergence objection.
- *Multilevel statistical modelling.* Partitions variance between individual and contextual levels. Returns a partition conditional on the levels specified, which is entry 2.6's limitation at a social scale.
- *Natural experiments in institutional change.* Where arrangements change and personnel do not, or the reverse, comparison is available. Rare, and confounded by selection into the change.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 5 and 8. Structuration absorbs the phenomenon by making the two inseparable, which its critics hold removes the question; multilevel modelling returns a partition conditional on supplied levels, which is a proved limitation of the design.

**What has been settled so far.** The aggregation problem for individualist derivations. The variance-partition limitation. That structuration and morphogenetic accounts each redescribe the relation on terms their critics identify precisely.
**What would settle the rest.** A design varying arrangement and personnel independently, with outcomes measured.

## 2.33 Bell correlations

**The question.** Two particles prepared together are measured far apart. The results are correlated more strongly than any account giving each particle its own properties allows. What produces the correlation?

**One answer given.** Nothing local does, and the theorem settles it. Bell derived an inequality satisfied by any theory in which each particle carries its own state and no influence travels faster than light. Experiments violate it, and loophole-free experiments since 2015 close detection and locality simultaneously.

**The other answer given.** The theorem constrains a class of theories, and which assumption to give up is not settled. Nonlocality, denial of measurement independence, retrocausality, and superdeterminism each preserve the correlations at a different cost, and the field has no agreed criterion for choosing among them.

**Origin conditions.** Each system carries its own state; measurement outcomes are fixed by that state and the local setting; settings are chosen independently of the state; no influence propagates faster than light.

**What both answers require.** That the correlation is produced by something specifiable at one side or transmitted between the sides.

**What the question is about.** What produces the correlation.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Coincidence counts at two stations.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Close the loopholes.* Detection, locality, and freedom-of-choice loopholes closed, the last using cosmic photons and human random choices. The correlation stands; the interpretation does not follow.
- *Local hidden variables.* Excluded by the theorem plus experiment. This route is closed, which is unusual in this collection.
- *Nonlocal hidden variables.* De Broglie–Bohm reproduces everything, explicitly nonlocal. Empirical equivalence.
- *Superdeterminism.* Preserves locality by denying setting independence. Not excluded by any experiment, and its critics hold it undermines the practice of experiment; unsettled.
- *Retrocausal accounts.* Consistent constructions exist; no distinguishing prediction so far.
- *Information-theoretic reconstructions.* Derive quantum correlations from principles such as information causality, and reach the observed bound. Explains the magnitude without settling what produces it.

**Closure.** Endings 4 and 5, with one route closed outright. Bell's theorem plus loophole-free experiment excludes local hidden variables — a closure rare in this collection — while the surviving accounts are empirically equivalent to each other.

**What has been settled so far.** Local hidden variable theories are excluded, given the theorem's assumptions, which the loophole-free experiments close except for measurement independence. The observed correlation bound matches the quantum prediction and is reproduced from information-theoretic principles.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion, agreed in the field, for choosing which of the theorem's assumptions to give up; or an experiment distinguishing the surviving accounts.

## 2.34 Wave and particle

**The question.** The same entity shows interference in one experiment and localized detection in another. Which is it?

**One answer given.** Neither, and the question is malformed. The formalism is complete; wave and particle are classical pictures, and complementarity states that the two descriptions apply to mutually exclusive experimental arrangements.

**The other answer given.** Complementarity names the situation rather than explaining it. Delayed-choice and quantum-eraser experiments show the arrangement can be settled after the entity has passed, and accounts differ on what this establishes.

**Origin conditions.** That an entity has a determinate nature specifiable independently of the measurement made on it; that the two classical pictures exhaust the available descriptions.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about what the entity is, or a demonstration that there is not.

**What the question is about.** What the entity is between preparation and detection.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Preparation settings and detection events.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Which-path measurement.* Obtaining path information destroys interference, quantitatively, by measured duality relations. Establishes the trade exactly; does not settle the nature.
- *Delayed choice.* Wheeler's experiment performed; the outcome matches quantum prediction under every arrangement. The interpretation is disputed.
- *Quantum eraser.* Interference recoverable by correlation with the marker. Widely misreported as retrocausal; the field states the correlations require the joint measurement.
- *Weak measurement of trajectories.* Reconstructs average trajectories in a two-slit apparatus. What an average trajectory establishes about individual entities is disputed.
- *Interpretation.* Each interpretation gives its own answer, and they are empirically equivalent.

**Closure.** Endings 2 and 3. The duality relation quantifies an adjacent trade exactly; interpretations are empirically equivalent by construction.

**What has been settled so far.** The quantitative duality relation between path information and fringe visibility. Delayed-choice and eraser outcomes, all matching the formalism.
**What would settle the rest.** An experiment distinguishing interpretations, which by their construction none does.

## 2.35 Incompleteness and Hilbert's programme

**The question.** Can a formal system strong enough for arithmetic prove every arithmetical truth, and prove its own consistency?

**One answer given.** No, proved. Gödel's first theorem gives a true sentence unprovable in the system; the second shows consistency is unprovable within it. The programme as stated cannot be carried out.

**The other answer given.** The programme survives in modified form. Gentzen proved the consistency of arithmetic by transfinite induction to ε₀, and reverse mathematics has established exactly which axioms are needed for which theorems. What was lost is the finitary requirement, not the enterprise.

**Origin conditions.** That mathematical truth is captured by derivability in a formal system; that consistency can be established by means weaker than the system itself.

**What both answers require.** That there is a determinate set of arithmetical truths, and a system either captures it or does not.

**What the question is about.** All arithmetical truths.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Derivations in a specified system.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Strengthen the system.* Each strengthening has its own unprovable sentence. Ends by construction at every stage.
- *Gentzen's consistency proof.* Succeeds, using transfinite induction to ε₀, which is not available within arithmetic. Ends by requiring what it was meant to secure at a lower level.
- *Reverse mathematics.* Maps exactly which axioms suffice for which theorems, with striking regularity across five subsystems. A substantial positive result addressing a different question.
- *Ordinal analysis.* Extends Gentzen's route upward; each system's proof-theoretic ordinal is established, and each needs a stronger metatheory.
- *Set-theoretic realism.* Holds truth outruns provability, and the unprovable sentences are true. Consistent, and supplies no method.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 5. Each strengthening carries its own unprovable sentence; Gentzen and reverse mathematics answer adjacent questions precisely; the theorems are the proved limitation.

**What has been settled so far.** Both theorems. Gentzen's proof. The reverse-mathematics classification. Proof-theoretic ordinals for a range of systems.
**What would settle the rest.** Nothing within the programme as originally stated: it is closed by proof. The residual question, whether the unprovable sentences have determinate truth values, remains.

## 2.36 The continuum hypothesis

**The question.** Is there a set larger than the integers and smaller than the real numbers?

**One answer given.** The question has no answer. Gödel showed the hypothesis consistent with the standard axioms and Cohen showed its negation consistent. It is independent, and independence is the answer.

**The other answer given.** Independence from one axiom set is not the end. The axioms are incomplete, and the question is which further axioms are true. Programmes on inner models and on forcing axioms aim at settling it, and some of them favour a definite value.

**Origin conditions.** That the sets form a determinate universe about which questions have answers; that the standard axioms describe that universe.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about the universe of sets, or a demonstration that there is not.

**What the question is about.** Whether a further axiom is true of the sets, beyond being consistent with them.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Consequences of chosen axiom systems.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Prove it from the axioms.* Excluded by Gödel and Cohen. Closed by proof.
- *Large cardinal axioms.* Settle many questions; provably do not settle this one, since forcing preserves them.
- *Forcing axioms.* Martin's Maximum and relatives imply a definite value for the continuum. Ends where the choice of forcing axiom is itself unsettled.
- *Inner model programme.* Aims at a canonical model for large cardinals. Ongoing, technically formidable, and its architects describe it as incomplete.
- *Woodin's programmes.* Have proposed and revised more than one criterion, including reversals of direction. Open, and the field does not regard the matter as decided.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Independence is proved twice over; large-cardinal axioms provably do not settle it, since forcing preserves them.

**What has been settled so far.** Independence, proved twice over. The invariance of large-cardinal axioms under forcing. Several coherent extensions each giving a definite value.
**What would settle the rest.** An agreed criterion for accepting a new axiom as true rather than as merely consistent.

## 2.37 Gettier

**The question.** Knowledge was defined as justified true belief. Cases exist in which a belief is justified, true, and by accident. What is missing?

**One answer given.** A fourth condition. Candidate additions — no false lemmas, defeasibility, causal connection, reliability, safety, sensitivity — each handle the original cases, and the project is to find the right one.

**The other answer given.** No fourth condition works. Each proposal has generated counterexamples within a few years, for fifty years, and some argue the analysis project itself is misconceived and knowledge is unanalysable.

**Origin conditions.** That knowledge decomposes into individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions; that intuitions about cases adjudicate proposals.

**What both answers require.** That cases sort into knowledge and non-knowledge, and that a correct analysis reproduces the sorting.

**What the question is about.** The concept's extension over all possible cases.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Cases constructed and judged, one at a time.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Add a condition.* Each has been met by a counterexample. The pattern is documented and consistent over decades.
- *Knowledge first.* Williamson takes knowledge as primitive and analyses the others from it. Reverses the direction and is contested; it does not supply the analysis it declines to give.
- *Contextualism.* Standards vary with context. Accounts for variation; the original cases persist within a fixed context.
- *Experimental philosophy.* Reports variation in case judgments across groups. Bears on the adjudication method rather than the analysis.
- *Denying the phenomenon.* Few take it; the cases are widely found compelling.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 8. Each added condition meets a counterexample; knowledge-first reverses the direction rather than supplying the analysis.

**What has been settled so far.** That each major proposed analysis has met a published counterexample, which is a documented record. Whether some current analysis stands uncountered is disputed by its proponents.
**What would settle the rest.** An analysis surviving counterexample, or a demonstration that the concept admits none.

## 2.38 The Münchhausen trilemma

**The question.** Any claim can be asked for its grounds. The grounds can be asked in turn. Where does this end?

**One answer given.** It ends at basic beliefs. Some beliefs are self-justifying or justified non-inferentially, and the regress stops there. Foundationalism, in some form, is what everyone practises.

**The other answer given.** It does not end. Every proposed foundation either takes its warrant from something further, which continues the regress, or from itself, which is circular, or from stipulation, which is arbitrary. Coherentists accept a form of circularity; infinitists accept a form of regress.

**Origin conditions.** That justification is a relation between claims; that a claim's warrant derives from its relation to other claims or from itself.

**What both answers require.** That justification terminates, circles, or continues, and that these three exhaust the options.

**What the question is about.** The structure of justification in general.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Particular justifications, each already inside the structure.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Foundationalism.* Identifies basic beliefs. Each candidate class — sense data, self-evident propositions, cogito-like claims — has been argued to require justification of its own.
- *Coherentism.* Accepts mutual support without foundations. Faces the isolation objection: a coherent set may be unconnected to anything outside it.
- *Infinitism.* Accepts infinite chains. Faces finite-mind objections; its proponents argue these confuse having a chain with traversing one.
- *Contextualism.* Grounds stop where the context does not question them. Accounts for practice; the trilemma recurs when the context is questioned.
- *Rejecting the demand.* Holds the regress question malformed. Its critics reply that the demand arises from ordinary practice.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 8. Each horn is developed and carries a standing objection; contextualism absorbs the phenomenon by relativising the demand.

**What has been settled so far.** That the three horns exhaust the structural options is widely accepted and has been disputed by infinitists and by contextualists, who accept a horn rather than adding a fourth. Each horn has a developed defence and a standing objection, both documented.
**What would settle the rest.** A ground that is neither derived, nor self-supporting, nor stipulated.

**Note.** The statement contains no subject matter. It concerns the relation of grounding and nothing else.

## 2.39 The liar

**The question.** "This sentence is false." If it is true it is false, and if it is false it is true. What is wrong?

**One answer given.** The sentence is not well formed for truth evaluation. Tarski showed a consistent truth predicate for a language cannot be defined within that language, and the hierarchy of metalanguages removes the sentence from the domain.

**The other answer given.** Natural language has one level and speakers use "true" of their own sentences constantly. Hierarchies are stipulated rather than found, and revenge sentences reconstruct the paradox at every level of every proposed solution.

**Origin conditions.** That sentences bear truth values; that a truth predicate applies to sentences of the language it occurs in.

**What both answers require.** That the sentence either has a truth value or is shown to fall outside the domain.

**What the question is about.** The truth predicate as used.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Formal languages, each with a stipulated structure.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Tarski hierarchy.* Proved and consistent, at the cost of denying that a language contains its own truth predicate.
- *Kripke's fixed points.* Assign the liar no truth value, consistently. A revenge sentence — "this sentence is not true" — reconstructs the problem, which Kripke states.
- *Paraconsistent treatments.* Accept the sentence as both true and false and restrict inference to prevent explosion. Consistent as a system; its critics hold the cost is the inference rules everyone uses.
- *Contextual treatments.* The truth predicate shifts with context of evaluation. Accounts for the reasoning and faces its own revenge cases.
- *Denying truth-bearing.* Various forms; each has been met by a strengthened liar.

**Closure.** Endings 5 and 8. Tarski's undefinability is proved; every family of solution has been met by a revenge sentence, which its own authors record.

**What has been settled so far.** Tarski's undefinability theorem. Kripke's fixed-point construction. The general phenomenon of revenge, documented against every family of solution.
**What would settle the rest.** A treatment of the truth predicate with no revenge sentence constructible in it.

## 2.40 Yablo's paradox

**The question.** An infinite list of sentences, each saying only that all the ones after it are untrue. No sentence refers to itself. The paradox arises anyway. What produces it?

**One answer given.** Self-reference is not required. The construction is explicitly free of it, so whatever produces liar-type paradox is more general than circularity — the quantification over an unbounded tail is enough.

**The other answer given.** Circularity is present in a hidden form. Priest argues the construction requires a fixed point and is therefore circular at the level of the property defined, and the dispute over whether it is genuinely non-circular has continued since 1993.

**Origin conditions.** That paradox arises from a sentence's reference structure; that self-reference is identifiable in a construction.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about whether the construction is circular.

**What the question is about.** What produces paradox in general.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Particular constructions, analysed one at a time.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Inspect for self-reference.* No sentence refers to itself, by construction, which is agreed.
- *Priest's fixed-point analysis.* Locates circularity in the defining property. Contested and not resolved.
- *Graph-theoretic analysis.* Cook and others characterise paradoxicality by properties of the reference graph, giving a criterion that classifies both liar and Yablo. Advances the analysis; whether the graph property is circularity is the same dispute in new terms.
- *Arithmetical versions.* Constructed, showing the phenomenon in a formal setting.

**Closure.** Endings 5 and 6. The graph-theoretic criteria are a formal advance; whether the construction is circular is where the field's analyses disagree on one object.

**What has been settled so far.** The construction, its arithmetisation, and the graph-theoretic criteria. The circularity question is documented as open.
**What would settle the rest.** An agreed criterion for when a construction is circular.

## 2.41 Newcomb's problem

**The question.** A reliable predictor has already filled a box based on a prediction of your choice. Take one box or two?

**One answer given.** Take one. Those who take one box receive far more, reliably. A decision theory whose recommendation is refuted by the outcome distribution is not a good decision theory.

**The other answer given.** Take two. The contents are already fixed and your choice does not change them. Taking both dominates: whatever is in the opaque box, you get more by taking both.

**Origin conditions.** That the predictor is reliable; that the contents are fixed before the choice; that both principles — maximise expected utility given evidence, and dominance — are correct.

**What both answers require.** That the two principles are both valid and that one applies here.

**What the question is about.** Which decision principle governs when they conflict.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Cases, judged by the principles in dispute.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Evidential decision theory.* Recommends one box. Faces the smoking lesion case, where it gives an answer widely judged wrong.
- *Causal decision theory.* Recommends two. Faces Newcomb, and faces cases where it recommends against outcomes it can foresee.
- *Functional and updateless variants.* Developed to handle both; each has its own cases, and none is settled.
- *Deny the setup.* Argue perfect prediction is incompatible with free choice. Reframes; the problem is restated with a merely reliable predictor.
- *Empirical study.* Reports what people choose, split. Not the question.

**Closure.** Ending 6 throughout. Each decision theory handles the cases the other fails, which is two instruments returning opposite verdicts on the same cases.

**What has been settled so far.** That the two principles conflict in this case, which is the point of the case. That each theory has cases the other handles better, documented over five decades.
**What would settle the rest.** A decision theory handling every standard case without introducing new ones. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current state of the functional and updateless literature would need checking before this route line is complete.

## 2.42 Zeno's dichotomy

**The question.** To cross a distance one must first cross half, and half of the remainder, without end. How is the crossing completed?

**One answer given.** The series converges. The infinite sum of intervals is finite, and the mathematics of limits, made rigorous in the nineteenth century, dissolves the puzzle entirely.

**The other answer given.** Convergence shows the sum is finite, not how infinitely many tasks are completed. The supertask question is distinct from the arithmetic and has its own literature, including constructions where a supertask yields a determinate but paradoxical final state.

**Origin conditions.** That a distance is composed of parts; that traversal is composed of traversals of parts.

**What both answers require.** That the traversal decomposes into the stated sequence.

**What the question is about.** The completion of an infinite sequence of tasks.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The arithmetic of the sequence, and physical measurements at finite resolution.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Convergence.* Settles the arithmetic completely, and the supertask literature holds this to be a distinct question.
- *Thomson's lamp and successors.* Show that a supertask can leave the final state undetermined by the sequence. Establishes that the arithmetic does not always fix the end state.
- *Discrete space or time.* Removes the decomposition. Not established, and faces its own difficulties in relativistic settings.
- *Denying the decomposition.* Holds the motion is not composed of sub-motions. Reframes; the decomposition is what the argument constructs.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 6. Convergence settles the arithmetic completely and the supertask literature treats it as a different question; the lamp shows final states can be underdetermined.

**What has been settled so far.** Convergence. The supertask results, showing final states can be underdetermined.
**What would settle the rest.** An account of task completion applying to the convergent case and the lamp alike.

## 2.43 The three-body problem

**The question.** Three bodies attract each other by gravity. Is there a formula giving their positions at any later time?

**One answer given.** No. Poincaré showed there are no sufficient integrals for the general problem, and the dynamics is chaotic: nearby initial conditions diverge exponentially, so no closed form of the sought kind exists.

**The other answer given.** Sundman produced a convergent series solution for the three-body problem, and Wang extended it to n bodies. A closed form in the classical sense is absent; a solution in the sense of a convergent expression is not.

**Origin conditions.** That a solution means a closed-form expression in known functions; that the initial conditions are exactly specified.

**What both answers require.** That the system's future is determined by its present state, and that the question is whether that determination is expressible.

**What the question is about.** Positions at arbitrary later times, for arbitrary initial data.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Numerical integration at finite precision, over finite times.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Search for integrals.* Poincaré and Bruns: no additional algebraic integrals. Closed by proof.
- *Sundman series.* Converges, and so slowly as to be useless for computation, which is stated in the literature. Answers the letter and not the use.
- *Numerical integration.* Accurate over intervals set by the Lyapunov time, after which precision requirements grow exponentially. Ends by measuring more of what was reached.
- *Perturbation and KAM theory.* Establish stability for most initial conditions in near-integrable regimes. A major positive result covering a restricted region.
- *Restricted and special cases.* Solved exactly — Lagrange points, figure-eight orbits, families of periodic solutions. Each is a measure-zero set.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 5. Poincaré and Bruns prove non-existence of integrals; Sundman's series converges and is useless, answering the letter and not the use.

**What has been settled so far.** Non-existence of algebraic integrals; Sundman convergence; KAM stability results; a large catalogue of exact special solutions.
**What would settle the rest.** Nothing further in the classical sense; the question of long-term solar system stability remains open and is pursued numerically.

## 2.44 The Fermi paradox

**The question.** The galaxy is old and large. If technological life is common, some should be evident. None is. Why?

**One answer given.** Life or technology is rare. A step in the sequence from chemistry to detectable technology is improbable enough to make us close to alone, and the absence of evidence is what rarity looks like.

**The other answer given.** The absence is not evidence yet. The volume searched, in frequency, sky coverage, sensitivity and duration, is a negligible fraction of the space, and quantitative accounting of the searched fraction shows it is close to zero.

**Origin conditions.** That technological civilisations would produce detectable signatures; that expansion or emission over galactic timescales is the expected behaviour.

**What both answers require.** That a civilisation would be detectable by the means used, so that absence bears on abundance.

**What the question is about.** Abundance, inferred from a searched fraction that is small and quantified.
**What the field's instruments reach.** A small and quantified fraction of the search space.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Radio search.* Sixty years, no confirmed detection. The searched fraction has been quantified — a small volume of a large space — which both sides accept.
- *Technosignature searches.* Waste heat, megastructures, atmospheric markers. Null so far, with sensitivity improving.
- *Great Filter reasoning.* Locates the improbable step. Where it lies is unconstrained by any measurement, which its proponents state.
- *Rare Earth arguments.* Assemble contingencies for our own case. Sample size one, which the field notes.
- *Anthropic and selection-effect arguments.* Show what our existence does and does not license inferring. Constrains inference; supplies no abundance.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 4. The searched fraction is quantified and small, and every route narrows without closing.

**What has been settled so far.** The searched fraction, quantified. Exoplanet abundance, now measured and large — which removes one candidate explanation.
**What would settle the rest.** A detection, or a search covering a fraction of the space large enough that a null result constrains abundance.

## 2.45 Poverty of the stimulus

**The question.** Children acquire grammar rapidly from limited and imperfect input. Is grammatical knowledge substantially innate?

**One answer given.** Yes. The input underdetermines the grammar acquired; children converge on structure-dependent rules without evidence distinguishing them from structure-independent alternatives, and formal learnability results show unconstrained learners cannot converge from positive evidence alone.

**The other answer given.** No, or much less than claimed. Corpus studies find the allegedly absent evidence is present at low but non-zero frequency; statistical learners trained on realistic corpora acquire the structures in question; and the learnability results assume a class of grammars and a learning criterion that are choices.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a determinate grammar acquired; that the input is characterisable as evidence for or against it; that learning is convergence on that grammar.

**What both answers require.** That the acquired grammar and the available input can each be specified well enough to compare.

**What the question is about.** What the learner brings, as against what the input supplies.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Corpora, acquisition timelines, and trained models.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Corpus frequency studies.* Find relevant constructions at low frequency. Whether low frequency suffices is the dispute, restated quantitatively.
- *Formal learnability.* Gold's negative result, and positive results under different criteria and priors. Each is relative to its class and criterion, which the literature states.
- *Computational modelling.* Statistical learners acquire several target structures from realistic input. Whether the model's architecture encodes the constraint in question is the recurring objection.
- *Large language models.* Acquire structure-dependence from text, with data volumes far exceeding a child's. The comparison of data volumes is now the disputed point.
- *Cross-linguistic and atypical acquisition studies.* Constrain timelines; both sides accommodate the findings.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Corpus frequency and modelling results are read oppositely by the two sides, which is the instruments disagreeing on the same evidence.

**What has been settled so far.** Corpus frequencies, measured. Gold's result and its dependence on criterion. That statistical learners acquire some target structures.
**What would settle the rest.** A specification of the learner's prior that both sides accept, tested against realistic input. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the recent modelling literature moves quickly and this route line would need checking.

## 2.46 Morphogenesis

**The question.** A fertilised egg becomes a body with parts in the right places. What determines where each part goes?

**One answer given.** Positional information. Cells read their position from concentration gradients of signalling molecules and interpret it against thresholds. Gradients are measured, thresholds are identified, and manipulating a gradient relocates the structure.

**The other answer given.** Self-organisation. Reaction-diffusion systems generate pattern with no pre-existing coordinates, produce the observed spacing and its scaling, and account for regeneration and for pattern in tissues where no gradient is found.

**Origin conditions.** That pattern is specified by information distinguishable from the tissue that carries it; that position is a variable cells can read.

**What both answers require.** That there is a determinant of pattern locatable either in a signal or in the dynamics.

**What the question is about.** What determines placement.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Concentrations, expression domains, and the effects of perturbation, all measured in tissue where both processes run together.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Gradient manipulation.* Relocates structures, establishing gradients are instructive where tested. Ends without excluding self-organising dynamics operating on them.
- *Reaction-diffusion identification.* Candidate Turing systems identified in digit spacing, hair follicle spacing and palatal ridges, with predicted perturbation outcomes confirmed. Establishes the mechanism in those cases.
- *Precision measurement of gradients.* Shows readout precision near physical limits in the fly embryo, which constrains how much information a gradient can carry.
- *Synthetic reconstitution.* Organoids generate axes and structures without maternal gradients. Establishes self-organisation is sufficient in those settings.
- *Modelling.* Both frameworks fit most datasets, which is the recurring finding, and hybrid models fit better than either.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 6. Gradient manipulation and Turing identification each establish a mechanism in its own cases; both frameworks fit most datasets, which is the disagreement.

**What has been settled so far.** Gradients are instructive in identified cases. Turing mechanisms operate in identified cases. Organoids self-organise. Readout precision is measured.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion determining, for a given tissue, which process sets the pattern — rather than fitting both. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the recent synthetic and organoid literature would need checking.

## 2.47 Homochirality

**The question.** Biological amino acids are almost all left-handed and sugars right-handed. Ordinary chemistry produces both equally. Why one hand?

**One answer given.** Amplification of a small initial bias. Autocatalytic reactions amplify tiny enantiomeric excesses to near-purity, demonstrated in the Soai reaction, and small biases are available from circularly polarised light or crystal surfaces. The initial bias may be accidental.

**The other answer given.** A systematic cause. The weak interaction is parity-violating and makes one enantiomer very slightly more stable, and this is not accidental; a universal bias would explain why the same hand appears everywhere rather than by chance.

**Origin conditions.** That the asymmetry originated once and was inherited; that a mechanism either selects a hand or amplifies whatever bias is present.

**What both answers require.** That the observed excess traces to a determinable origin.

**What the question is about.** The initial excess, and whether a bias that small is amplifiable.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Present chemistry, laboratory amplification, and meteoritic samples.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Autocatalytic amplification.* Demonstrated conclusively in the Soai reaction. Establishes amplification; does not supply the initial bias.
- *Parity-violation energy difference.* Calculated, and of order 10⁻¹⁷ in relative terms. Whether amplification can act on a bias that small over available timescales is disputed and is the crux.
- *Circularly polarised light.* Produces measurable excesses; astronomical sources of such light are identified in star-forming regions.
- *Meteoritic amino acids.* Show left-handed excesses in some samples, which supports an extraterrestrial bias and relocates rather than settles the origin.
- *Crystallisation and surface effects.* Produce excesses readily, without a preferred direction.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 4. Amplification is demonstrated and does not supply the bias; parity-violation energy differences are calculated and their sufficiency is the crux.

**What has been settled so far.** Amplification, demonstrated. Parity-violation energy differences, calculated. Meteoritic excesses, measured. Astronomical polarised sources, identified.
**What would settle the rest.** A demonstration that a bias of the size available is amplified to observed purity under prebiotic conditions.

## 2.48 Matter and antimatter asymmetry

**The question.** The laws produce matter and antimatter nearly symmetrically. The universe is matter. Where did the antimatter go?

**One answer given.** Known physics is insufficient and new physics is required. Sakharov's conditions are agreed, and the CP violation measured in the Standard Model is smaller than the observed asymmetry by roughly ten orders of magnitude.

**The other answer given.** The mechanism is unidentified rather than the physics wrong. Leptogenesis, electroweak baryogenesis with an extended Higgs sector, and other mechanisms are consistent with all data; the failure is to distinguish among them, not a failure of the framework.

**Origin conditions.** That the initial state was symmetric; that the asymmetry was generated dynamically; that Sakharov's three conditions are necessary and sufficient.

**What both answers require.** That the asymmetry was produced by a mechanism operating in the early universe.

**What the question is about.** Conditions at energies and epochs not accessible.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Collider energies, precision CP measurements, and cosmological observations of the resulting abundance.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Standard Model CP violation.* Measured precisely in kaon, B and now D systems. Established, and quantitatively insufficient by many orders.
- *Electroweak baryogenesis.* Requires a first-order transition, which the measured Higgs mass excludes in the minimal model; extensions remain viable and are constrained by collider and electric dipole moment searches.
- *Leptogenesis.* Consistent and attractive; its scale is typically far above collider reach, which its proponents state. Neutrinoless double beta decay would support the required Majorana nature; searches are null so far.
- *Electric dipole moment searches.* Improving by orders of magnitude and null, excluding parameter space for several mechanisms.
- *Direct search for baryon number violation.* Proton decay searches null, with lifetime limits now extremely long.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 4. Standard Model CP violation is measured and insufficient by orders; every proposed mechanism narrows without closing.

**What has been settled so far.** Sakharov's conditions. The Standard Model's CP violation, measured and insufficient. Exclusion of minimal electroweak baryogenesis. Substantial dipole-moment and proton-decay limits.
**What would settle the rest.** Observation of a mechanism's signature — neutrinoless double beta decay, a non-zero dipole moment, or new states at accessible energy.

## 2.49 The strong CP problem

**The question.** A parameter in the strong interaction could take any value between zero and 2π. Measurement bounds it below 10⁻¹⁰. Why is it so small?

**One answer given.** A mechanism sets it. The Peccei-Quinn symmetry makes the parameter dynamical and relaxes it to zero, predicting a new particle, the axion. The mechanism is economical and has other uses.

**The other answer given.** It may require no explanation. The parameter is a free constant and small values are permitted; the demand for explanation rests on a measure over parameter space that is stipulated, and decades of axion searches have returned nothing.

**Origin conditions.** That a dimensionless parameter's value calls for explanation when it is far from a typical value under an assumed measure.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about whether a value requires explanation.

**What the question is about.** A measure over possible parameter values.
**What the field's instruments reach.** One measured value, bounded.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Axion searches.* Haloscopes, helioscopes and astrophysical bounds have excluded substantial mass ranges and continue. Narrows without closing; open.
- *Massless up quark.* Would remove the problem; lattice calculations exclude it. Closed.
- *Nelson-Barr mechanisms.* Impose CP as a spontaneously broken symmetry. Viable, with model-building costs, and no distinguishing observation.
- *Anthropic reasoning.* Argues no explanation is needed. Its critics note the parameter has little anthropic relevance, which weakens the argument here specifically.
- *Neutron dipole moment measurement.* The bound that defines the problem, improving steadily and null.

**Closure.** Endings 4 and 5, with one route closed. Lattice calculation excludes the massless up quark; axion searches narrow without closing.

**What has been settled so far.** The bound itself. Exclusion of the massless up quark. Large excluded regions of axion parameter space.
**What would settle the rest.** Detection of an axion, or an agreed criterion for when a parameter value requires explanation.

## 2.50 The problem of time in quantum gravity

**The question.** Quantum mechanics treats time as an external parameter. General relativity treats it as part of the dynamical geometry. What is time in a theory of both?

**One answer given.** Time is emergent and the fundamental equation is timeless. The Wheeler-DeWitt equation contains no time parameter, and apparent time arises from correlations between subsystems — one variable serving as a clock for the rest.

**The other answer given.** A timeless fundamental theory has not been shown to recover ordinary dynamics, and the conditional-probability route has known technical problems. Some programmes reintroduce a preferred time and reproduce general relativity's predictions in the tested regime.

**Origin conditions.** That a quantum theory requires a time parameter for evolution; that general covariance forbids a preferred one.

**What both answers require.** That the two treatments must be reconciled in one theory covering both regimes.

**What the question is about.** The structure of a theory at scales not accessible.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The two theories separately, each confirmed in its own regime.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Wheeler-DeWitt with an internal clock.* Faces operator ordering, the measure, and the question of which variable is the clock.
- *Page-Wootters conditional probabilities.* Recovers evolution relative to a clock subsystem; early objections were addressed and the interpretation remains disputed.
- *Shape dynamics and preferred-foliation approaches.* Reproduce general relativity where tested, at the cost of the symmetry the other side takes as fundamental.
- *Semiclassical emergence.* Recovers a time parameter in the appropriate limit, with corrections; the limit is where both theories already work.
- *Observation.* No experiment probes the regime. The field states this.

**Closure.** Endings 2, 3 and 4. Semiclassical emergence recovers time where both theories already work; no observation reaches the regime.

**What has been settled so far.** The formal structure of the constraint equations. The semiclassical recovery in its own limit. That no observation currently discriminates.
**What would settle the rest.** An observable consequence at accessible energies. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the recent Page-Wootters and quantum-reference-frame literature would need checking.

## 2.51 Chronic pain

**The question.** Pain persists in many patients after tissue damage has healed, or with no damage found. What produces it?

**One answer given.** Central sensitisation. Changes in the nervous system's own processing amplify and sustain signalling independently of peripheral input; the changes are measurable, and the pain is real and neurally produced.

**The other answer given.** Peripheral drivers persist and are missed. Small-fibre pathology, occult inflammation and nerve injury are found in substantial fractions of patients previously classed as centrally driven, and improved detection keeps moving cases from one category to the other.

**Origin conditions.** That pain has a locatable driver, peripheral or central; that finding it determines the treatment.

**What both answers require.** That the pain is produced by an identifiable process at one location or the other.

**What the question is about.** Which process drives the persistence in a given patient.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Imaging, quantitative sensory testing, and biopsy, each returning findings compatible with both accounts in most patients.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Peripheral blockade.* Relieves some patients and not others, which both sides read as supporting their account for those patients.
- *Skin biopsy for small-fibre density.* Moves a measured fraction of patients into the peripheral category. Narrows and does not close.
- *Functional imaging.* Identifies central changes reliably. Whether they are cause or consequence is not settled by the imaging, which the field states.
- *Psychological and multidisciplinary treatment.* Improves outcomes substantially without identifying a driver. Ends by explaining something adjacent.
- *Nerve growth factor and channel-targeted drugs.* Show efficacy in specific conditions, defining subgroups rather than settling the general question.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Imaging establishes central change without separating cause from consequence; biopsy moves a measured fraction between categories.

**What has been settled so far.** Central changes, measured. Small-fibre pathology in a measured fraction. Efficacy of specific targeted agents in defined subgroups.
**What would settle the rest.** A test assigning an individual patient's pain to a driver, prospectively. **nye — not yet explored fully:** this field moves quickly and the route record here is stated from general knowledge.

## 2.52 The axiom of choice

**The question.** Given infinitely many non-empty sets, can one always select an element from each?

**One answer given.** Yes, and the axiom is indispensable. Without it, standard results fail: every vector space has a basis, every field has an algebraic closure, arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact, and countable unions of countable sets are countable. Mathematics as practised uses it constantly.

**The other answer given.** Its consequences include results widely regarded as unacceptable. Banach-Tarski: a solid ball decomposes into finitely many pieces reassembling into two balls of the same size. The pieces are non-measurable, so no contradiction follows, and constructive mathematics proceeds without the axiom at known cost.

**Origin conditions.** That a selection may be asserted without a rule producing it; that sets exist independently of any construction of them.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about which axioms hold of the sets.

**What the question is about.** Whether a selection may be asserted where no rule produces it.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Consequences of chosen axiom systems.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Prove or refute from the other axioms.* Gödel showed consistency, Cohen independence. Closed by proof.
- *Weaker choice principles.* Countable choice, dependent choice, ultrafilter lemma — each suffices for part of the practice and not all, and the hierarchy is mapped precisely.
- *Determinacy axioms.* Contradict full choice, imply all sets of reals are measurable, and remove Banach-Tarski. Consistent relative to large cardinals, and they conflict with choice, which is the trade.
- *Constructive mathematics.* Proceeds without the axiom, and the results obtainable are precisely characterised. A known and exact cost.
- *Locate the fault in measurability.* Banach-Tarski uses non-measurable sets, whose existence follows from choice. The dispute becomes whether non-measurable sets are acceptable, which is the original dispute.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Independence is proved; the determinacy alternative is a stated trade; constructive mathematics has an exact and known cost.

**What has been settled so far.** Independence. The full hierarchy of weaker choice principles. The determinacy alternative and its consistency strength. The precise scope of constructive mathematics.
**What would settle the rest.** An agreed criterion for accepting a set-existence axiom as true rather than as consistent and useful.

## 2.53 Skolem's paradox

**The question.** Set theory proves uncountable sets exist, and has a countable model. How can a countable model contain uncountable sets?

**One answer given.** There is no paradox. Uncountability is a statement about the absence of a bijection, and the required bijection exists outside the model but not inside it. Countability is relative to a model, which the Löwenheim-Skolem theorem establishes.

**The other answer given.** The relativity is the difficulty, not its removal. If "uncountable" holds relative to a model, then either there is an absolute notion the models approximate, or set-theoretic language does not determine its own subject matter. Skolem drew the second conclusion, and the dispute over which follows has continued.

**Origin conditions.** That first-order axioms characterise the structures they are about; that cardinality is a property of a set.

**What both answers require.** That the axioms are about something, and the question is whether they determine it.

**What the question is about.** Whether the axioms fix their subject matter.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Models satisfying the axioms, of which there are many non-isomorphic ones.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Relativisation.* Removes the contradiction completely. Both sides accept it; the disagreement is about what follows.
- *Second-order logic.* Categorical characterisations become available. Its semantics presuppose the set theory in question, which is the standing objection.
- *Categoricity results.* Full second-order set theory is quasi-categorical, given a background notion of set. Same objection at one remove.
- *Structuralism.* Holds that only structure is determined and nothing further is missing. Reframes; whether it answers the determinacy question is disputed.
- *Skolem's own conclusion.* That set-theoretic notions are relative. Held by a minority and not refuted.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Relativisation removes the contradiction and both sides accept it; second-order categoricity presupposes the set theory in question.

**What has been settled so far.** Löwenheim-Skolem and its consequences. The relativity of cardinality to a model. The precise sense in which second-order characterisations are categorical and what they presuppose.
**What would settle the rest.** An account of how first-order axioms could determine their subject matter, or an accepted demonstration that determination is not required.

## 2.54 The reference class problem

**The question.** A probability is assigned to a single case by placing it in a class of similar cases. Every case belongs to many classes with different frequencies. Which class?

**One answer given.** The narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist. Reichenbach's rule, refined by a homogeneity requirement: narrow until no further partition changes the frequency, and stop there. The rule is applied daily and successfully in actuarial practice, medicine and forecasting, where reference classes are chosen by exactly this criterion and the resulting probabilities are calibrated against outcomes.

**The other answer given.** No rule selects the class. Narrowing terminates in the single case, where the frequency is one or zero; stopping earlier requires a judgment the rule does not supply. The problem recurs in every interpretation of probability, including subjectivist ones where it appears as the choice of what to condition on.

**Origin conditions.** That a single case's probability derives from a frequency in a class; that classes are given prior to the assignment.

**What both answers require.** That the single case has a probability and a class determines it.

**What the question is about.** Which of many true descriptions of a case governs.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Frequencies in classes, once a class is chosen.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Narrowest homogeneous class.* Terminates at the single case unless stopped by a judgment about relevance.
- *Propensity interpretations.* Assign probability to the case's own physical setup, removing the class. Faces the difficulty of identifying the setup, which is the reference class question restated.
- *Subjective probability.* Removes the class in favour of degrees of belief. The choice of conditioning information is the same problem, as its own literature notes.
- *Statistical model selection.* Chooses among candidate classes by fit and penalty. Returns a class relative to a criterion.
- *Legal and actuarial practice.* Fixes classes by convention and stipulation. Works, and the stipulation is the thing in question.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Narrowing terminates at the single case; every interpretation absorbs the problem at its own site, which its own literature records.

**What has been settled so far.** That the problem recurs in every interpretation is documented across the literature, including by proponents of each.
**What would settle the rest.** A principled selection of the class, not itself resting on a judgment of relevance.

## 2.55 Grue

**The question.** Evidence that all observed emeralds are green equally supports the prediction that they are grue — green if observed before a time, blue if after. Why does one projection succeed and not the other?

**One answer given.** Predicates differ in projectibility, and entrenchment settles which. Goodman's own answer: predicates that have figured in successful past inductions are entrenched, and entrenchment is a matter of record.

**The other answer given.** Entrenchment describes practice without justifying it. The grue predicate is definable from green and a time, and green is equally definable from grue and bleen; the asymmetry is not in the logic, and every proposed non-circular criterion of naturalness has been contested.

**Origin conditions.** That evidence supports a hypothesis by a relation statable in logical terms; that the vocabulary is given.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about which predicates support projection.

**What the question is about.** Which vocabulary the world is described in.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observations, which are describable in either vocabulary.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Entrenchment.* Describes practice. Its status as justification is the dispute.
- *Natural kinds and natural properties.* Lewis's naturalness ranking supplies the asymmetry, at the cost of a primitive the account does not derive.
- *Simplicity of definition.* Grue requires a time reference; the interdefinability shows this is symmetric, which Goodman constructs precisely to show.
- *Causal or nomic accounts.* Project only predicates in laws. Laws are stated in projectible predicates, which is the circle.
- *Bayesian priors.* Assign lower prior to grue-like hypotheses. The prior encodes the asymmetry rather than deriving it, which its proponents accept.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Entrenchment describes practice; naturalness and Bayesian priors encode the asymmetry rather than deriving it, which their proponents accept.

**What has been settled so far.** The interdefinability, which is a formal result. That every proposed criterion so far either takes naturalness as primitive or encodes it in a prior.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion for projectibility derived from something other than a prior judgment of which predicates are natural.

## 2.56 The ravens

**The question.** "All ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens." Does observing a white shoe confirm that all ravens are black?

**One answer given.** Yes, by a negligible amount. Bayesian analysis gives the confirmation as real and tiny, proportional to the relative sizes of the classes, and the appearance of absurdity is a failure of intuition about small quantities.

**The other answer given.** The Bayesian resolution depends on assumptions about the sampling and the class sizes that are supplied rather than derived, and reverses under different but equally reasonable assumptions. The relation between a generalisation and its instances is not settled by the calculation.

**Origin conditions.** That confirmation is a relation between statements, invariant under logical equivalence; that instances confirm generalisations.

**What both answers require.** That there is a confirmation relation with determinate values.

**What the question is about.** The confirmation relation in general.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Particular calculations under particular assumptions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Bayesian quantification.* Yields tiny confirmation under standard assumptions. The assumptions are the disputed point, and reversals are exhibited in the literature.
- *Deny equivalence-invariance of confirmation.* Blocks the paradox and gives up a condition most accounts regard as basic.
- *Sampling considerations.* Whether the shoe was drawn from non-black things or from shoes changes the result. Establishes that the relation depends on how evidence is gathered, which is not in the original statement.
- *Selective confirmation.* Restricts confirmation to instances of the subject class. Ad hoc by its critics' account, and it blocks the paradox.
- *Reject the whole confirmation-relation framework.* Some do; the alternative accounts have their own open problems.

**Closure.** Endings 6 and 8. The Bayesian calculation reverses under equally reasonable assumptions; selective confirmation blocks the paradox at an acknowledged cost.

**What has been settled so far.** The equivalence, which is trivial. The Bayesian calculation and its dependence on the priors. Documented reversals under alternative assumptions.
**What would settle the rest.** A confirmation relation whose values do not depend on assumptions supplied case by case.

## 2.57 Personal identity over time

**The question.** A thing has all its parts replaced gradually. Is it the same thing? For a person, what makes someone the same person as the child they were?

**One answer given.** Psychological continuity. Overlapping chains of memory, intention and character constitute identity, and the account matches judgments about ordinary cases and about imagined transfers.

**The other answer given.** Biological continuity. A person is an organism, and the organism persists through psychological discontinuity — dreamless sleep, coma, early infancy — where the psychological account gives the wrong result or none.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a determinate fact about whether a later thing is the earlier one; that a criterion states it.

**What both answers require.** That identity over time is determinate and criterion-governed.

**What the question is about.** A relation across time.
**What the field's instruments reach.** States at times, and the relations between them, all compatible with both criteria.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Fission cases.* A person divided into two psychologically continuous successors. Both criteria give unacceptable results, and the case is agreed to be decisive against simple versions.
- *Parfit's response.* Identity is not what matters; what matters is continuity, which admits of degree. Widely influential and widely rejected as changing the question.
- *Four-dimensionalism.* Persons are extended in time and fission is two overlapping persons. Consistent, at the cost of a metaphysics the other side rejects.
- *Animalism.* Solves fission by the organism. Faces transplant intuitions, which its proponents accept and bite.
- *Deny determinacy.* Some cases have no answer. Its critics hold that first-person survival does not admit of indeterminacy.

**Closure.** Ending 5, by case. Fission defeats simple versions of both criteria, which both sides accept as decisive.

**What has been settled so far.** That fission defeats simple versions of both criteria. That each surviving account carries a cost its rivals identify precisely.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion handling fission, sleep, and gradual replacement without introducing a cost the other side counts as decisive.

## 2.58 Path dependence and lock-in

**The question.** Some technologies and institutions persist though better alternatives exist. Is the persistence inefficiency, or evidence that the alternative is not better?

**One answer given.** Lock-in is real and inefficient. Increasing returns, network effects and switching costs make an early advantage self-reinforcing, and the outcome need bear no relation to merit. Formal models produce it reliably.

**The other answer given.** Claimed cases dissolve on inspection. Liebowitz and Margolis re-examined the standard examples and found the alleged superior alternatives were not superior on the relevant margins, and that no case of remediable inefficiency had been demonstrated.

**Origin conditions.** That the alternatives can be compared on a standard independent of what was adopted; that adoption reflects or fails to reflect that standard.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about which alternative is better, statable independently of which was adopted.

**What the question is about.** A counterfactual: how the unadopted alternative would have performed at scale.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The adopted path, and small-scale or laboratory comparisons.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Formal modelling.* Produces lock-in under stated conditions. Whether the conditions obtain is the empirical question.
- *Historical case studies.* The standard cases were re-examined and contested; the re-examinations were contested in turn, and no case is agreed.
- *Laboratory experiments.* Produce lock-in reliably under controlled network effects. Transfer to markets with entry and side payments is disputed.
- *Look for remediable inefficiency.* Requires the counterfactual performance at scale, which is unobservable by construction.
- *Ex post comparison after a switch.* Where switches occur, comparison is available. Selection into switching is the confounding, which the field notes.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Historical cases were re-examined and the re-examinations contested, which is the field's instruments disagreeing on the same episodes.

**What has been settled so far.** The formal conditions producing lock-in. The laboratory demonstrations. That the standard historical cases are contested in both directions.
**What would settle the rest.** A case in which the unadopted alternative's performance at scale is established independently.

## 2.59 The hermeneutic circle

**The question.** Understanding a part of a text requires understanding the whole; understanding the whole requires understanding the parts. How does interpretation begin?

**One answer given.** The circle is productive rather than vicious. Interpretation proceeds by provisional understanding of the whole, revised as parts are read, and revised again — a spiral rather than a circle, and it is how understanding actually works.

**The other answer given.** Naming it productive does not remove the problem of validation. If every reading is revisable in the light of the whole and the whole in the light of readings, no reading is ever adjudicated, and the criterion distinguishing better interpretation from worse is exactly what the circle withholds.

**Origin conditions.** That a text has a determinate meaning; that interpretation approaches it; that parts and whole are distinguishable.

**What both answers require.** That there is something the interpretation is of, against which readings can be better or worse.

**What the question is about.** A criterion for the correctness of a reading.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Readings, each already inside the circle.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Authorial intention as the criterion.* Hirsch. Faces the difficulty that intention is itself reconstructed by interpretation, which is the circle.
- *Historical context as the criterion.* Context is a text requiring interpretation, at one remove.
- *Community of interpreters.* Fish and others: validity is what the community accepts. Accounts for practice and gives up the independent criterion, which its proponents accept.
- *Philological method.* Establishes what the words meant at the time, with real results. Underdetermines the readings in dispute, which are usually about significance rather than lexis.
- *Reject the whole-part framing.* Reframes; the dependence reappears at whatever units are chosen.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Philological method settles a class of questions and not the disputed class; community-of-interpreters accounts give up the independent criterion.

**What has been settled so far.** That the dependence is real and not removable by ordering the reading. That philological method settles a class of questions and not the disputed class.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion for a reading's correctness not itself established by interpretation.

## 2.60 Hard cases in law

**The question.** Some legal cases are not settled by the applicable rules. Does the law contain an answer, or does the judge make one?

**One answer given.** The law contains an answer. Beyond rules there are principles with weight, and the correct answer is the one fitting and justifying the legal materials as a whole. Judges are not exercising discretion but seeking that fit.

**The other answer given.** Where rules run out, the judge exercises discretion and makes new law. Legal materials are open-textured, they underdetermine outcomes in exactly the hard cases, and disagreement among competent judges over the same materials is the evidence.

**Origin conditions.** That the legal materials either determine an outcome or do not; that a judge's task is to apply the law.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about whether the materials determine the case.

**What the question is about.** Whether the materials determine an outcome.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Decided cases, in which an outcome was reached either way.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Point to the decided outcome.* Every hard case gets an outcome, which both sides predict and read oppositely.
- *Analyse judicial reasoning.* Judges write as though finding rather than making. Both sides accept the practice and read it differently.
- *Empirical study of judicial behaviour.* Finds ideology predicts outcomes in contested cases with measurable effect. Constrains the finding account and does not settle whether an answer existed.
- *Inclusive positivism.* Admits moral criteria into the rule of recognition where a legal system so provides. Narrows the dispute and leaves the determinacy question.
- *Critical legal studies.* Holds indeterminacy pervasive. Its critics note most cases are not litigated because the answer is clear, which is measurable and true.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 3. Litigation rates and ideology studies bear on adjacent questions; every hard case gets an outcome, which both sides predict.

**What has been settled so far.** That most legal questions are determinate, from litigation rates. That ideology has measurable predictive power in contested cases. The scope of inclusive positivism's concession.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion, applicable before decision, determining whether the materials contain an answer.

## 2.61 Natural law and legal positivism

**The question.** Is an unjust rule, properly enacted, law?

**One answer given.** Yes. Law and morality are separate questions: what the law is, is settled by social facts about enactment and acceptance; whether it is just is a further question. Keeping them separate is what makes criticism of law possible.

**The other answer given.** Not entirely. A rule of sufficient injustice fails as law, and the tradition holds legal systems have an internal morality without which they do not function as legal systems at all. Post-war tribunals and the treatment of enacted atrocity are the standing cases.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a fact about what the law is, determined by criteria; that the criteria are either exhausted by social facts or include moral ones.

**What both answers require.** That "law" has a determinate extension.

**What the question is about.** The criteria of legal validity.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Legal practice, in which officials apply criteria they may state or not.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *The Hart-Fuller exchange over the grudge informer cases.* Each account produced a coherent treatment and reached a different characterisation of the same events. Documented and unresolved.
- *Rule of recognition.* Locates validity in official practice. Whether the practice can incorporate moral criteria is the inclusive-positivism debate, ongoing.
- *Internal morality of law.* Fuller's eight desiderata. Their necessity is accepted widely; whether they constitute a moral rather than an efficacy constraint is disputed.
- *Constitutional practice.* Many systems incorporate moral criteria explicitly, which positivism accommodates as a contingent fact and natural law reads as recognition.
- *Comparative and historical study.* Documents both patterns extensively; supports both readings.

**Closure.** Ending 6. The Hart-Fuller exchange produced two coherent treatments of one set of events reaching different characterisations.

**What has been settled so far.** That the desiderata are necessary for a functioning legal system. That systems incorporating moral criteria are common. The precise terms of the inclusive-exclusive division.
**What would settle the rest.** A determination of whether the moral criteria are part of the concept or contingently adopted, not resting on which cases are chosen as central.

## 2.62 Information as fundamental

**The question.** Is physical reality made of information, or is information a description we apply to physical things?

**One answer given.** Information is fundamental. Landauer's principle links erasure to a thermodynamic cost, measured experimentally; black hole entropy scales with area in units of information; and quantum theory has been reconstructed from information-theoretic axioms.

**The other answer given.** These are results about physical systems described informationally, not evidence that information is the substrate. Information requires a physical carrier and an encoding; the reconstruction results show quantum theory follows from principles about what can be done with systems, which presupposes systems.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a fact about what is fundamental; that the relation between information and its carrier is one of dependence in one direction.

**What both answers require.** That "fundamental" has a determinate sense in which one of these is it.

**What the question is about.** A dependence relation between two descriptions, both of which apply.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Physical systems, measured, and describable both ways.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Landauer's principle.* Verified experimentally at the predicted bound. Establishes a link and is read both ways: information has a physical cost, or physics is informational.
- *Black hole thermodynamics.* Entropy scales with area. A major result, and its interpretation is the dispute at one more site.
- *Information-theoretic reconstructions of quantum theory.* Derive the formalism from axioms about information processing. The axioms quantify over systems, which is the standing objection.
- *It-from-bit programmes.* Propose informational ontologies. No distinguishing prediction has been produced.
- *Digital physics and cellular automaton models.* Constrained by Lorentz invariance and by Bell correlations; specific proposals have been excluded.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 4. Landauer and area scaling are established and read both ways; digital models are constrained and specific proposals excluded.

**What has been settled so far.** Landauer's bound, verified. Area scaling of black hole entropy. The reconstruction theorems. Exclusion of specific digital models.
**What would settle the rest.** A prediction differing between the two readings.

## 2.63 Meno's paradox

**The question.** If you know what you are looking for, you need not look. If you do not, you would not recognise it. How is inquiry possible?

**One answer given.** By partial knowledge. Inquiry begins from knowing enough to specify the question and not enough to answer it, and the two horns falsely assume knowledge is all or nothing. The structure is ordinary: a detective knows the crime and not the culprit, a chemist knows the reaction and not the mechanism, and in each case the object is specified under one description and sought under another. Recognition is by the specifying description, which the inquirer has, so neither horn bites.

**The other answer given.** Specifying the question already presupposes the concepts the inquiry is meant to acquire, and the difficulty recurs at the level of concepts: acquiring a genuinely new concept cannot be described as recognising an answer to a question posed in prior terms. Fodor argued that concept learning is impossible for this reason, and the argument has not been generally defeated.

**Origin conditions.** That inquiry is the search for something specifiable in advance; that recognising a result requires a prior criterion.

**What both answers require.** That inquiry has an object identifiable by the inquirer.

**What the question is about.** Acquisition of what was not already available.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Learners already equipped with concepts, studied acquiring further ones.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Partial knowledge.* Dissolves the classical version. Does not reach the concept-acquisition version.
- *Recollection.* Plato's own answer. Not taken up as literal by later accounts.
- *Statistical and associative learning.* Accounts for acquisition of many categories from data. Whether the primitives are learned or presupposed is the dispute, and the architecture supplies them, which is the objection.
- *Fodor's radical concept nativism.* Concludes primitives are innate. Widely resisted, and no generally accepted refutation of the argument itself.
- *Developmental studies.* Document the sequence of acquisition. Establishes when, not how the recognition problem is solved.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Partial knowledge dissolves the classical version and not the acquisition version; Fodor's argument stands unrefuted rather than accepted.

**What has been settled so far.** The classical version dissolves. The acquisition sequence is documented. Fodor's argument stands as unrefuted rather than accepted, which is its documented status.
**What would settle the rest.** An account of acquiring a primitive concept not presupposing it in the mechanism.

## 2.64 The equity premium puzzle

**The question.** Stocks have returned far more than safe bonds over long periods. Standard models say the gap should be small. Why is it large?

**One answer given.** The models are wrong about preferences. Matching the observed premium with standard utility requires implausibly high risk aversion, and alternative preference specifications — habit formation, recursive utility, loss aversion — reproduce the premium with plausible parameters.

**The other answer given.** The premium may be smaller than measured. Survivorship bias in the sample of surviving markets, a possible peso problem in which rare disasters did not occur in the sample, and the ex post rather than ex ante nature of the measurement all inflate the estimate. Rare disaster models reproduce the premium with standard preferences.

**Origin conditions.** That asset prices are set by a representative agent maximising expected utility over consumption; that historical returns estimate expected returns.

**What both answers require.** That there is a true expected premium the models should reproduce.

**What the question is about.** Expected returns, and preferences over states that did not occur.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Realised returns in samples that did occur.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Raise risk aversion.* Reproduces the premium and produces the risk-free rate puzzle, which is the same problem at the other price.
- *Alternative preferences.* Habit and Epstein-Zin specifications fit; each introduces parameters not independently measured, which the field states.
- *Rare disasters.* Barro's calibration to international disaster frequencies reproduces the premium with standard preferences. Requires disasters at rates estimated from other countries' histories, and is not testable within the sample.
- *Broaden the sample.* Cross-country and long-horizon datasets reduce the estimate somewhat and do not remove it.
- *Heterogeneous agents and limited participation.* Reduce the required aversion. Fit improves and the fitting parameters multiply.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 8. Alternative preference specifications fit by introducing parameters not independently measured; disaster calibration is not testable within the sample.

**What has been settled so far.** The magnitude of the historical premium in many countries. The risk-free rate puzzle's connection to the aversion route. That disaster calibrations reproduce the premium.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurement of the relevant preference parameters independent of asset prices.

## 2.65 The Easterlin paradox

**The question.** Within a country, richer people report greater wellbeing. Over time, as a country grows richer, average reported wellbeing does not rise correspondingly. Which is the relation?

**One answer given.** Relative income governs. Wellbeing depends on position within a reference group, so raising everyone's income leaves positions unchanged and average reports flat. The cross-sectional and time-series findings are then consistent.

**The other answer given.** The time-series flatness is a measurement artefact. Stevenson and Wolfers, using larger international datasets and a log specification, find no satiation point and a consistent income-wellbeing relation within and across countries and over time; the paradox arose from short series and bounded scales.

**Origin conditions.** That reported wellbeing measures a quantity comparable across persons and times; that a bounded scale can register unbounded change.

**What both answers require.** That the reports measure the same thing in both comparisons.

**What the question is about.** Wellbeing, compared across times and populations.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Responses on bounded scales, whose interpretation may shift with the population.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Extend the time series.* Both sides have done so and read the extended series differently; the dispute continued through successive datasets.
- *Change the functional form.* Log income removes the apparent satiation. Whether the log form is correct is not settled by the data, which fits both.
- *Distinguish evaluative from experienced wellbeing.* The two measures behave differently, with evaluative tracking income further. Refines the question and does not settle the original comparison.
- *Anchoring vignettes.* Correct for scale interpretation across cultures and change some cross-country rankings. A genuine advance, applied to a subset of datasets.
- *Objective correlates.* Health, longevity, and time-use measures rise with income. Both sides accept these and dispute whether they are the subject.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Both sides read the extended series differently, which is the instruments disagreeing; anchoring corrects some cross-country rankings.

**What has been settled so far.** The cross-sectional relation. The distinction between evaluative and experienced measures. The effect of scale anchoring on cross-country comparison.
**What would settle the rest.** A wellbeing measure demonstrably comparable across populations and times, not bounded in a way that could absorb the trend.

## 2.66 Allais and Ellsberg

**The question.** People make choices that violate expected utility axioms, consistently and on reflection. Are the axioms wrong, or the choices?

**One answer given.** The axioms describe rationality and the choices are errors. Independence follows from a sure-thing principle most people endorse when it is stated to them abstractly, a person violating it can be presented with a sequence of trades leaving them worse off by their own lights, and many shown the inconsistency revise. A theory of rational choice answers to what people would endorse on reflection, not to what they do.

**The other answer given.** Many do not revise on reflection, and the Ellsberg pattern reflects a real distinction between risk with known probabilities and ambiguity without them, which the axioms cannot represent. A normative theory that competent reflective agents reject is not obviously the normative one.

**Origin conditions.** That preferences over outcomes are represented by a utility function and probabilities; that the independence axiom holds.

**What both answers require.** That there is a standard of correct choice against which the pattern is right or wrong.

**What the question is about.** Whether the axioms state the standard.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observed choices, and reports after being shown the inconsistency.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Show the inconsistency and re-ask.* Some revise, some do not, and the split is stable across studies.
- *Weaken independence.* Rank-dependent and prospect theories accommodate Allais. Each introduces functions fitted to the data they explain, which their authors state.
- *Non-additive beliefs and multiple priors.* Accommodate Ellsberg by representing ambiguity. Establishes representability and not which representation is correct.
- *Raise the stakes.* Violations persist at substantial stakes in several studies; the highest-stakes evidence is limited.
- *Neuroeconomic measurement.* Distinct activity for ambiguity and risk. Establishes the distinction is registered; does not adjudicate the normative question.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Rank-dependent and prospect theories accommodate the patterns with functions fitted to the data they explain.

**What has been settled so far.** The robustness of both patterns. The representability of ambiguity aversion. That reflective revision is partial and stable.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion for the normative status of an axiom other than its acceptance on reflection.

## 2.67 Time preference and preference reversal

**The question.** People discount future rewards at rates that fall as the delay grows, and reverse earlier choices as a date approaches. Is discounting a stable rate?

**One answer given.** Not a single rate. Hyperbolic discounting fits the reversals directly and is documented across humans and animals, and the reversals follow from the shape of the curve rather than from any change in preference.

**The other answer given.** Measured hyperbolic patterns can arise from aggregation over heterogeneous exponential discounters, from uncertainty about receipt, and from procedural features of the elicitation. Individual-level data are less clearly hyperbolic than aggregate data, and the reversals may reflect changing information rather than changing preference.

**Origin conditions.** That a person's valuation of a delayed reward is a function of delay alone; that elicited choices measure that function.

**What both answers require.** That there is a discount function to be measured.

**What the question is about.** An individual's valuation function.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Elicited choices, aggregated, and sensitive to format.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Elicit at individual level.* Reduces the aggregation artefact; heterogeneity within individuals across domains remains.
- *Vary elicitation format.* Changes the estimated rates substantially, which is documented and is the standing methodological problem.
- *Control for uncertainty of receipt.* Reduces apparent hyperbolicity in some designs. Not eliminated.
- *Field measurement from real decisions.* Estimates from borrowing and saving behaviour vary by orders of magnitude across studies and domains.
- *Commitment device studies.* Show demand for commitment, which supports the reversal account behaviourally. Interpretation of demand is contested.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 6. Estimated rates vary by elicitation format and by domain, which is the instruments disagreeing on the same quantity.

**What has been settled so far.** Format sensitivity. Aggregation effects. Real demand for commitment devices. The wide spread of field estimates.
**What would settle the rest.** An elicitation whose estimates are invariant to format and domain.

## 2.68 The productivity paradox

**The question.** Large investments in information technology were followed by measured productivity growth that did not rise correspondingly. Where did the gains go?

**One answer given.** Measurement misses them. Output quality, variety, and free digital services are poorly captured in national accounts, and the gains are real but unmeasured. Successive studies find substantial unmeasured consumer surplus.

**The other answer given.** Mismeasurement is insufficient in magnitude. Syverson showed the slowdown is broad across countries and sectors, including sectors with little digital output, and that plausible mismeasurement adjustments do not close the gap. The gains may be smaller than expected, or delayed by complementary reorganisation.

**Origin conditions.** That productivity is output per input, both measurable; that technology raises output for given inputs.

**What both answers require.** That there is a true productivity change the statistics approximate.

**What the question is about.** Output including what the accounts do not price.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Priced transactions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Adjust for quality and free goods.* Estimates of unmeasured surplus are large and, by Syverson's accounting, still short of the gap.
- *Cross-country comparison.* The slowdown appears where digital output shares differ widely, which constrains the mismeasurement account.
- *Firm-level studies.* Find large returns to IT combined with organisational change. Establishes complementarity; aggregate gap remains.
- *Lag hypotheses.* General purpose technologies diffuse over decades; the record for electricity supports a lag. Predicts eventual acceleration, which has not been observed and remains available.
- *Intangible capital measurement.* Adding intangibles raises measured output somewhat and shifts timing.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 4. Mismeasurement adjustments are estimated and fall short; the lag hypothesis remains available and unobserved.

**What has been settled so far.** The breadth of the slowdown across countries and sectors. Firm-level complementarity. Magnitudes of plausible mismeasurement adjustments.
**What would settle the rest.** A measure of output including unpriced goods, constructed on a basis agreed in advance rather than to close the gap.

## 2.69 The transformation problem

**The question.** In a theory where value derives from labour time, prices are observed to deviate systematically from labour values. How do the two relate?

**One answer given.** The transformation can be completed. Successive formulations — including single-system interpretations that value inputs and outputs at prices rather than values — preserve the aggregate equalities and remove the inconsistency the original procedure carried.

**The other answer given.** The transformation is redundant. Sraffa and later Steedman showed prices and the profit rate are determined by physical input-output data and the real wage alone, with no role for labour values in the determination; the value magnitudes do no work.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a magnitude, labour value, standing behind price; that aggregate equalities between the two systems should hold.

**What both answers require.** That labour values are determinate magnitudes and that their relation to prices is a question with an answer.

**What the question is about.** Whether one determination underlies the other.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observed prices and physical input-output tables.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Bortkiewicz and successors.* Correct the original procedure; the aggregate equalities cannot all be preserved simultaneously in the standard framework, which is proved.
- *Single-system interpretations.* Preserve the equalities by redefining the value of inputs. Their critics hold the redefinition makes the equalities definitional.
- *Sraffian critique.* Establishes that prices and profit are determined without value magnitudes. Not disputed as mathematics.
- *Empirical correlation studies.* Find high correlation between labour values and prices across sectors. Contested as an artefact of sector size, which the field has examined and not settled.
- *Reject the framework.* Marginalist alternatives determine prices differently; the dispute is then between frameworks rather than within one.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 5 and 7. Bortkiewicz proves the aggregate equalities cannot all hold; the Sraffian result determines prices without value magnitudes, which parts the accountings.

**What has been settled so far.** The impossibility of preserving all aggregate equalities in the standard framework. The Sraffian determination result. The correlation findings and the size-artefact objection.
**What would settle the rest.** A determination in which value magnitudes do work not done by the physical data.

## 2.70 Money illusion

**The question.** Do people respond to nominal amounts rather than to real purchasing power?

**One answer given.** Yes. Experimental and survey evidence shows systematic responses to nominal framing; downward nominal wage rigidity is documented and is hard to explain without it, and it is a load-bearing assumption in models that fit inflation-output dynamics.

**The other answer given.** Apparent illusion is often rational under uncertainty about relative prices, or an artefact of framing in surveys where real magnitudes are not salient. Nominal rigidity is explicable by contracting and fairness considerations without any error about real values.

**Origin conditions.** That agents have preferences over real quantities; that nominal magnitudes are transformations of them.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about which magnitude the agent responds to.

**What the question is about.** What the agent is responding to.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Choices and reports under nominal presentation, with the real magnitude inferable but not presented.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Framing experiments.* Shafir, Diamond and Tversky find robust nominal effects. Whether laboratory framing transfers to consequential decisions is the standing question.
- *Wage rigidity evidence.* Nominal cuts are rare and real cuts via inflation are accepted. Consistent with illusion and with fairness norms about nominal terms.
- *Housing and asset markets.* Nominal anchoring effects documented in transaction data. Alternative explanations by liquidity constraints are available.
- *High-inflation environments.* Illusion diminishes where inflation is salient, which both sides predict.
- *Neuroimaging.* Distinct response to nominal gains at equal real value. Establishes registration and not the decision weight.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 3. Framing effects are robust in the laboratory; wage rigidity is consistent with fairness norms as well as with error.

**What has been settled so far.** Robustness of framing effects. Rarity of nominal wage cuts. Attenuation under high inflation.
**What would settle the rest.** A consequential-stakes design distinguishing illusion from rational inattention to relative prices.

## 2.71 The two-fold cost of sex

**The question.** An asexual lineage produces twice as many reproducing offspring per parent. Sexual reproduction is nonetheless nearly universal in multicellular life. What pays the cost?

**One answer given.** Recombination clears deleterious mutations and combines beneficial ones. Muller's ratchet operates in asexual lineages, and the mutational-deterministic account requires only that mutations interact in a specified way.

**The other answer given.** The Red Queen: parasites adapt to common host genotypes, and recombination generates rare combinations. Field systems where sexual and asexual forms coexist show sexual forms favoured under high parasite pressure, which the mutational account does not predict.

**Origin conditions.** That the cost is correctly stated at two-fold; that an advantage must be located that repays it in each generation.

**What both answers require.** That the maintenance of sex is explained by a benefit exceeding the cost.

**What the question is about.** The advantage, measured against a stated two-fold cost.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Particular systems where the comparison can be made.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Snail and fish systems with mixed reproduction.* Support the parasite account in those systems, with measured parasite pressure. System-specific.
- *Experimental evolution with and without recombination.* Recombination speeds adaptation under stress in several organisms. Establishes a benefit; magnitude relative to the two-fold cost is contested.
- *Mutation accumulation experiments.* Confirm the ratchet in asexual lines. Establishes the mechanism; rates required for the deterministic account are at the edge of measured values.
- *Ancient asexual lineages.* Bdelloid rotifers were the standing counterexample; horizontal transfer and other genetic exchange were subsequently found, which weakens the counterexample and complicates the categories.
- *Theoretical pluralism.* Most current accounts combine mechanisms. Fits, and the combination's weights are not independently measured.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 4 and 8. Each mechanism is demonstrated in its own system; pluralist accounts combine them with weights not independently measured.

**What has been settled so far.** The ratchet, demonstrated. Parasite-driven advantage, demonstrated in specific field systems. Faster adaptation under recombination, demonstrated experimentally. The bdelloid case, complicated.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurement, in a natural population, of the total advantage against the two-fold cost. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the bdelloid and experimental-evolution literature moves and this route line would need checking.

## 2.72 Peto's paradox

**The question.** Cancer arises from mutations in dividing cells. Large long-lived animals have vastly more cell divisions and do not have proportionally more cancer. Why?

**One answer given.** Evolved additional suppression. Larger species carry additional tumour-suppressor mechanisms — elephants have many copies of a key suppressor gene, whales show alterations in DNA repair pathways — and the suppression scales with the risk.

**The other answer given.** The framing may overstate the paradox. Multistage models with species-specific parameters can produce the observed pattern without additional mechanisms, and cross-species cancer incidence data are sparse, biased toward captive and veterinary populations, and not standardised.

**Origin conditions.** That cancer risk is a function of cell number and division rate; that species are comparable on a standardised incidence measure.

**What both answers require.** That a cross-species incidence comparison is meaningful.

**What the question is about.** Lifetime risk across species.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Necropsy and veterinary records, unevenly sampled across species and conditions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Comparative genomics.* Identifies suppressor duplications and repair alterations in large species. Establishes mechanisms exist; their quantitative sufficiency is unmeasured.
- *Zoo and necropsy databases.* Assemble incidence across many species and confirm weak scaling with body size. Sampling bias is acknowledged in the studies themselves.
- *Multistage modelling.* Reproduces patterns under fitted parameters. Fits, and the parameters are not independently measured.
- *Cell-level experiments.* Cells from large species show different sensitivity to damage. Establishes differences; the link to whole-organism incidence is not measured.
- *Within-species scaling.* Larger individuals within a species do show higher risk, which is consistent with the framing and does not resolve the cross-species case.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 8. Comparative genomics establishes mechanisms without quantifying sufficiency; multistage models fit with parameters estimated from the same data.

**What has been settled so far.** Weak cross-species scaling, with acknowledged sampling limits. Suppressor duplications in specific lineages. Within-species scaling.
**What would settle the rest.** Standardised lifetime incidence across species, measured on comparable populations. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the comparative oncology databases have grown and this would need checking.

## 2.73 The C-value paradox

**The question.** Genome size varies more than a thousandfold among organisms of similar complexity. What determines it?

**One answer given.** Non-coding content is largely neutral. Genome size tracks the accumulation of repetitive elements, and effective population size governs how efficiently selection removes them — a mutational-hazard account with no functional role required.

**The other answer given.** Much non-coding DNA is functional. Regulatory elements, structural roles, and biochemical activity across most of the genome indicate function, and the assumption of neutrality was a placeholder rather than a finding.

**Origin conditions.** That genome content is either functional or not; that function is identifiable by an operational criterion.

**What both answers require.** That "functional" has a criterion both accept.

**What the question is about.** Whether sequence has a fitness-relevant role.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Biochemical activity, conservation, and effects of deletion — three criteria giving different answers on the same sequence.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Biochemical activity assays.* Report activity over most of the genome. Whether activity is function is the dispute, and the field debated exactly this in print.
- *Comparative conservation.* Constrains functional fraction to a much smaller figure. A different criterion, giving a different number.
- *Deletion experiments.* Large deletions of conserved non-coding regions produce viable mice in some cases. Establishes dispensability under laboratory conditions.
- *Onion test and relatives.* Species with similar biology and vastly different genome sizes. A standing challenge to functional accounts, not answered.
- *Population-genetic modelling.* Reproduces size variation from effective population size. Fits, and depends on parameters estimated from the same data.

**Closure.** Ending 6, in its clearest form. Three criteria of function — biochemical activity, conservation, deletion tolerance — return different fractions on the same sequence.

**What has been settled so far.** The activity fraction and the conservation fraction, both measured and differing greatly. Deletion tolerance in specific cases. The population-size correlation.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion of function that the field agrees on before the measurement is taken.

## 2.74 The paradox of the plankton

**The question.** Competition theory says the number of coexisting species cannot exceed the number of limiting resources. Plankton communities show dozens of species on a handful of resources. How?

**One answer given.** Equilibrium never arrives. Environmental fluctuation, mixing, and predation reset competition before exclusion completes, and the exclusion principle applies to an equilibrium the system does not reach.

**The other answer given.** The niches are not being counted correctly. Spatial structure, temporal partitioning, and species-specific predation supply more limiting factors than the resource count records, and coexistence is niche-based after all.

**Origin conditions.** That coexistence requires niche differences; that limiting factors are enumerable.

**What both answers require.** That the number of limiting factors is countable in principle.

**What the question is about.** All limiting factors operating.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Measured resource concentrations and identified predators.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Chesson's coexistence framework.* Partitions mechanisms into fluctuation-dependent and fluctuation-independent, with each measurable in principle. A major advance; measurement in natural plankton communities is partial.
- *Chaotic dynamics.* Huisman and Weissing showed competition among few resources generates chaos permitting many species. Establishes sufficiency in models; field demonstration is partial.
- *Neutral theory.* Reproduces diversity patterns with no niche differences at all. Fits abundance distributions and mispredicts other patterns.
- *Add measured factors.* Each addition raises the ceiling; whether the count converges is the open part.
- *Long-term microcosm experiments.* Sustained coexistence observed beyond the resource count.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 4 and 8. Chaotic coexistence is established in models; the continuum framing accommodates both accounts without measuring their weights.

**What has been settled so far.** Chaotic coexistence in models. Neutral theory's fit to abundance distributions and its failures elsewhere. Sustained coexistence in controlled microcosms.
**What would settle the rest.** A complete accounting of limiting factors in one natural community, measured rather than inferred from coexistence. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether Chesson's stabilising and equalising components have since been measured in a diverse natural plankton community, as against models and microcosms.

## 2.75 Sleep

**The question.** Sleep is universal among animals with nervous systems, costly, and dangerous. What is it for?

**One answer given.** Restoration and clearance. Metabolic waste clearance rises during sleep, and specific molecular and cellular repair processes are sleep-dependent; deprivation produces measurable physiological damage and, in some species, death.

**The other answer given.** Synaptic and informational function. Sleep supports memory consolidation and synaptic downscaling, with replay of waking activity documented; the informational account explains why brain activity is patterned rather than merely reduced, which restoration alone does not.

**Origin conditions.** That sleep has a function; that identifying its primary function is the question.

**What both answers require.** That there is a primary function to be identified.

**What the question is about.** The contribution of sleep, separable from the state that carries it.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Correlates and effects of deprivation, both of which the two accounts predict.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Deprivation studies.* Produce cognitive and physiological deficits together. Both accounts predict this.
- *Glymphatic clearance measurement.* Clearance rises in sleep in rodents. The magnitude and the technique have been disputed in subsequent work.
- *Replay and consolidation studies.* Documented in hippocampus and cortex, with targeted reactivation improving retention. Establishes an informational role; whether primary is not settled.
- *Comparative studies.* Sleep duration and architecture vary widely with ecology and phylogeny. Constrains single-function accounts.
- *Unihemispheric and reduced sleep species.* Show the requirement is modifiable, and the residual requirement is not zero.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 6. Replay and clearance findings each establish a role; the clearance results have been contested in technique, which is the instruments in dispute.

**What has been settled so far.** Deprivation effects. Replay and targeted reactivation. Comparative variation. Clearance findings and the disputes about them.
**What would settle the rest.** An intervention removing one candidate function while leaving the other intact, with sleep need measured. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the clearance literature has been actively contested and would need checking.

## 2.76 The binding problem

**The question.** Different features of an object are processed in separate brain regions. Experience presents one object. What binds them?

**One answer given.** Temporal synchrony. Neurons responding to features of one object fire in synchrony while those responding to different objects do not, which supplies a tag that is available without dedicated conjunction cells and scales combinatorially where convergence does not. Gamma-band coherence is the documented signature, it appears across species and tasks, and it tracks perceptual grouping in the predicted direction.

**The other answer given.** Synchrony is a correlate, and no mechanism reading it has been identified. Hierarchical convergence onto neurons responsive to conjunctions, plus attention, accounts for the phenomena without a separate binding process, and the problem may be posed by an assumption about how experience must be constructed.

**Origin conditions.** That features are represented separately; that unified experience requires a process combining them.

**What both answers require.** That the unity of the object in experience corresponds to a combining operation.

**What the question is about.** What makes the features one object in experience.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Neural activity and behavioural report.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Gamma synchrony studies.* Documented across species and tasks. Whether it is causal or a consequence of shared drive is disputed, and the disputes are in print on both sides.
- *Illusory conjunction studies.* Show binding failures under attentional load. Establishes attention's role; not a mechanism.
- *Convergence and conjunction cells.* Found in higher visual areas. Handles some conjunctions; combinatorial coverage is the objection.
- *Optogenetic manipulation of synchrony.* Produces behavioural effects in specific paradigms. Strongest causal route; scope so far is narrow.
- *Dissolve the problem.* Argue unity is a feature of report rather than a construction requiring a process. Reframes; the phenomena remain to be accounted.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Synchrony is documented and the causal-versus-consequence question is where the field's analyses disagree.

**What has been settled so far.** Synchrony's presence. Illusory conjunctions under load. Conjunction-selective cells. Specific optogenetic effects.
**What would settle the rest.** A demonstrated read-out mechanism sensitive to synchrony, or a convergence account covering the combinatorial range. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the scope of optogenetic synchrony manipulation beyond the single paradigms cited, and the current state of the causal-versus-epiphenomenal dispute over gamma coherence.

## 2.77 Other minds

**The question.** I know my own experience directly. I infer everyone else's from behaviour. What justifies the inference?

**One answer given.** Inference to the best explanation. Other bodies resemble mine, behave as mine does, and share my physiology; experience in them is by far the best explanation of the behaviour, and the inference is as good as any in science.

**The other answer given.** The inference is from a single case, which is the weakest form of induction there is, and no independent check on it is available. Alternatives — that experience is a way of describing behaviour, or that the demand for justification is malformed — have been developed precisely because the argument from analogy is regarded as insufficient.

**Origin conditions.** That experience is directly known in one's own case and not in others'; that the asymmetry is a fact about access rather than about the concepts.

**What both answers require.** That there is something in others whose presence the inference is about.

**What the question is about.** Experience in another.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Behaviour and physiology.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Argument from analogy.* One case, no check. Its weakness is acknowledged by its defenders.
- *Criterial accounts.* Wittgenstein and successors: behaviour in context is a criterion rather than evidence. Removes the inference and is contested as changing the question.
- *Inference to the best explanation.* Depends on the explanatory framework already admitting experience as an explanans, which is what is in question.
- *Neurophysiological similarity.* Establishes similarity, which is the analogy premise measured.
- *Deny the asymmetry.* Some accounts hold self-knowledge is also inferential. Levels the two cases and does not supply the missing check.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Criterial accounts remove the inference and are contested as changing the question; one-case induction is formally weak by a result both sides accept.

**What has been settled so far.** The physiological similarity, measured. The formal weakness of one-case induction. The scope of criterial accounts and their standing objections.
**What would settle the rest.** An independent check on the presence of experience in another.

## 2.78 Symbol grounding

**The question.** A system manipulates symbols by rules. How do its symbols come to be about anything?

**One answer given.** By causal connection to the world. Grounding is achieved through sensorimotor interaction: symbols acquired through perception and action are connected to what they denote, and systems built this way are not merely manipulating shapes.

**The other answer given.** Causal connection underdetermines reference. Any causal chain admits multiple assignments of what the symbol is about, and the indeterminacy arguments have not been answered; adding sensors relocates the question to what the sensor states are about.

**Origin conditions.** That symbols have determinate content; that content is fixed by a relation between the system and the world.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about what a symbol is about.

**What the question is about.** The content relation.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The system's behaviour and its causal history.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Robotic grounding.* Builds systems whose categories arise from interaction. Establishes categories are acquirable; whether they are about anything is the original question.
- *Causal and informational semantics.* Fodor's asymmetric dependence, Dretske's indicator theory. Each faces the disjunction problem and misrepresentation cases, documented and unresolved.
- *Teleosemantics.* Fixes content by selection history. Faces indeterminacy of the selected function, which Fodor pressed and which remains contested.
- *Chinese room argument.* Holds syntax insufficient for semantics. The systems reply and the robot reply are standing responses; the exchange has not converged.
- *Large language models.* Acquire behaviour previously thought to require grounding. What this shows about content is actively disputed and the dispute is live.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 5 and 8. Indicator theories face the proved disjunction problem; teleosemantics absorbs it and inherits indeterminacy of the selected function.

**What has been settled so far.** The disjunction problem for indicator theories. The scope of robotic category acquisition. The persistence of the Chinese room exchange without convergence.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion determining what a symbol is about, applicable to a system whose causal history is fully known. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current dispute about language models is moving and would need checking.

## 2.79 Interpretability of built systems

**The question.** A trained network produces outputs. Can its computation be explained in terms a person can check?

**One answer given.** Yes, increasingly. Circuits have been identified, features localised, and specific behaviours traced to specific components; sparse decomposition methods extract features that are more interpretable than raw units, and the field's methods improve steadily.

**The other answer given.** Explanations obtained so far cover a small fraction of computation, and the standard for success is not agreed. Explanations that predict behaviour under intervention are rare; many are post hoc narratives fitting observed activity, and the field's own evaluations find explanations that appear plausible and fail causal tests.

**Origin conditions.** That the computation decomposes into components with stateable roles; that a person-checkable explanation exists.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about what a component does, independent of the explanation offered.

**What the question is about.** What the system's computation is.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Activations, ablations, and behaviour under intervention.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Feature visualisation and probing.* Produces plausible descriptions. Probes can recover information the system does not use, which the field established and now controls for.
- *Causal ablation and patching.* Stronger, and results are component-specific and do not compose into whole-model accounts.
- *Sparse dictionary methods.* Extract more interpretable features than raw units. Coverage of total computation remains partial and is measured.
- *Formal verification.* Available for small networks and specific properties. Scales poorly, which is the known limit.
- *Behavioural evaluation.* Establishes what the system does, not how.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 4 and 5. Probing was shown by the field to recover information the system does not use — a proved limitation on its own instrument.

**What has been settled so far.** That probing can recover unused information. Specific verified circuits. Measured coverage figures for decomposition methods.
**What would settle the rest.** An explanation of a full model's computation that predicts behaviour under novel intervention. **nye — not yet explored fully:** this field moves quickly and the route record here would need checking.

## 2.80 Machine and animal sentience

**The question.** Which systems have experience, and how would one tell?

**One answer given.** By identifying the mechanisms that produce it in the case we know. Behavioural and neural markers — nociception with motivational trade-offs, flexible learning, integration — provide graded evidence, and precautionary frameworks are built on them for animals.

**The other answer given.** The markers are selected by resemblance to us and have no independent validation. A system engineered to display them displays them; an organism with different architecture may have experience without them. No test distinguishes presence from absence.

**Origin conditions.** That experience is present or absent in a system; that markers correlate with it.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about presence, and that evidence bears on it.

**What the question is about.** Presence, indexed by markers with no independent validation.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Behaviour, architecture, and physiology.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Marker frameworks.* Applied to invertebrates with legislative consequence in several jurisdictions. Establishes a graded evidential practice; the validation problem is stated within the frameworks themselves.
- *Comparative neuroanatomy.* Finds candidate structures in distant lineages. Establishes analogous organisation; the inference to experience is the original question.
- *Behavioural trade-off tests.* Motivational trade-offs against noxious stimuli, demonstrated in decapods and insects. The strongest behavioural evidence; engineered systems can produce the same pattern.
- *Theory-driven tests.* Apply a theory of consciousness and read off a verdict. The theory is what is in dispute.
- *Deny determinacy.* Some hold there is no fact in marginal cases. Contested, and consequential for practice.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Marker frameworks lack independent validation, stated by their own authors, which is a limitation on the method.

**What has been settled so far.** Trade-off behaviour in specific taxa. Comparative anatomy. That marker frameworks lack independent validation, stated by their authors.
**What would settle the rest.** A marker validated against a case where presence is known independently, which the construction of the problem does not supply.

## 2.81 Credit assignment

**The question.** An outcome follows a long sequence of actions. Which actions produced it?

**One answer given.** Statistical assignment works. Temporal difference methods propagate value backward without a model, eligibility traces distribute credit across a decaying window, and backpropagation through time assigns it exactly in differentiable settings. These are the basis of systems that learn tasks with horizons of thousands of steps from sparse reward, which is a demonstrated result rather than a proposal, and the convergence properties of the methods are proved for stated conditions.

**The other answer given.** These assign credit relative to a model and a reward specification, both supplied. Where the sequence is long, the signal sparse, and the environment non-stationary, the assignment is unidentifiable in principle — many action sequences are consistent with the same outcome — and the methods succeed by imposing structure rather than by recovering it.

**Origin conditions.** That an outcome is produced by identifiable contributions; that a reward signal is specified.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about which actions contributed.

**What the question is about.** Contribution of individual actions.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Outcomes, and counterfactual rollouts within a model.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Temporal difference learning.* Works, with performance degrading as horizon and sparsity grow, in a measured way.
- *Counterfactual estimation.* Requires a model or the ability to re-run, which real settings often deny.
- *Causal identification.* Formal conditions for identifying effects from observational data are known and are frequently unmet in sequential settings.
- *Reward shaping.* Improves learning and changes what is credited, which is exactly the concern.
- *Hierarchical decomposition.* Assigns credit at multiple timescales. The decomposition is supplied.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. Formal identification conditions are proved and are frequently unmet in sequential settings.

**What has been settled so far.** Performance scaling with horizon and sparsity, measured. The identification conditions, proved. The effect of shaping.
**What would settle the rest.** An assignment recovering contributions without a supplied model or decomposition.

## 2.82 Generalization in learning

**The question.** A model fits its training data and performs well on new data. Classical theory says a model with more parameters than data should not. Why does it?

**One answer given.** Implicit regularisation. Optimisation dynamics select solutions with properties that generalise, and the double-descent curve shows test error falling again past the interpolation threshold; the classical bound was about a different quantity than the one that governs.

**The other answer given.** No current theory predicts the observed generalisation in advance. Uniform-convergence bounds have been shown insufficient for these regimes by explicit construction, the proposed measures fail to rank models correctly in large-scale empirical comparisons, and the explanations are fitted after the fact.

**Origin conditions.** That generalisation is governed by a complexity measure of the model class; that a bound stated before training predicts performance.

**What both answers require.** That there is a property of the trained model determining its generalisation.

**What the question is about.** Performance on data not drawn yet.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Held-out samples from the same distribution.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Uniform convergence bounds.* Shown insufficient for interpolating regimes by explicit construction, in the field's own literature.
- *Margin and norm-based measures.* Large-scale comparisons find they do not rank models by generalisation reliably; this was measured across thousands of models.
- *Implicit bias analysis.* Proved for specific architectures and losses. Coverage of practical settings is partial.
- *PAC-Bayes bounds.* Non-vacuous bounds achieved in specific settings. A genuine advance; tightness in general settings remains open.
- *Empirical scaling laws.* Predict performance accurately as a function of scale. Predictive without explanatory content, which the field states.

**Closure.** Ending 5 throughout. Uniform convergence was proved insufficient by explicit construction, and proposed complexity measures were measured not to rank models.

**What has been settled so far.** The insufficiency of uniform convergence in these regimes. The empirical failure of proposed complexity measures to rank. Non-vacuous PAC-Bayes bounds in specific settings. Scaling laws.
**What would settle the rest.** A measure computable before deployment that ranks models by generalisation across settings.

## 2.83 Anesthesia

**The question.** Chemically diverse molecules all abolish consciousness. What do they have in common?

**One answer given.** Convergent network effects. The agents act on different receptors and converge on one outcome: disruption of long-range cortical communication and of thalamocortical integration. The convergence is measured directly across chemically unrelated agents, appears at the concentrations where responsiveness is lost, and reverses in the same order as responsiveness returns, which is what a common final pathway predicts.

**The other answer given.** Distinct agents produce distinct signatures. Propofol, ketamine and dexmedetomidine differ in their spectral and connectivity effects, and treating them as one phenomenon obscures the differences; there may be several routes to unresponsiveness rather than one mechanism.

**Origin conditions.** That loss of consciousness is a single state; that unresponsiveness indexes it.

**What both answers require.** That there is one state the agents produce.

**What the question is about.** Consciousness, indexed by unresponsiveness.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Responsiveness, and neural correlates.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Common molecular target search.* No single target found; agents act on different receptors, established.
- *Network-level measurement.* Finds convergent disruption of integration. Also finds agent-specific signatures, which is the dispute.
- *Perturbational complexity index.* Distinguishes conscious from unconscious states across conditions with high accuracy, and is calibrated on the cases in question.
- *Disconnected consciousness.* Patients report experience without responsiveness after emergence, which shows the index is not responsiveness.
- *Reversal agents and arousal circuits.* Specific circuits restore responsiveness. Establishes control of arousal, distinguished from consciousness in the same literature.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. No common molecular target exists; convergent disruption and agent-specific signatures are both measured, which is the disagreement.

**What has been settled so far.** Absence of a common molecular target. Convergent network disruption alongside agent-specific signatures. Complexity index performance. Dissociation of responsiveness from experience.
**What would settle the rest.** A measure of experience independent of responsiveness and of report. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the perturbational complexity index's performance on cases outside its calibration set, and whether agent-specific signatures have since been reconciled with the convergence account.

## 2.84 Phantom limb

**The question.** People feel a limb that is not there, often painfully. What produces the sensation?

**One answer given.** Cortical reorganisation. After amputation, adjacent representations invade the deafferented region, and the degree of reorganisation correlates with pain in imaging studies; therapies aimed at reversing it have effects.

**The other answer given.** The representation is preserved rather than reorganised. Later imaging with individuated finger stimulation in amputees found the missing limb's map largely intact and correlated with vivid phantom sensation, which reverses the direction of the original account.

**Origin conditions.** That the sensation is produced by an identifiable neural state; that imaging measures the relevant organisation.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about whether the representation is reorganised or preserved.

**What the question is about.** The map's state and its relation to the sensation.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Imaging at a resolution and paradigm that has changed between the two literatures.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Early mapping studies.* Found shifted representations correlating with pain. Established the original account.
- *Individuated-digit imaging in amputees.* Found preserved maps. Directly contests the earlier finding, and the field has not fully reconciled them.
- *Mirror therapy.* Produces relief in some patients; controlled trials give mixed results and the mechanism is disputed.
- *Peripheral interventions.* Neuroma treatment and targeted reinnervation help some patients, supporting a peripheral contribution.
- *Congenital absence cases.* Phantoms reported without any prior limb, which constrains accounts requiring a lost representation.

**Closure.** Ending 6, in its clearest form. Two imaging paradigms on the same phenomenon returned opposite directions, and the field has not reconciled them.

**What has been settled so far.** Both imaging results, in their own paradigms. The mixed trial record for mirror therapy. Phantoms in congenital absence.
**What would settle the rest.** A reconciliation of the two imaging paradigms on the same subjects. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether the preserved-map and reorganisation findings have since been tested on one cohort with both paradigms, and the current status of mirror therapy trial evidence.

## 2.85 The problem of the criterion

**The question.** To know which beliefs are knowledge, one needs a criterion. To know a criterion is correct, one needs to know which beliefs are knowledge. Where does one start?

**One answer given.** With particular cases. Particularism starts from clear instances of knowledge and derives the criterion from them, which is how every inquiry actually proceeds.

**The other answer given.** With the criterion. Methodism starts from a standard and sorts cases by it, on the ground that calling instances clear already applies an unstated standard. Each starting point assumes what the other requires, and Chisholm posed the two as exhaustive.

**Origin conditions.** That knowledge has an extension and a criterion, and that one determines the other.

**What both answers require.** That there is a correct criterion and a correct extension, matching.

**What the question is about.** The relation between criterion and extension.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Judgments about cases, each already reflecting a standard.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Particularism.* Chisholm's own choice. Its critics note that selecting the clear cases is where the criterion enters unannounced.
- *Methodism.* Descartes's route. The criterion's warrant is the standing objection, and Cartesian attempts to secure it are widely held to fail.
- *Reflective equilibrium.* Adjusts both until they cohere. Accounts for practice; whether coherence between two things each adjusted to the other is warrant is the same circle, which its proponents address and critics do not accept as answered.
- *Externalist reliabilism.* Makes the criterion a matter of fact about reliability, not something one must know. Shifts the demand; the generality problem — which process type a belief instantiates — is its own open problem.
- *Skeptical conclusion.* Neither can start, so neither is available. Held by few and not refuted.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Reflective equilibrium adjusts both to each other, which its critics hold is the circle restated; reliabilism shifts the demand and inherits the generality problem.

**What has been settled so far.** That the two starting points each presuppose the other, which both sides accept. The generality problem for reliabilism, documented and open.
**What would settle the rest.** A starting point that is neither a case-judgment nor a criterion.

## 2.86 Skepticism about the external world

**The question.** Experience would be the same whether it is caused by an external world or not. What justifies belief in the world?

**One answer given.** Inference to the best explanation. An external world explains the coherence, involuntariness and predictability of experience far better than any rival, and skeptical scenarios are constructed to have no explanatory content at all.

**The other answer given.** The rivals are constructed to be empirically equivalent, so no evidence favours one, and best-explanation reasoning applied here presupposes standards of explanation the skeptic is asking about. The point is old and no proposed refutation has been generally accepted.

**Origin conditions.** That experience is one thing and its cause another; that justification runs from the first to the second.

**What both answers require.** That belief in the world stands in need of justification of some kind.

**What the question is about.** The cause of experience.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Experience.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Cartesian proof.* Requires a guarantor, whose establishment is generally held to fail.
- *Moore's proof.* Exhibits a hand. Its critics hold it begs the question; its defenders hold the demand is the mistake. Unresolved and long-standing.
- *Transcendental arguments.* Kant and successors: the skeptical hypothesis is unstatable without presupposing what it doubts. Stroud's objection — that they establish at most how we must think — has not been generally answered.
- *Semantic externalism.* Putnam's argument that a brain in a vat cannot truly say it is one. Widely discussed, and widely held not to reach the epistemic question.
- *Contextualism.* Skeptical standards apply only in skeptical contexts. Accounts for the pattern of judgments; whether it answers the argument is contested.
- *Reject the demand.* Various forms; each faces the charge of changing the subject.

**Closure.** Endings 2, 3 and 8. Skeptical scenarios are empirically equivalent by construction; transcendental arguments establish at most how one must think, by Stroud's objection.

**What has been settled so far.** The empirical equivalence of the scenarios, by construction. Stroud's objection to transcendental arguments. The scope of the semantic argument.
**What would settle the rest.** A justification not itself drawn from experience.

## 2.87 Substance and bundle

**The question.** An object has properties. Is there something that has them, or is the object just the properties together?

**One answer given.** Something has them. Properties are had by something, and without a bearer there is no account of what unifies a bundle, nor of how an object persists through property change.

**The other answer given.** Nothing further is needed. The bearer is characterised only as that which has properties, so it has no nature of its own and does no work; bundles unified by compresence do the same job without positing a featureless item.

**Origin conditions.** That an object decomposes into a bearer and properties, or into properties alone; that one of these is correct.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about the constitution of an object.

**What the question is about.** Whether a bearer does work the properties do not.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Properties, measured.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Bare substratum.* Posits a bearer with no properties of its own. Its critics hold an item with no nature is unintelligible; its defenders accept the characterisation as the point.
- *Bundle theory.* Faces the identity of indiscernibles: two exactly similar objects would be one. Responses invoke tropes or spatiotemporal relations, each with costs.
- *Trope theory.* Properties as particulars, bundled. Solves the indiscernibles case and faces questions about what bundles them, which is the original question.
- *Appeal to physics.* Field values at points, or structures without relata. Read as supporting bundles by some and as presupposing a bearer by others.
- *Deflate the question.* Hold it terminological. Its critics note the two accounts give different verdicts in indiscernibles cases, so something turns on it.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Trope theory solves the indiscernibles case and inherits the bundling question; deflation is contested because the accounts differ in verdict.

**What has been settled so far.** The indiscernibles objection and the trope response. That the substratum has no characterisation beyond its role.
**What would settle the rest.** A discriminating case both sides accept as decisive.

## 2.88 Why there is something

**The question.** Why is there something rather than nothing?

**One answer given.** The question is malformed. Explanation relates states of affairs, and there is no prior state from which the existence of anything could be explained. Nothing is not a possible alternative that failed to obtain.

**The other answer given.** The question is intelligible and unanswered. Physical accounts of a universe arising from a quantum vacuum explain the arising from something — a vacuum with laws is not nothing — and relocate the question rather than closing it, which the physicists who give such accounts have been told repeatedly by philosophers and sometimes accept.

**Origin conditions.** That existence admits of explanation; that nothingness is a coherent alternative.

**What both answers require.** That the question either has an answer or is demonstrably malformed.

**What the question is about.** The obtaining of anything at all.
**What the field's instruments reach.** What obtains.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Necessary being.* A being that could not fail to exist. The modal status is what is in question, and ontological arguments for it are widely held to fail.
- *Quantum vacuum origination.* Explains a universe from a vacuum state and laws, which are something. Both sides agree on the physics and disagree on what it answers.
- *Brute fact.* Existence is unexplained. Consistent, and its critics hold it concedes the question.
- *Principle of sufficient reason.* If accepted, requires an answer; its acceptance is the dispute, and unrestricted versions have known costs.
- *Deflation.* Hold nothingness incoherent. Its critics find the notion coherent enough to state.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. Quantum origination explains arising from a structured state, which both sides accept; brute fact absorbs the question at an acknowledged cost.

**What has been settled so far.** That quantum-origination accounts start from a structured state, which their authors and critics both acknowledge. The costs of unrestricted sufficient reason.
**What would settle the rest.** An explanation whose starting point is not itself something.

## 2.89 The simulation hypothesis

**The question.** Could what we observe be a computation run at another level of reality, and could we tell?

**One answer given.** The statistical argument stands. If civilisations run many high-fidelity ancestor simulations, simulated observers vastly outnumber unsimulated ones, so one of three disjuncts holds and the hypothesis is live.

**The other answer given.** The argument requires an indifference principle over observers and an assumption that consciousness is substrate-independent, both of which are assumed rather than established; and the hypothesis makes no differential prediction, so it is not a scientific claim in the ordinary sense.

**Origin conditions.** That observers can be counted across levels; that experience is substrate-independent; that a simulating level is possible.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about the level one is at.

**What the question is about.** A level not accessible from within.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observations within the level.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Statistical argument.* Yields a disjunction, not a conclusion, which its author states explicitly.
- *Search for computational artefacts.* Proposed tests for lattice signatures in cosmic ray spectra. Null so far, and they test one implementation among many.
- *Computational cost arguments.* Argue full simulation is infeasible. Assume the simulating level's physics resembles ours, which the hypothesis does not require.
- *Anthropic and indifference reasoning.* Constrains inference; the indifference principle over observers is itself contested with known paradoxes.
- *Unfalsifiability objection.* Places it outside empirical inquiry. Its proponents accept the disjunctive form is not a prediction.

**Closure.** Endings 2, 3 and 4. The statistical argument yields a disjunction by its author's statement; proposed lattice signatures test one implementation and are null.

**What has been settled so far.** The disjunctive structure of the argument. Null results for the proposed lattice signatures. Known paradoxes in observer-indifference reasoning.
**What would settle the rest.** A prediction differing between levels.

## 2.90 Laws of nature

**The question.** Are laws prescriptive — governing what happens — or descriptive summaries of what happens?

**One answer given.** Descriptive. The best-system account: laws are the axioms of the simplest, strongest true systematisation of the facts. Nothing governs; regularity is all there is, and this avoids positing a modal relation nobody has explained.

**The other answer given.** Prescriptive. Regularities alone cannot distinguish laws from accidental generalisations, cannot support counterfactuals, and cannot explain why the regularity holds. A relation of necessitation between universals, or a primitive modal fact, is required.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a fact distinguishing laws from accidents; that laws either govern or summarise.

**What both answers require.** That the distinction between law and accident is real.

**What the question is about.** The modal status of a regularity.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The regularities themselves.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Best-system analysis.* Yields the distinction from simplicity and strength. The measures are language-relative, which is the standing objection, addressed by appeal to natural properties, which is a primitive.
- *Necessitation between universals.* Armstrong. Van Fraassen's identification problem — what makes the relation necessitate — has not been answered to general satisfaction.
- *Primitivism about laws.* Takes governing as basic. Consistent, and explains nothing further, which its proponents accept.
- *Anti-realism about laws.* Van Fraassen: no laws, only models that fit. Accommodates practice and is contested on the counterfactual role.
- *Look to physics.* Symmetry principles are treated as more fundamental than laws in practice. Both accounts accommodate this.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 5 and 8. Best-system simplicity is language-relative, addressed by appeal to a primitive; van Fraassen's identification problem is unanswered.

**What has been settled so far.** The language-relativity of simplicity. The identification problem. That physics treats symmetries as prior to individual laws.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion distinguishing law from accident that neither presupposes natural properties nor takes governing as primitive.

## 2.91 Presentism and eternalism

**The question.** Do past and future events exist, or only present ones?

**One answer given.** Only the present. What is past no longer exists and what is future does not yet; the tensed character of thought and language reflects a real feature of the world.

**The other answer given.** All times exist. Relativity gives no privileged present, and McTaggart's argument shows the tensed series is incoherent as an objective feature: an event must be past, present and future, and the attempt to relativise these generates a regress.

**Origin conditions.** That existence is a matter of fact not relative to a time; that the tensed and tenseless descriptions are rivals.

**What both answers require.** That one of the two descriptions is correct.

**What the question is about.** What exists, unqualified.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observations, all made at a time.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *McTaggart's argument.* Widely regarded as showing a difficulty for objective tense; presentists respond by taking tense as primitive, and the exchange is old and unresolved.
- *Relativity.* Excludes a frame-independent present. Presentists respond with a preferred foliation undetectable in principle, which is consistent and unfalsifiable.
- *Truthmaker objection.* Asks what makes past-tensed statements true if the past does not exist. Presentists answer with present traces or primitive tensed facts; each response is contested.
- *Growing block.* Past and present exist, future does not. Faces the objection that its inhabitants could not know they are at the edge.
- *Linguistic analysis of tense.* Establishes how tense functions; both sides accept the semantics and dispute the metaphysics.

**Closure.** Endings 2, 3 and 8. A preferred foliation is consistent and undetectable in principle; both accounts accept the linguistic semantics and dispute the metaphysics.

**What has been settled so far.** The relativistic result. McTaggart's argument and the standard responses. The truthmaker difficulty and its proposed answers.
**What would settle the rest.** A discriminating observation, which the empirical equivalence of the two accounts excludes.

## 2.92 The effectiveness of mathematics

**The question.** Mathematics developed for its own reasons turns out to describe physical phenomena precisely. Why?

**One answer given.** Selection and construction. Mathematics is developed in continual contact with physical problems, the branches that apply were often built for application, and the successes are salient while the vast unapplied remainder is not counted. There is no mystery, only a selection effect.

**The other answer given.** The selection account does not cover the striking cases. Non-Euclidean geometry, group theory and complex analysis were developed with no physical application in view and later proved indispensable, and predictive success far beyond the data used to construct the theory is not explained by contact or selection.

**Origin conditions.** That mathematics and the physical world are two domains; that applicability is a relation between them calling for explanation.

**What both answers require.** That the applicability is a phenomenon with an explanation.

**What the question is about.** Why the relation obtains.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Instances of application, and the historical record of their development.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Selection effect accounting.* Explains many cases. Both sides dispute whether it covers the striking ones, and the dispute is over the historical record, which is available and read differently.
- *Structural realism.* The world has mathematical structure and theories capture it. Explains applicability by making it constitutive; what a structure without relata is remains contested.
- *Mathematical naturalism.* Mathematics is abstracted from physical structure. Faces the branches with no physical origin.
- *Platonism.* Mathematics describes an independent realm the world instantiates. The access problem — how physical beings know about abstract objects — is its standing difficulty.
- *Deflation.* Hold the question rhetorical. Its critics hold the unreasonable cases are specific and documented.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Both sides read the same historical record differently; platonism absorbs the applicability and inherits the access problem.

**What has been settled so far.** The historical record for the striking cases, which both sides accept as a record. The access problem for platonism.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion for when applicability calls for explanation, agreed before the cases are examined.

## 2.93 Goldbach's conjecture

**The question.** Is every even number greater than two the sum of two primes?

**One answer given.** Yes. Verified past 4 × 10¹⁸, heuristic density arguments predict the number of representations grows rather than shrinks, and near-results are strong: every sufficiently large odd number is a sum of three primes, proved unconditionally.

**The other answer given.** No proof exists, and the available methods have a known limit. The circle method handles three primes and does not handle two; sieve methods reach every even number as a prime plus a product of at most two primes, and the last step is not a matter of refinement.

**Origin conditions.** That the additive and multiplicative structures of the integers relate in a way a proof could exhibit.

**What both answers require.** That the statement is true or false of all even numbers.

**What the question is about.** All even numbers.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Verification to a bound, and asymptotic methods with stated limits.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Computation.* Verified to 4 × 10¹⁸. Ends by measuring more of what was reached.
- *Circle method.* Proves the ternary case, by Helfgott completing Vinogradov's programme. The binary case is beyond the method's reach for reasons the method itself makes precise.
- *Sieve methods.* Chen's theorem: every sufficiently large even number is a prime plus a number with at most two prime factors. The parity problem in sieve theory is a known and stated obstruction to reaching two primes.
- *Density and heuristic arguments.* Predict many representations. Not proofs, and known to be unable to become proofs by refinement.
- *Assume the Riemann hypothesis.* Improves bounds and does not close the binary case.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. The parity problem in sieve theory is a proved limitation stopping this entry and entry 2.94 at the same place.

**What has been settled so far.** Verification bound. The ternary theorem. Chen's theorem. The parity obstruction, which is itself a proved limitation on the method, and which stops entry 2.94 at the same place.
**What would settle the rest.** A method not subject to the parity obstruction.

## 2.94 Twin primes

**The question.** Are there infinitely many pairs of primes differing by two?

**One answer given.** Yes. The Hardy-Littlewood conjecture gives an explicit asymptotic density for twin pairs, derived from a heuristic in which primes behave randomly subject to their known congruence constraints, and it matches computation closely over every range checked. The same heuristic predicts densities for other prime constellations, and those predictions have matched wherever they can be tested.

**The other answer given.** No proof exists, and the same parity obstruction blocks the route. Zhang's breakthrough and the Polymath refinements established bounded gaps — infinitely many pairs differing by at most 246 — and the reduction from 246 to 2 is stated in the literature as blocked by parity rather than by effort.

**Origin conditions.** That prime distribution is governed by a density, and that a density argument can establish infinitude of a specific gap.

**What both answers require.** That the statement is true or false of the integers.

**What the question is about.** All primes.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Sieve methods with a proved obstruction, and computation to a bound.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Computation.* Very large twin pairs found; no bearing on infinitude.
- *Bounded gaps.* Zhang, then Maynard and Tao, then Polymath, reaching 246. A major advance with a stated floor: 6 under the Elliott-Halberstam conjecture, and 2 not reachable by these methods.
- *Parity problem.* Selberg's obstruction, proved: sieve methods cannot distinguish numbers with an even number of prime factors from an odd number. This is a proved limitation on the method.
- *Brun's theorem.* The sum of reciprocals of twin primes converges, which is consistent with finitely or infinitely many.
- *Random models of the primes.* Predict the density accurately and are models.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. Bounded gaps reach 246 with a stated floor, and the parity obstruction is the same proved limitation that stops entry 2.93.

**What has been settled so far.** Bounded gaps at 246, and 6 conditionally. Brun's theorem. The parity obstruction, proved, and the same obstruction that stops entry 2.93.
**What would settle the rest.** A method not subject to the parity obstruction.

## 2.95 The Gibbs paradox

**The question.** Mixing two different gases raises entropy. Mixing a gas with itself does not. The change is discontinuous as the gases are made more alike. Why?

**One answer given.** Indistinguishability is fundamental. Identical particles are not merely similar but the same kind, and the correct counting divides by the permutations; quantum mechanics supplies this and the discontinuity is real because identity is not a matter of degree.

**The other answer given.** The entropy of mixing depends on what can be separated by available operations, so it is relative to the observer's capacity to discriminate. Jaynes argued the paradox arises from treating entropy as a property of the system alone rather than of the description, and the discontinuity is a discontinuity in the description.

**Origin conditions.** That entropy is a property of a system; that particle identity is determinate.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about the entropy change on mixing.

**What the question is about.** Whether entropy is a property of the system or of the description.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Measurable work extractable given available operations.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Quantum indistinguishability.* Supplies the correct counting and the discontinuity. Its critics note classical statistical mechanics needed the division before quantum theory supplied it.
- *Jaynes's information-theoretic treatment.* Makes the entropy relative to available operations. Reproduces every measurement, and the disagreement is over whether entropy is thereby subjective.
- *Isotope experiments.* Separable isotopes give mixing entropy; the separation capacity is what changed historically, which supports the operational reading.
- *Continuous interpolation.* Constructing particles continuously interpolating between two species is not available for real particles, so the discontinuity is not directly probed.
- *Resource-theoretic thermodynamics.* Makes the operation set explicit and derives entropy relative to it. A formal advance that states the relativity rather than removing it.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Jaynes's treatment reproduces every measurement and relocates entropy to the description; the resource-theoretic formulation states the relativity rather than removing it.

**What has been settled so far.** The correct counting. The isotope cases. The resource-theoretic formulation.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurement distinguishing entropy as a system property from entropy relative to an operation set.

## 2.96 The surprise examination

**The question.** An examination will be held next week and its day will be a surprise. The students argue it cannot happen. It happens, and is a surprise. Where is the error?

**One answer given.** The backward induction is invalid. The announcement's truth conditions involve the students' knowledge, and after the argument they no longer know the exam will occur, so the eliminating step fails at the first move.

**The other answer given.** No consensus account exists. Epistemic, temporal-retention, and self-referential analyses each block the argument at a different step, each has counterexamples in the literature, and the paradox has been reformulated repeatedly to evade each solution.

**Origin conditions.** That the announcement is known to be true; that knowledge persists through the reasoning; that surprise is definable in terms of what is known.

**What both answers require.** That there is a determinate error in the argument.

**What the question is about.** The status of a self-referential knowledge claim.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Formal reconstructions, each with its own assumptions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Epistemic blocking.* The announcement is not knowable in the relevant sense. Formalised, and the reformulations restore the puzzle with weaker assumptions.
- *Temporal retention analysis.* Knowledge is not retained through the eliminations. Blocks the induction; whether the failure is principled is disputed.
- *Self-reference analysis.* Treats the announcement as a Moore-type sentence. Illuminating and does not settle which step fails.
- *Game-theoretic treatment.* Models it as a strategic interaction with mixed strategies. Changes the problem, which its authors note.
- *Deny the phenomenon.* Hold that the surprise is not the announced one. Contested.

**Closure.** Ending 8, throughout. Each proposed solution has been met by a reformulation restoring the puzzle, documented over seventy years.

**What has been settled so far.** That each proposed solution has generated a reformulation restoring the puzzle, documented over seventy years.
**What would settle the rest.** An analysis surviving reformulation.

## 2.97 Determinism and randomness

**The question.** Are outcomes fixed by prior conditions, or genuinely chancy?

**One answer given.** Chancy. Quantum measurement outcomes are unpredictable in principle, not merely in practice, and no local account restoring determinism reproduces the correlations.

**The other answer given.** Determinism remains available. De Broglie–Bohm is deterministic and reproduces every prediction; many-worlds is deterministic at the level of the state. Apparent randomness is compatible with underlying determinism, and no measurement distinguishes them.

**Origin conditions.** That an outcome is either fixed by prior conditions or not; that unpredictability indicates chance.

**What both answers require.** That there is a fact about whether the outcome was fixed.

**What the question is about.** Whether prior conditions fix outcomes.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observed frequencies, which both accounts reproduce.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Statistical tests for randomness.* Confirm the distributions and cannot distinguish deterministic chaos or hidden determination from chance, which is a general result about such tests.
- *Bell tests.* Exclude local determination. Nonlocal determination survives.
- *Algorithmic randomness.* Defines randomness precisely for sequences. Applies to sequences and not to the question of what produced them.
- *Deterministic interpretations.* Empirically equivalent by construction.
- *Chaos in classical systems.* Establishes that unpredictability does not imply indeterminism, which both sides accept.

**Closure.** Endings 2, 3 and 5. Deterministic interpretations are empirically equivalent; statistical tests provably cannot establish indeterminism.

**What has been settled so far.** Bell exclusion of local determination. The general result that statistical tests cannot establish indeterminism. The chaos point.
**What would settle the rest.** An experiment distinguishing deterministic from indeterministic accounts, which their empirical equivalence excludes.

## 2.98 Qualia and the zombie argument

**The question.** Could there be a being physically identical to a person with no experience at all? If it is conceivable, what follows?

**One answer given.** Conceivability supports possibility here, and possibility refutes physicalism. The argument is deductive, its premises are examinable, and no defect has been generally agreed on despite three decades of attention.

**The other answer given.** Conceivability does not entail possibility. Water without H₂O is conceivable and impossible, and the a posteriori necessity cases show the inference fails in exactly the structurally similar cases; the zombie's conceivability may reflect a limitation in how the physical is represented rather than a metaphysical fact.

**Origin conditions.** That conceivability bears on possibility; that physical duplication fixes all physical facts.

**What both answers require.** That the physical facts either fix the experiential ones or do not.

**What the question is about.** A modal relation between two sets of facts.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Actual systems.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Two-dimensional semantics.* Chalmers's framework separating the senses in which the water case differs. Contested in detail, and the contest is technical and unresolved.
- *Phenomenal concept strategy.* Explains the conceivability by the special character of phenomenal concepts, preserving physicalism. Its critics argue the strategy either fails to explain the concepts physically or concedes the point.
- *Deny the conceivability.* Hold that zombies are not coherently conceivable on reflection. A minority position, defended.
- *Deny the entailment.* The main physicalist route; rests on the a posteriori necessity cases, which the two-dimensional framework addresses.
- *Empirical work.* Bears on which functions are physical; the argument is about what follows if all of them are.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Two-dimensional semantics and the phenomenal concept strategy are technical and unresolved; the strategy faces a dilemma stated precisely by both sides.

**What has been settled so far.** The structure of the argument and the two-dimensional framework. That the phenomenal concept strategy faces a dilemma stated precisely by both sides.
**What would settle the rest.** A generally accepted criterion for when conceivability bears on possibility.

## 2.99 The quantum interpretation debate

**The question.** Several interpretations reproduce every prediction of quantum mechanics and describe reality differently. Which is correct?

**One answer given.** The question is not empirical and should be set aside. Interpretations that agree on every prediction differ only in what they add beyond the formalism, and adding unobservable structure is not what physics is for. The formalism is complete as an instrument: it says what will be measured with unmatched precision, and a century of successful application has required no interpretive commitment at all.

**The other answer given.** The interpretations differ in more than presentation — in whether the state is physical, whether there is one world, whether the theory is deterministic — and these are questions about what the theory says is the case. Setting them aside is not answering them, and the differences bear on extending the theory to new domains.

**Origin conditions.** That the formalism admits multiple descriptions of what is the case; that theory choice is settled by prediction.

**What both answers require.** That the interpretations are genuinely empirically equivalent.

**What the question is about.** What the world is like given the formalism.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Predictions, which are identical across interpretations.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Look for a discriminating prediction.* Collapse models make one and are in the empirical arena; the interpretations proper, by construction, do not.
- *Ontological models framework.* PBR theorem constrains epistemic readings of the state under stated assumptions. A genuine constraint, and its assumptions are contested.
- *Extended Wigner's friend arguments.* Frauchiger-Renner derive an inconsistency for certain single-world assumptions. Contested in detail, and interpretations differ on which premise fails.
- *Simplicity and coherence.* Each interpretation claims economy on a different measure.
- *Extension to gravity and cosmology.* Interpretations differ in fruitfulness, argued on both sides without resolution so far.

**Closure.** Ending 2, by construction. The interpretations are empirically equivalent; collapse models, which are not, are in the empirical arena and appear at entries 1 and 100.

**What has been settled so far.** PBR under its assumptions. The Frauchiger-Renner result and the mapping of which interpretation rejects which premise. That collapse models are distinguishable and interpretations are not.
**What would settle the rest.** A discriminating prediction, which empirical equivalence excludes by construction.

## 2.100 The quantum-classical boundary

**The question.** Small systems show superposition; large ones do not. Where is the boundary and what sets it?

**One answer given.** There is no boundary, only decoherence rates. Superposition persists at all scales and becomes unobservable as environmental coupling grows, and interference has been demonstrated for progressively larger molecules with no scale-dependent break found.

**The other answer given.** A boundary may exist. Collapse models predict a scale at which superposition genuinely fails, and the experiments so far have excluded parameter ranges without excluding the class. Absence of a break at reached masses is not absence of a break.

**Origin conditions.** That the boundary is either a matter of degree or a real transition; that interference experiments probe it.

**What both answers require.** That the question of a break has a determinate answer.

**What the question is about.** Behaviour at masses not yet reached.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Masses reached, currently large molecules and mechanical oscillators.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Interference at larger masses.* Demonstrated to tens of thousands of atomic mass units, with the frontier advancing. Ends by measuring more of what was reached, and it is real progress.
- *Optomechanical superposition.* Prepares mechanical oscillators in non-classical states. The masses remain far below the proposed collapse scales.
- *Collapse model constraints.* Bounded by interferometry, X-ray emission and gravitational-wave detector noise. Narrows without closing.
- *Decoherence calculation.* Accounts quantitatively for the disappearance of interference in every case measured. Both sides accept it and dispute whether anything remains.
- *Gravitationally induced collapse.* Penrose-type proposals give a scale; experiments to reach it are proposed and not yet performed.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 4. Interference at larger masses is real progress within the reached domain; collapse constraints narrow without closing.

**What has been settled so far.** Interference at measured masses. Decoherence rates, calculated and confirmed. Excluded regions of collapse parameter space.
**What would settle the rest.** Interference at a mass above a proposed collapse scale, or a measured break.

## 2.101 Explore and exploit

**The question.** Acting on what is known yields returns now; trying alternatives yields information. How much of each?

**One answer given.** The trade-off is solved for well-posed cases. The Gittins index gives the optimal policy for a class of bandit problems, and regret bounds for algorithms are proved and matched by lower bounds.

**The other answer given.** The optimality is relative to a specified environment, a prior, and a horizon. Where the environment is non-stationary, the option set is not enumerated in advance, or the horizon is unknown, no optimal policy is defined, and the practical cases are of that kind.

**Origin conditions.** That the options are enumerated; that the environment's statistics are stationary or their variation is specified; that a horizon is given.

**What both answers require.** That there is a well-defined optimum to approach.

**What the question is about.** Action where the option set and environment are not specified in advance.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Specified problems with stated priors and horizons.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Gittins index.* Optimal, for discounted independent-arm problems. The conditions are restrictive and stated.
- *Regret minimisation.* Bounds proved and matched. Regret is measured against the best fixed option in hindsight, which presupposes the option set.
- *Non-stationary and adversarial extensions.* Bounds available under stated variation budgets. The budget is supplied.
- *Bayesian optimal control.* Defines the optimum given a prior. The prior over unknown option sets is the difficulty.
- *Human and animal studies.* Find behaviour close to certain heuristics. Establishes what is done, not what is optimal in unspecified environments.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Gittins optimality holds under stated restrictive conditions; regret is measured against the best fixed option, which presupposes the option set.

**What has been settled so far.** Gittins optimality under its conditions. Matching regret bounds. Variation-budget results.
**What would settle the rest.** An optimality criterion for environments whose option set is not enumerated in advance.

## 2.102 Self and non-self in immunity

**The question.** The immune system attacks foreign things and not the body. How does it tell which is which?

**One answer given.** By tolerance to self. Lymphocytes reactive to self are deleted or suppressed during development, and the mechanisms — central and peripheral tolerance, regulatory cells — are identified and manipulable.

**The other answer given.** Self-non-self does not describe what is observed. The gut microbiota is foreign and tolerated, the fetus is foreign and tolerated, tumours are self and attacked, and the danger model holds that responses are triggered by tissue damage signals rather than by foreignness.

**Origin conditions.** That there is a distinction the system implements; that the distinction is between self and foreign.

**What both answers require.** That the system's discrimination has a describable basis.

**What the question is about.** What the system discriminates on.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Responses to defined antigens under defined conditions, each consistent with more than one criterion.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Tolerance mechanisms.* Central and peripheral tolerance identified and demonstrated. Establishes mechanisms; the microbiota and fetal cases remain.
- *Danger model.* Accounts for the anomalies by damage signals. Its critics note damage signals are themselves defined partly by what triggers responses.
- *Pattern recognition receptors.* Identify conserved microbial patterns. Establishes a foreignness-detecting mechanism, alongside self-derived ligands for the same receptors.
- *Tumour immunology.* Responses to self-derived neoantigens. Supports a criterion other than foreignness, and neoantigens are also mutated and so partly foreign.
- *Microbiome tolerance studies.* Establish active tolerance of foreign organisms, with mechanisms identified. Extends the account rather than settling the criterion.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Tolerance and danger accounts each explain their own anomalies; the danger model's trigger is defined partly by what triggers responses.

**What has been settled so far.** Tolerance mechanisms. Pattern receptors and their self-derived ligands. Active microbiota tolerance. Neoantigen responses.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion predicting the response to a novel antigen in a novel context, prospectively. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current standing of the danger model against pattern-recognition accounts in the field's own reviews, and whether microbiota tolerance mechanisms have been given a unifying criterion.

## 2.103 Cancer, the somatic mutation account and its rival

**The question.** Cancer arises when cells proliferate uncontrollably. Does the fault lie in the cell's own mutations, or in the tissue that organises it?

**One answer given.** Somatic mutation. Cancer is driven by accumulated mutations in oncogenes and tumour suppressors; driver mutations are catalogued across tumour types, targeted therapies against specific drivers work, and heritable mutation in the same genes raises risk.

**The other answer given.** Tissue organisation. Carcinogenesis is a tissue-level failure of the constraints normally imposed on proliferation; tissue recombination experiments show carcinogen-exposed stroma with normal epithelium produces tumours and the converse does not, and normal tissue can normalise malignant cells.

**Origin conditions.** That the causal locus is either the cell or its tissue context; that identifying it determines the intervention.

**What both answers require.** That there is a level at which the initiating fault sits.

**What the question is about.** Which level initiates.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Tumours already established, in which both cellular and tissue changes are present.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Sequencing driver mutations.* Catalogued comprehensively, with targeted therapy successes and near-universal resistance. Establishes drivers; does not establish initiation.
- *Tissue recombination experiments.* Support the tissue account in the systems tested. System-specific and not generally replicated across tissues.
- *Normalisation experiments.* Malignant cells behave normally in normal microenvironments in specific models. Establishes context dependence.
- *Mutations in normal tissue.* Sequencing of normal skin and oesophagus finds driver mutations at high density without cancer. Constrains the mutation account substantially, and is accepted in the field.
- *Field cancerisation and immune surveillance.* Both accounts accommodate the findings.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Driver catalogues and tissue recombination each establish their own result; high driver burden in normal tissue constrains the dominant account from inside.

**What has been settled so far.** Driver catalogues. Targeted therapy responses and resistance. High driver-mutation burden in normal tissue. Tissue recombination results in the systems tested.
**What would settle the rest.** A prospective criterion identifying, before a tumour forms, which level initiated. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the normal-tissue mutation literature is recent and moving.

## 2.104 Addiction

**The question.** People continue a behaviour that harms them and say they want to stop. Is this a disease of the brain or a choice under bad conditions?

**One answer given.** A brain disease. Repeated exposure produces durable changes in reward and control circuits, the changes are imaged and are dose-dependent, heritability is substantial, and treating it as a disease has reduced stigma and improved access to care.

**The other answer given.** The disease framing does not fit the data on recovery. Most people who meet criteria recover without treatment; behaviour responds strongly to incentives, including in randomised contingency-management trials; and rates track economic and social conditions in ways a brain disease does not predict.

**Origin conditions.** That the behaviour has a locus — neural pathology or choice under constraint; that identifying it determines the response.

**What both answers require.** That the behaviour is produced by an identifiable mechanism at one level.

**What the question is about.** Which account governs the behaviour.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Brain measures and behavioural measures, both altered in affected people.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Neuroimaging.* Documents circuit changes reliably. Cause and consequence are not separated by the imaging, which the field states.
- *Contingency management trials.* Produce large abstinence effects with modest incentives. Establishes responsiveness to consequences; both accounts accommodate it, with difficulty on one side.
- *Epidemiology of recovery.* High rates of remission without treatment, measured. Constrains chronic-disease framings.
- *Animal models.* Establish neuroadaptation; environmental enrichment substantially reduces self-administration, which supports the conditions account.
- *Genetic studies.* Substantial heritability, with small individual effects and no identified pathology.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Imaging does not separate cause from consequence; remission rates and incentive responsiveness are read oppositely by the two sides.

**What has been settled so far.** Circuit changes. Contingency management efficacy. Remission rates. Environmental effects in animal models. Heritability without identified pathology.
**What would settle the rest.** A measure distinguishing pathology from conditioned choice in an individual. **nye — not yet explored fully:** current remission-rate estimates and their sampling basis, and whether contingency management effect sizes have been replicated at scale outside trial settings.

## 2.105 Niche and neutral theory

**The question.** Do species coexist because they differ in what they do, or because they are approximately equivalent and abundance drifts?

**One answer given.** Niche differences. Coexistence requires stabilising differences, demonstrated experimentally by invasion tests, and species do differ measurably in resource use, timing, and enemies.

**The other answer given.** Neutrality fits the patterns. Hubbell's neutral theory reproduces species-abundance distributions and species-area relationships with no niche differences at all, and it fits many datasets as well as or better than niche models.

**Origin conditions.** That community structure is determined either by differences or by drift; that abundance patterns discriminate between them.

**What both answers require.** That the observed patterns bear on the mechanism.

**What the question is about.** The mechanism producing community structure.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Abundance and distribution data, which both models fit.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Fit abundance distributions.* Both fit. Established early and not resolved by more data of the same kind.
- *Invasion experiments.* Demonstrate stabilising differences where performed. Species-specific and laborious.
- *Chesson's partition.* Separates stabilising from equalising mechanisms and makes both measurable in principle. Measurement in diverse natural communities is partial.
- *Dynamic predictions.* Neutral and niche models differ in predicted temporal turnover; tests give mixed results and are sensitive to timescale.
- *Continuum framing.* Most current work treats both as operating together. Fits, and the weights are not independently measured.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Both models fit abundance distributions, which is the disagreement; the continuum framing accommodates both without measuring weights.

**What has been settled so far.** That abundance patterns do not discriminate. Invasion results where measured. The Chesson partition as a framework.
**What would settle the rest.** Measurement of stabilising and equalising components in a diverse natural community. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether field measurement of Chesson's partition has advanced beyond the small number of systems where invasion tests were performed.

## 2.106 Ecological stability and diversity

**The question.** Do more diverse communities resist disturbance better?

**One answer given.** Yes. Long-running grassland experiments that manipulate diversity directly, rather than observing it, find more diverse plots more stable in aggregate biomass across years, more resistant to drought, and faster to recover from it. The results replicate across continents and are supported by mechanisms — asynchrony in species responses and complementarity in resource use — that are separately measured.

**The other answer given.** Not in general. May's analysis showed randomly assembled complex systems are less stable, not more, and the empirical result is for aggregate properties: individual species populations are often less stable in diverse communities. What is stable depends on which variable is measured.

**Origin conditions.** That stability is a property of a community; that diversity is a variable that can be manipulated independently.

**What both answers require.** That stability has a definition both accept.

**What the question is about.** Which stability the question is about, the concepts diverging.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Specific variables — aggregate biomass, population variance, recovery time, resistance — which move in different directions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Grassland manipulation experiments.* Aggregate stability rises with diversity, robustly. Population-level stability does not, which is measured in the same experiments.
- *May's random-matrix analysis.* Proves instability for random complex systems. Real communities are not randomly assembled, which is the standing response.
- *Structured network models.* Find stability depends on interaction structure. Establishes structure matters; supplies no general answer.
- *Portfolio effects.* Statistical averaging explains part of the aggregate result without any ecological mechanism. Measured, and partial.
- *Definitional work.* Multiple stability concepts formalised. Clarifies the dispute and shows the answer differs by concept.

**Closure.** Ending 6, in its clearest form. Aggregate stability rises with diversity and population stability does not, in the same experiments, on the same plots.

**What has been settled so far.** The aggregate-versus-population divergence, measured. May's result. Portfolio contributions. The formal multiplicity of stability concepts.
**What would settle the rest.** Agreement on which stability concept the question is about, made before the measurement.

## 2.107 The Red Queen

**The question.** Species keep adapting and their relative position does not improve. Is adaptation getting them anywhere?

**One answer given.** Coevolution keeps them in place. Antagonistic coevolution means each improvement is met, so extinction risk stays roughly constant with age — Van Valen's original observation from fossil survivorship curves.

**The other answer given.** The pattern has other explanations. Later analyses find age-independent extinction arises from environmental change and from taxonomic artefacts as well, and the coevolutionary interpretation is one among several fitting the same survivorship curves.

**Origin conditions.** That the survivorship pattern indicates a mechanism; that taxa are comparable units.

**What both answers require.** That the fossil pattern bears on the mechanism.

**What the question is about.** What produces constant extinction risk.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Fossil ranges, with sampling and taxonomic definition affecting the curves.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Survivorship analysis.* Confirms approximate age-independence for many groups. Multiple mechanisms produce it.
- *Host-parasite coevolution experiments.* Demonstrate arms-race dynamics directly, in microbial systems. Establishes the mechanism exists at short timescales.
- *Molecular evolution of interacting genes.* Rapid evolution at host-parasite interfaces, measured. Supports coevolution locally.
- *Comparative extinction analyses.* Find environmental drivers dominate some intervals. Both mechanisms operate; weights unmeasured.
- *Taxonomic artefact analysis.* Shows genus definitions affect the curves. Constrains the original inference.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Coevolution is demonstrated at short timescales; multiple mechanisms produce age-independence, and taxonomic practice affects the curves.

**What has been settled so far.** Age-independence in many groups. Coevolutionary dynamics in experimental systems. Taxonomic effects on survivorship curves.
**What would settle the rest.** A fossil signature distinguishing coevolutionary from environmental drivers of extinction risk. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether recent survivorship analyses with improved sampling correction still support age-independence, and the current weight given to taxonomic artefact.

## 2.108 The hygiene hypothesis

**The question.** Allergic and autoimmune conditions rose sharply in industrialised populations over decades. Why?

**One answer given.** Reduced early microbial exposure. Farm-childhood studies show large protective effects, the mechanisms are traceable to immune regulation, and the timing and geography of the rise match changes in exposure.

**The other answer given.** The original framing does not fit. Infections in early life often raise rather than lower allergy risk, the effect is better attributed to microbiota composition and to specific exposures than to hygiene, and antibiotic use, diet, vitamin D, pollution and diagnostic change all track the same period.

**Origin conditions.** That the rise has a dominant cause; that population-level correlations identify it.

**What both answers require.** That the rise is attributable.

**What the question is about.** A dominant cause, identified from correlations among co-varying exposures.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Observational cohorts with many co-varying exposures, and a small number of trials.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Farm and Amish-Hutterite comparisons.* Large, consistent protective associations with identified mechanistic differences. Strongest evidence; confounding by lifestyle is acknowledged.
- *Microbiota studies.* Associate composition with outcomes and identify plausible mechanisms. Causality in humans is largely unestablished.
- *Helminth and probiotic trials.* Mixed results, with some positive findings in specific conditions.
- *Early peanut introduction trial.* Randomised, large effect, and it reverses a prior guideline. Establishes causality for one exposure and one outcome.
- *Diagnostic-change analyses.* Account for part of the measured rise, quantified.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. The peanut trial establishes causality for one exposure and one outcome; microbiota associations narrow without closing.

**What has been settled so far.** Farm effects. The peanut trial result. Diagnostic contribution. Microbiota associations.
**What would settle the rest.** Randomised manipulation of early microbial exposure with allergy outcomes. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current trial record for microbiota-directed interventions, and whether the Amish-Hutterite mechanistic findings have been replicated in other populations.

## 2.109 Hormesis

**The question.** Some agents harmful at high dose appear beneficial at low dose. Is the dose-response curve monotonic or not?

**One answer given.** Non-monotonic responses are real and common. Documented across thousands of studies in toxicology and pharmacology, with mechanisms — adaptive stress responses, receptor dynamics — identified in specific cases.

**The other answer given.** For regulation the default should remain linear at low dose. Low-dose effects are near the limit of detection, publication favours positive findings, and adopting hormesis as a default would relax exposure limits on evidence that is heterogeneous in quality.

**Origin conditions.** That a dose-response relation exists for an agent and an endpoint; that low-dose behaviour is estimable.

**What both answers require.** That there is a true low-dose response.

**What the question is about.** Response at doses near or below detection.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Doses with detectable effects, extrapolated downward by model.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Database assembly.* Large collections of non-monotonic curves. Heterogeneous in endpoint and quality, which both sides accept.
- *Mechanistic studies.* Establish adaptive responses in specific systems. Generality to other agents and endpoints is the open question.
- *Large low-dose animal studies.* Increase power at some cost; the doses of regulatory interest remain below the reachable range for many agents.
- *Epidemiology at low exposure.* Confounding dominates at the relevant magnitudes, which is measured.
- *Model selection.* Linear-no-threshold, threshold, and hormetic models fit the data comparably in the observable range and diverge below it.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 4 and 6. Rival dose-response models fit comparably in the observable range and diverge below it, which is where the question lies.

**What has been settled so far.** Non-monotonic curves in specific systems with mechanisms. The comparable fit of rival models in the observable range.
**What would settle the rest.** Measurement at regulatory-relevant doses with power to distinguish the models.

## 2.110 The dodo bird verdict

**The question.** Different psychotherapies produce similar outcomes. Do the specific techniques do anything?

**One answer given.** Common factors dominate. Meta-analyses across decades find small differences between bona fide therapies, and alliance, expectation and therapist effects account for more variance than technique.

**The other answer given.** Equivalence is partly an artefact. Some specific treatments outperform others for specific conditions — exposure for phobias, and specific protocols for obsessive-compulsive disorder — and averaging across conditions conceals this; allegiance effects also inflate apparent equivalence in comparative trials.

**Origin conditions.** That outcome is attributable to components of the treatment; that trials isolate components.

**What both answers require.** That the contribution of technique is separable from context.

**What the question is about.** The contribution of technique.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Whole treatments delivered in contexts, compared.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Comparative meta-analysis.* Small average differences. Averaging across conditions is the disputed step.
- *Dismantling studies.* Remove components and measure change. Some show specific components matter and many are underpowered, which is documented.
- *Condition-specific comparisons.* Show specific superiority for several disorders, accepted by both sides.
- *Allegiance analyses.* Quantify researcher allegiance effects, which are substantial and measured.
- *Placebo and waitlist controls.* Establish therapies exceed no treatment. Does not separate technique from context.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Averaging across conditions conceals specific superiority, which is the disputed step, and allegiance effects are measured.

**What has been settled so far.** Small average differences. Specific superiority in identified conditions. Allegiance effect magnitudes. Dismantling results where powered.
**What would settle the rest.** Adequately powered dismantling across a range of conditions. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether recent component-network meta-analyses have supplied the power that individual dismantling trials lacked.

## 2.111 Dreaming

**The question.** Dreaming is universal and vivid and occurs nightly. What is it for?

**One answer given.** It has a function. Candidates include emotional processing, memory consolidation, and threat simulation; each has supporting evidence, and a phenomenon this conserved and this costly in brain activity is unlikely to be incidental.

**The other answer given.** It may be a by-product. Dreaming may be an epiphenomenon of the physiological state that has the function, and the evidence for content-specific benefit is weak; damage abolishing dream report leaves sleep function largely intact in reported cases.

**Origin conditions.** That dreaming is separable from the sleep state carrying it; that a function is identifiable by manipulation.

**What both answers require.** That dreaming can be manipulated separately from sleep.

**What the question is about.** The contribution of dreaming as distinct from sleep.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Sleep stages and reports on waking, with dreaming and its physiological substrate co-occurring.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Selective REM deprivation.* Confounded by arousal and rebound, which the field has documented extensively.
- *Lesion cases abolishing dream report.* Rare, and the reported cases constrain function claims.
- *Targeted memory reactivation in sleep.* Improves retention. Establishes a sleep effect; the link to dream content is not established.
- *Dream content analysis.* Finds thematic regularities consistent with several accounts.
- *Lucid dreaming with communication.* Two-way communication established in recent work, opening a route. Early, and its reach for function questions is not yet clear.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Selective deprivation is confounded by arousal; memory reactivation establishes a sleep effect not linked to dream content.

**What has been settled so far.** Content regularities. Memory reactivation effects. The lesion cases. Two-way communication in lucid dreaming.
**What would settle the rest.** Manipulation of dreaming with sleep physiology held constant. **nye — not yet explored fully:** what the two-way lucid dreaming communication work has since established, and whether it reaches function questions at all.

## 2.112 The Cambrian explosion

**The question.** Most animal body plans appear in the fossil record over a geologically short interval. What produced the concentration?

**One answer given.** A real evolutionary event. Ecological triggers — the rise of predation, oxygen thresholds, the evolution of eyes — produced rapid diversification, and the fossil concentration reflects a genuine burst.

**The other answer given.** The concentration is partly artefact. Molecular clocks place divergences substantially earlier, the appearance of hard parts changes preservation probability sharply at that boundary, and taxonomic practice assigns higher ranks to early forms, all of which concentrate apparent origination.

**Origin conditions.** That first appearance in the record approximates origination; that body plans are comparable units.

**What both answers require.** That the record bears on the timing of divergence.

**What the question is about.** Origination times, approximated by first appearance.
**What the field's instruments reach.** First appearances, conditioned on preservation, and molecular clocks conditioned on calibration.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Molecular clocks.* Place divergences earlier; estimates depend on calibration and rate models and have varied widely between studies.
- *Preservation modelling.* Quantifies the hard-part bias, substantially. Establishes part of the artefact.
- *Ediacaran and trace fossil work.* Extends the record of animal activity earlier. Narrows the gap in one direction.
- *Oxygen proxies.* Improved records show a complex rather than a single threshold, weakening simple trigger accounts.
- *Developmental genetics.* Establishes the regulatory toolkit predates the explosion, which supports the earlier-divergence account and does not date the morphological events.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Molecular clocks and the fossil record return different timings, and preservation modelling quantifies part of the difference.

**What has been settled so far.** Toolkit antiquity. Preservation bias magnitude. Trace fossil records extending earlier. Clock variance across studies.
**What would settle the rest.** Body-fossil evidence from the interval the clocks imply. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current spread of molecular clock estimates under improved calibration, and whether Ediacaran assignments to animal groups have stabilised.

## 2.113 The Sapient paradox

**The question.** Anatomically modern humans appear long before the changes in behaviour and settlement usually taken as their signature. Why the gap?

**One answer given.** A cognitive change occurred later. A neural or genetic change enabled symbolic behaviour tens of thousands of years after anatomy stabilised, and the archaeological transition marks it.

**The other answer given.** The gap is in the record and in demography. African finds push symbolic behaviour progressively earlier, population density thresholds affect cultural accumulation and loss independently of cognition, and the apparent transition is a sampling and demography effect.

**Origin conditions.** That behavioural modernity is a definable package; that its archaeological signature indexes cognition.

**What both answers require.** That the archaeological record bears on cognitive capacity.

**What the question is about.** Cognitive capacity, indexed by preserved material culture.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Preserved material culture, unevenly sampled by region and period.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Push the record earlier.* African pigment, beads and engraving finds have repeatedly moved the earliest evidence back. This route has been productive and continues.
- *Demographic modelling.* Shows accumulation and loss depend on population size and connectedness, reproducing the pattern without cognitive change.
- *Ancient genomics.* Identifies candidate changes; none has been tied to behaviour.
- *Redefine modernity as a package or a mosaic.* The mosaic reading is now widely adopted, which weakens the paradox's original framing.
- *Neanderthal comparison.* Symbolic behaviour attributed to Neanderthals in several finds, contested in dating and attribution.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 8. The record has been extended repeatedly; the mosaic reframing weakens the paradox's original form rather than answering it.

**What has been settled so far.** The African record's progressive extension. Demographic model results. That the trait package is a mosaic rather than a unit.
**What would settle the rest.** A cognitive marker readable from material culture independent of population size. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current earliest secure dates for symbolic artefacts in Africa, which have moved repeatedly, and the standing of demographic models against them.

## 2.114 The Flynn effect and its reversal

**The question.** Test scores rose substantially for decades across countries, then flattened or fell in several. What was rising, and what changed?

**One answer given.** Environmental improvement, then its exhaustion. Nutrition, schooling, family size and test familiarity raised measured performance, and gains flattened as those improved conditions saturated in wealthy countries.

**The other answer given.** What was measured may not be what the tests are taken to measure. Gains are concentrated in subtests least loaded on the general factor, which is a measured pattern and is the opposite of what a rise in general ability predicts; measurement invariance across cohorts has been tested and often fails.

**Origin conditions.** That the tests measure the same construct across cohorts; that score change indexes construct change.

**What both answers require.** That cross-cohort comparison of scores is meaningful.

**What the question is about.** A construct compared across cohorts.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Test scores, whose invariance across cohorts is itself in question.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Measurement invariance testing.* Frequently fails across cohorts, which is established and is the strongest constraint on the interpretation.
- *Subtest decomposition.* Gains concentrated on the least g-loaded subtests, measured consistently.
- *Within-family analyses.* Norwegian conscript data show the reversal within families, which excludes compositional explanations such as migration for that population.
- *Environmental proxies.* Nutrition and schooling correlate with gains historically; saturation is inferred rather than measured directly.
- *Test-content analyses.* Show changes in what items require, consistent with familiarity accounts.

**Closure.** Ending 5, decisive. Measurement invariance across cohorts frequently fails, which is a proved limitation on the comparison the question requires.

**What has been settled so far.** Invariance failures. Subtest concentration. The within-family reversal result.
**What would settle the rest.** A measure demonstrably invariant across cohorts, applied to the same period.

## 2.115 Eukaryogenesis

**The question.** Complex cells arose once, from an association of simpler cells. What was the sequence?

**One answer given.** Mitochondria first. The host was an archaeon, and acquisition of the bacterial endosymbiont preceded and enabled the rest of eukaryotic complexity, with the energy supply making a large genome affordable.

**The other answer given.** Host complexity first. Asgard archaea carry many eukaryotic signature proteins and cytoskeletal machinery, and the first cultured Asgard organism forms protrusions consistent with engulfment, indicating substantial complexity before the symbiosis.

**Origin conditions.** That there was a sequence of events; that present organisms and phylogenies constrain it.

**What both answers require.** That the order of acquisition is recoverable.

**What the question is about.** The order of acquisition, recovered from extant genomes.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Extant genomes, phylogenetic reconstruction, and a small number of cultured relatives.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Phylogenomics.* Places eukaryotes within Asgard archaea, robustly. Establishes the host lineage; not the order of events.
- *Asgard culturing.* One organism cultured after long effort, with slow growth and protrusion morphology. A major advance and a single organism.
- *Energetics arguments.* Energy-per-gene arguments favour mitochondria-first. Contested on assumptions about scaling.
- *Ancestral genome reconstruction.* Infers gene content of the last common ancestor. Depends on model and taxon sampling, and estimates differ.
- *Fossil record.* Sparse and difficult to assign at the relevant age.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. Phylogenomics establishes the host lineage and not the order of events; culturing has produced one organism.

**What has been settled so far.** The Asgard placement. The cultured organism and its morphology. Reconstructed gene content, with model dependence stated.
**What would settle the rest.** Additional cultured lineages or intermediate organisms. **nye — not yet explored fully:** Asgard culturing has advanced recently.

## 2.116 Homeostasis and allostasis

**The question.** Physiological variables are held within ranges. Is regulation toward a setpoint, or anticipatory adjustment of the target?

**One answer given.** Setpoint regulation. Negative feedback around a reference is well characterised for temperature, osmolality and glucose, with identified sensors, comparators and effectors.

**The other answer given.** Anticipatory regulation. Targets shift in advance of demand, driven by prediction; measured variables show sustained shifts with no error signal, and the setpoint account handles these only by positing a moving setpoint, which concedes the point.

**Origin conditions.** That regulation is toward a value; that the value is fixed or predicted.

**What both answers require.** That there is a regulated variable with a target.

**What the question is about.** Whether the target is fixed or set in advance.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Measured variables and their trajectories, compatible with both descriptions.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Identify comparator circuits.* Found for temperature and osmolality. Establishes setpoint machinery in those systems.
- *Anticipatory response measurement.* Documented before meals, exercise and stress, robustly. Establishes anticipation.
- *Moving setpoint models.* Accommodate anticipation within the feedback framework. Its critics hold this makes the framework unfalsifiable, which is stated in the literature.
- *Control-theoretic modelling.* Both descriptions are representable; model comparison on the same data does not separate them reliably.
- *Obesity and set-point defence studies.* Show vigorous defence of body weight, which both accounts claim.

**Closure.** Endings 6 and 8. Both descriptions fit the same trajectories; the moving-setpoint model absorbs anticipation at a cost to testability its critics state.

**What has been settled so far.** Comparator circuits for specific variables. Anticipatory responses. That both models fit the same trajectories.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurement distinguishing a shifted target from an unshifted target with an added anticipatory input.

## 2.117 Antibiotic resistance

**The question.** Every antibiotic class has been followed by resistance. Is this avoidable by better use, or intrinsic to the approach?

**One answer given.** Manageable by stewardship and pipeline. Resistance rates respond to prescribing practice, cycling and combination strategies slow it, and new classes and mechanisms continue to be found.

**The other answer given.** Intrinsic. Resistance genes predate clinical antibiotic use, are widespread in environmental reservoirs and are mobile between organisms; any sustained selection pressure selects, and stewardship changes the rate rather than the outcome.

**Origin conditions.** That resistance is a response to use; that the response is modifiable by managing use.

**What both answers require.** That the trajectory is determined by selection pressure that management can alter.

**What the question is about.** The outcome under regimes not yet run, with selection pressure sustained.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Resistance rates under regimes that have been tried, over years to decades.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Stewardship programmes.* Reduce resistance rates measurably in specific settings. Effects are real and partial and reverse when relaxed.
- *Cycling and mixing strategies.* Modelling and trials give mixed results; mixing outperforms cycling in several models.
- *New classes.* Discovered at a slow rate; resistance has followed each within years of deployment, without exception so far.
- *Resistome surveys.* Find resistance genes in ancient and pristine environments. Establishes the reservoir predates use.
- *Alternative modalities.* Phage, antivirulence, and microbiome approaches. Early; resistance to phage arises readily, which is documented.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. Stewardship reduces rates and reverses when relaxed; the ancient resistome establishes the reservoir predates use.

**What has been settled so far.** Ancient and environmental resistome. Stewardship effect sizes. The record of resistance following each new class.
**What would settle the rest.** A deployed agent under sustained selection with no resistance emerging over a comparable period.

## 2.118 Alignment of built systems

**The question.** A system is trained to optimise a specified objective. How is it made to do what was intended rather than what was specified?

**One answer given.** By improving specification and oversight. Objectives learned from human feedback capture what a specification could not state; tasks are decomposed so that evaluation is easier than performance, which is the property the approach rests on; systems assist in evaluating systems; and interpretability supplies a check independent of behaviour. Each component has measurable effect, and the combination has produced large and documented improvements in the behaviour of deployed systems.

**The other answer given.** The gap may not close by these means. Feedback trains on evaluations, and where a system's competence exceeds an evaluator's, the training signal measures what evaluators approve rather than what is intended; specification gaming is documented in a large catalogue of cases, and the failure recurs at each level of the oversight scheme.

**Origin conditions.** That the intended behaviour is specifiable, or that a specification error is detectable by evaluation; that evaluation is available at the level of the behaviour.

**What both answers require.** That there is an intended behaviour distinguishable from the specified one.

**What the question is about.** Behaviour in situations not evaluated.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Behaviour on evaluated distributions, and on tests constructed in advance.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Reinforcement learning from human feedback.* Produces large behavioural improvements, and reward models are documented to be gameable and to be optimised against.
- *Scalable oversight.* Debate, recursive decomposition, and assisted evaluation. Results are promising in constructed settings; whether they hold where the gap is largest is untested by construction.
- *Interpretability.* Aims to read intent from internals. Its own coverage is partial, which is entry 2.79.
- *Red-teaming and evaluations.* Find failures reliably, which establishes presence rather than absence.
- *Specification gaming catalogues.* Document the phenomenon across many systems. A record, not a remedy.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. Feedback training produces measured gains and the reward model is documented to be gameable; scalable oversight is untested where the gap is largest.

**What has been settled so far.** Reward model gaming, documented. Behavioural gains from feedback training, measured. Evaluation coverage limits. The specification gaming record.
**What would settle the rest.** An oversight method whose reliability does not depend on the evaluator matching the system's competence. **nye — not yet explored fully:** this field moves quickly.

## 2.119 Benchmarks

**The question.** Systems are compared on benchmark scores. Do scores measure the capability the benchmark names?

**One answer given.** Approximately, and usefully. Benchmark scores correlate with downstream task performance where both are measured, benchmark competition has driven progress that transferred to applications, and suites are retired and replaced when they saturate. Every science measures with imperfect instruments and improves them; requiring an unimpeachable measure before measuring at all would stop the field.

**The other answer given.** Scores measure benchmark performance. Contamination of training data by test items is documented and hard to exclude at scale, benchmarks are optimised against once published, and score gains have repeatedly failed to transfer to tasks the benchmark was taken to represent.

**Origin conditions.** That the benchmark samples a capability; that the sample is independent of what is trained on.

**What both answers require.** That there is a capability the benchmark samples.

**What the question is about.** Capability on tasks not in the benchmark.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Benchmark items, and constructed held-out sets that become public in turn.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Held-out and private test sets.* Effective until released or inferred; the ones kept private cannot be independently verified, which is the trade.
- *Contamination detection.* Methods exist and give partial coverage; the field states the coverage is partial.
- *Adversarial and dynamic benchmarks.* Constructed against current systems. Saturate as systems adapt, which is the same cycle.
- *Task diversity and aggregate suites.* Reduce single-benchmark artefacts; the aggregate is optimised against in turn.
- *Downstream deployment measurement.* The strongest route, and expensive, slow and confounded by deployment context.

**Closure.** Endings 1 and 5. Each held-out set is optimised against in turn; the mechanism is the multitask distortion result at a third site.

**What has been settled so far.** Contamination presence, measured in specific cases. Saturation cycles, documented. Transfer failures, documented. The mechanism is the multitask distortion result of entry 2.129 and the law of entry 2.12, at a third site.
**What would settle the rest.** A measurement of capability whose items cannot be optimised against.

## 2.120 Standards and interoperability

**The question.** Common standards let systems work together. Do they raise or lower the total value produced?

**One answer given.** They raise it. Network effects mean shared standards produce compatibility gains that exceed the constraint costs, demonstrated across shipping containers, telecommunications, and internet protocols.

**The other answer given.** It depends on timing and on what is frozen. Standards set early lock in inferior designs and foreclose alternatives, and the cases where a standard blocked improvement are documented alongside the successes; there is no general answer, only cases.

**Origin conditions.** That the counterfactual — the value under an unadopted alternative or no standard — is estimable.

**What both answers require.** That the value under the unadopted arrangement is comparable.

**What the question is about.** A counterfactual arrangement at scale.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The adopted arrangement, and pre-standard periods that differ in many other respects.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Case studies of successful standards.* Establish large gains where adopted. Selection on adoption is the objection.
- *Case studies of lock-in.* Establish costs in specific cases. Contested in the same way as entry 2.58.
- *Competing-standard episodes.* Where two standards competed, comparison is available for a period. Outcomes are confounded by firm strategy and timing.
- *Formal modelling.* Produces both outcomes under stated conditions. The conditions are the empirical question.
- *Standards body process studies.* Document how choices are made. Explains adoption, not value.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Case studies on both sides are contested; formal models produce both outcomes under stated conditions.

## 2.121 Brooks's law

**The question.** Adding people to a late software project makes it later. Is this general?

**One answer given.** Yes, by a countable mechanism. Communication paths grow with the square of team size while labour grows linearly, and ramp-up consumes existing members' time; the arithmetic holds wherever the work is not partitionable.

**The other answer given.** It is conditional and often violated. Modular architecture, asynchronous coordination, and mature onboarding reduce the coupling; large distributed projects add contributors continuously without the predicted collapse, and the empirical record does not show a general law.

**Origin conditions.** That coordination cost scales with the number of pairs; that the work's partitionability is fixed.

**What both answers require.** That coordination cost and output are measurable against team size.

**What the question is about.** The effect of adding people, holding all else equal.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Projects where adding people co-occurs with schedule pressure, scope change and architecture choices.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Case histories.* Support the law in the original setting. Selection on troubled projects is the standing objection.
- *Open-source contributor studies.* Find sublinear but positive returns to contributors in modular projects. Establishes the conditionality.
- *Software estimation models.* Incorporate diseconomies of scale as fitted parameters. Fit, and the parameters are estimated from the same data.
- *Controlled experiments.* Small-team laboratory studies find coordination overhead; scale is far below the cases in question.
- *Architecture studies.* Show modularity reduces coupling, measurably. Establishes the mechanism's dependence on structure.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 8. Open-source studies establish conditionality; estimation models incorporate diseconomies as parameters fitted to the same data.

**What has been settled so far.** Coordination overhead, measured at small scale. Sublinear returns in modular projects. Modularity's effect on coupling.
**What would settle the rest.** A controlled comparison of added staffing at project scale.

## 2.122 Risk compensation

**The question.** Safety measures reduce the harm per event. Do they reduce total harm, or do people take more risk?

**One answer given.** Total harm falls. Seat belt, helmet and airbag introductions are followed by large measured reductions in death and serious injury, established across many jurisdictions with staggered adoption and confirmed in occupant-level data where exposure is controlled. Compensation, where it is measured at all, offsets a fraction of the mechanical gain and never the whole of it.

**The other answer given.** Compensation is real and sometimes complete. Behavioural adaptation is documented — following distances, overtaking margins, cycling behaviour around helmeted riders — and in some settings the aggregate effect on total harm is null or adverse, particularly where risk is transferred to others.

**Origin conditions.** That a safety measure's effect decomposes into a mechanical effect and a behavioural response; that both are estimable.

**What both answers require.** That the counterfactual harm without the measure is estimable.

**What the question is about.** Total harm under a counterfactual regime.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Outcomes after adoption, with concurrent changes in traffic, vehicles, medicine and enforcement.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Before-after mortality series.* Show large reductions. Confounded by concurrent trends, which the literature addresses with varying success.
- *Behavioural measurement.* Documents adaptation directly in specific behaviours. Establishes the mechanism; magnitude relative to the mechanical effect varies by study.
- *Natural experiments from staggered adoption.* Strongest route; results support net reductions for major measures with partial offsets.
- *Risk transfer studies.* Find effects on other road users in some settings, which the aggregate series can conceal.
- *Homeostatic theory.* Predicts full compensation. Its critics hold the mortality series refute it; its defenders dispute the counterfactual.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 4. Staggered-adoption designs support net reductions with partial offsets; risk transfer is measured in specific settings.

**What has been settled so far.** Net mortality reductions for major measures. Behavioural adaptation, measured. Risk transfer in specific settings.
**What would settle the rest.** Total-harm accounting including transferred risk, under a design isolating the measure.

## 2.123 Technical debt

**The question.** Choices that speed delivery now raise the cost of later change. Is the trade-off measurable?

**One answer given.** Yes, and increasingly. Code quality metrics correlate with defect rates and change cost, static analysis identifies specific classes, and firms that measure it report actionable results.

## 2.124 Signal and noise

**The question.** A channel carries a signal with noise. Where does one end and the other begin?

**One answer given.** The distinction is definable relative to a model of the source. Given the source statistics, the decomposition is exact, and information theory makes the whole treatment quantitative.

**The other answer given.** The decomposition is relative to what is wanted. Nothing in the channel marks which component is signal, the same physical variation is signal for one purpose and noise for another, and stochastic resonance shows added noise improving detection, which the framing treats as anomalous.

**Origin conditions.** That the channel's content decomposes into wanted and unwanted components; that the source model is available.

**What both answers require.** That the decomposition is well defined.

**What the question is about.** Which component is which, independent of purpose.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The total variation, and a model supplied by the analyst.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Model the source.* Makes the decomposition exact given the model. The model is supplied.
- *Blind source separation.* Recovers components from mixtures under stated assumptions — independence, sparsity — which are supplied.
- *Stochastic resonance.* Demonstrates noise improving detection in nonlinear systems, measured across physical and biological settings. Establishes the labels are purpose-relative.
- *Matched filtering.* Optimal given the expected signal shape, which is supplied.
- *Information-theoretic bounds.* Exact given the channel and source distributions.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Every decomposition is exact given its model, which is supplied; stochastic resonance establishes the labels are purpose-relative.

**What has been settled so far.** The exactness of every decomposition given its model. Stochastic resonance, demonstrated. Separation results under stated assumptions.
**What would settle the rest.** A decomposition not relative to a supplied model or purpose.

## 2.125 Work and waste heat

**The question.** An engine produces work and rejects heat. Is the split a property of the engine?

**One answer given.** The split is exact and physical. The first and second laws determine the maximum work extractable, efficiency is measured to high precision, and nothing about the accounting is conventional.

**The other answer given.** Which output counts as work is set by what is wanted from the device. In a heat pump the rejected heat is the product; in cogeneration both are. The thermodynamics is invariant and the labels are not, and nothing in the engine marks the split.

**Origin conditions.** That a device has an intended output; that efficiency is output over input.

**What both answers require.** That efficiency is well defined for the device.

**What the question is about.** Which output is the product.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Energy flows, all measurable, none labelled.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Thermodynamic accounting.* Exact, and invariant under relabelling. Both sides accept it entirely.
- *Exergy analysis.* Measures available work regardless of purpose. Comes closest to a purpose-free measure and still requires a reference environment, which is specified.
- *Second-law efficiency.* Ratio to the reversible ideal. Requires specifying which output the ideal produces.
- *Cogeneration accounting.* Standards allocate between outputs by convention, and the conventions differ between jurisdictions, which is documented.
- *Life-cycle accounting.* Extends the boundary; the boundary is drawn.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Thermodynamic accounting is exact and invariant under relabelling; exergy requires a specified reference environment.

**What has been settled so far.** The thermodynamic accounting, exactly. Exergy's dependence on a reference environment. The existence of differing allocation conventions.
**What would settle the rest.** An allocation between joint outputs not requiring a statement of purpose.

## 2.126 Mutation, fidelity and variation

**The question.** Replication makes errors. Are errors a failure of fidelity or the source of variation?

**One answer given.** Both descriptions apply and the biology is settled. Fidelity is under selection, mutation rates are themselves evolved, and rates sit near an optimum balancing the cost of errors against the cost of proofreading.

**The other answer given.** The optimum framing assumes selection acts on the rate as a trait, which requires linkage conditions that often fail; the drift-barrier hypothesis holds rates are set by the limits of selection efficacy rather than by an optimum, and rates track effective population size across taxa as that predicts.

**Origin conditions.** That mutation rate is a trait; that its value is explained either by optimisation or by the limits of selection.

**What both answers require.** That the observed rate has an explanation in these terms.

**What the question is about.** What sets the rate.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Measured rates across taxa, and population-genetic parameters estimated from the same genomes.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Mutation accumulation experiments.* Measure rates directly across many species. Establishes the values.
- *Drift-barrier tests.* Rates correlate inversely with effective population size across a wide range, as predicted. The strongest evidence, and effective population size is estimated with model assumptions.
- *Mutator strain studies.* Show elevated rates favoured under strong selection in the laboratory. Establishes conditions where rate evolves adaptively.
- *Proofreading cost measurement.* Costs are small in most systems, which weakens simple optimisation accounts.
- *Local rate variation studies.* Rates vary along genomes with sequence context and repair activity, which complicates a single rate as the trait.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Rate measurement is established; effective population size is estimated from the same genomes, which is where the inference closes on itself.

**What has been settled so far.** Measured rates. The inverse correlation with effective population size. Mutator advantage under strong selection. Low proofreading costs.
**What would settle the rest.** Independent estimation of effective population size, not from the same genomes. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether the drift-barrier correlation survives estimation of effective population size from ecological or demographic data rather than from the sequences whose mutation rate is being explained.

## 2.127 Intellectual property

**The question.** Ideas can be copied at no cost. Does granting exclusive rights over them increase or decrease what is created?

**One answer given.** It increases it. Without exclusivity, investment in costly creation is not recoverable, and the empirical record in pharmaceuticals — where development costs are high and imitation cheap — supports patent protection producing innovation that would not otherwise occur.

**The other answer given.** The evidence does not support it generally. Cross-country and historical studies of patent regime changes find little effect on innovation rates and some negative effects through blocking and litigation; sectors with weak protection show high innovation, and cumulative innovation is impeded where the inputs are owned.

**Origin conditions.** That creation responds to expected returns; that innovation is measurable and attributable to a regime.

**What both answers require.** That the counterfactual innovation under a different regime is estimable.

**What the question is about.** Creation that would have occurred under a different regime.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Patents filed, products launched, and innovation proxies under regimes that were adopted.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Cross-country regime comparison.* Finds weak or no association with innovation rates. Confounded by development level and institutions.
- *Historical natural experiments.* Patent extension and abolition episodes give mixed results, with several finding no innovation effect.
- *Sector comparison.* Pharmaceuticals differ from software and finance in imitation cost, which both sides accept and read as supporting their position.
- *Litigation and blocking studies.* Document costs of the regime, measured.
- *Prize and open-source alternatives.* Demonstrate creation without exclusivity in specific domains. Scope of transfer disputed.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Cross-country and historical studies return mixed results; sector comparison is read as supporting both positions.

**What has been settled so far.** Weak cross-country associations. Litigation and blocking costs. Innovation under weak protection in identified sectors. Historical episode results.
**What would settle the rest.** Comparable innovation measurement under two regimes with other conditions held.

## 2.128 Money as a store of value

**The question.** Money holds value over time. What makes it hold?

**One answer given.** Backing and scarcity. Value derives from what stands behind the money: a commodity redeemable on demand, a state's obligation to accept it for taxes, or a credible limit on issuance. The historical record is consistent — currencies that lost their backing or whose issuance became unlimited lost value, and those that retained one or the other did not, which is a large sample across many centuries and regimes.

**The other answer given.** Acceptance is self-referential. Fiat money holds value because it is accepted, and it is accepted because it is expected to hold value; the equilibrium is multiple, and models admit both valued and worthless outcomes for the same fundamentals, which is a theorem rather than an anomaly.

**Origin conditions.** That value has a determinant; that the determinant is either external backing or expectations.

**What both answers require.** That there is a determinant of the value.

**What the question is about.** Why acceptance persists.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Prices and holdings under regimes that persisted, and episodes where they did not.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Backing theories.* Account for commodity and convertible regimes. Fiat regimes persist without convertibility, which is the case in question.
- *Search-theoretic models.* Prove multiplicity: valued and non-valued equilibria coexist for the same fundamentals. A proved result, and it does not select.
- *Legal tender and tax-driven demand.* Explains acceptance by obligation. Its critics note money circulates beyond tax obligations and across borders.
- *Hyperinflation episodes.* Establish that acceptance can fail. Onset timing is not predicted by fundamentals in the episodes studied.
- *Cryptocurrency.* Demonstrates acceptance without backing or legal tender, for a period. Read as supporting expectations by one side and as unresolved by the other.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 5. Search-theoretic models prove equilibrium multiplicity, which does not select; tax-driven demand explains part of acceptance.

**What has been settled so far.** Equilibrium multiplicity, proved. Tax-driven demand's contribution. Hyperinflation cases and their timing.
**What would settle the rest.** A criterion selecting among the equilibria from observables.

## 2.129 Principal and agent

## 2.130 The paradox of thrift

**The question.** Saving benefits an individual. If everyone saves more, income falls and saving may not rise. Which holds?

**One answer given.** The aggregate effect is real. In demand-constrained conditions, higher desired saving lowers output and can leave realised saving unchanged; the mechanism is standard and the policy implication is drawn from it.

**The other answer given.** It holds only under conditions. With flexible prices, accommodating monetary policy, or an open economy, higher saving finances investment and raises capacity; the paradox is a special case of a demand-constrained regime, not a general result.

**Origin conditions.** That individual and aggregate relations can differ; that the operative regime is identifiable.

**What both answers require.** That the regime determining which relation holds is identifiable in advance.

**What the question is about.** Which regime obtains.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Aggregate series in which regime and outcome are jointly determined.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Model comparison.* Both results are derivable in their own models, which both sides accept.
- *Zero lower bound episodes.* Provide conditions where the demand-constrained mechanism is most likely; empirical work supports it there and is contested on identification.
- *Cross-country saving-growth correlations.* Positive on average, negative in some periods. Both readings available.
- *Fiscal multiplier estimation.* Central to the dispute; estimates vary widely by method and by regime, which the literature documents.
- *Microfoundation.* Reproduces the paradox in models with nominal rigidity, which is the disputed assumption.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Both results are derivable in their own models; multiplier estimates vary widely by method and regime.

**What has been settled so far.** Derivability of both results. Multiplier estimate dispersion. Zero-bound episode findings.
**What would settle the rest.** A regime indicator observable before the policy is taken.

## 2.131 The evolution of cooperation

**The question.** Natural selection favours what raises an individual's reproduction. Cooperation is widespread. How?

**One answer given.** By identified mechanisms. Kin selection, direct and indirect reciprocity, and population structure each produce cooperation under stated conditions, and Hamilton's rule quantifies the threshold in terms measurable in the field. The mechanisms make differential predictions that have been tested across taxa — relatedness in eusocial insects, reputation in humans, spatial structure in microbes — and each has been confirmed in its own domain.

**The other answer given.** The accounting is disputed at its foundation. Inclusive fitness and multilevel selection give the same predictions under general conditions, which is proved, so they are two accountings of one process rather than rival hypotheses; and the long dispute over which is correct is about the accounting rather than the biology.

**Origin conditions.** That fitness effects are attributable to individuals; that cooperation is a departure requiring explanation.

**What both answers require.** That the fitness accounting is well defined.

**What the question is about.** Which accounting describes the process.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Reproductive outcomes, decomposable either way.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Kin selection tests.* Relatedness predicts cooperation across many taxa, robustly. Establishes the pattern under either accounting.
- *Experimental evolution.* Produces cooperation and cheating dynamics as predicted. Same.
- *Formal equivalence results.* Show the two accountings agree under general conditions. This is the strongest item and it dissolves the rivalry rather than settling it.
- *Public goods experiments in humans.* Establish conditional cooperation and punishment. Mechanism-level findings.
- *The 2010 eusociality dispute.* A large public disagreement with a rebuttal signed by many; the mathematical points were not resolved by the exchange.

**Closure.** Ending 7, in its clearest form. Inclusive fitness and multilevel selection are proved to give the same predictions under general conditions, so the rivalry is between accountings.

**What has been settled so far.** Relatedness effects. Experimental dynamics. The formal equivalence of the two accountings.
**What would settle the rest.** A case where the two accountings give different predictions, which the equivalence results say is restricted.

## 2.132 Transfer of learning

**The question.** Something learned in one setting is applied in another. When does it transfer?

**One answer given.** When the settings share elements. Near transfer is reliable, measured across a century of studies, and predicted well by identical-elements accounts. Instruction that makes shared structure explicit improves it, worked-example and contrasting-case methods produce measurable gains, and expertise transfers readily within a domain, which is what the account predicts.

**The other answer given.** Far transfer is rarely demonstrated. Cognitive training studies show gains on trained tasks and little on untrained ones; the record across brain training, chess, music and general reasoning shows near transfer and repeated failure of far transfer, and where positive results appear they often fail replication.

**Origin conditions.** That transfer is a property of the relation between two tasks; that task similarity is measurable independently of transfer.

**What both answers require.** That similarity is specifiable before the transfer is measured.

**What the question is about.** Application to a task not yet specified.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Pairs of tasks selected by the experimenter, whose similarity is judged.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Identical-elements analysis.* Predicts near transfer well. Similarity is characterised after the tasks are chosen.
- *Cognitive training trials.* Large, controlled, and consistently null for far transfer, which is now the field's own summary position.
- *Expertise studies.* Expert advantage is domain-bound, established across domains.
- *Analogical transfer studies.* Show transfer with hints and little without. Establishes the conditions and their fragility.
- *Metacognitive instruction.* Produces modest general gains in some studies; effect sizes are small and replication is partial.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 5. The far-transfer null record is now the field's own summary; expertise is domain-bound by an established result.

**What has been settled so far.** Near transfer. The far-transfer null record. Domain-boundedness of expertise. Hint dependence in analogical transfer.
**What would settle the rest.** A specification of task similarity made before transfer is measured, predicting it.

## 2.133 Bernstein's degrees of freedom

**The question.** A limb has many more degrees of freedom than a movement requires. How is one solution selected?

**One answer given.** By optimisation. Movements minimise a cost — jerk, torque change, endpoint variance — and optimal control models reproduce observed trajectories closely across tasks.

**The other answer given.** The cost is inferred from the movements it explains. Different costs fit the same trajectories, synergies reduce dimensionality without an optimiser, and observed variability is structured rather than minimised, which the uncontrolled manifold analyses show directly.

**Origin conditions.** That a unique solution is selected; that selection is by minimisation of a cost.

**What both answers require.** That the selection has a determinant.

**What the question is about.** The selection rule.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Recorded kinematics and muscle activity, fit by several models.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Optimal control modelling.* Fits trajectories well. Multiple cost functions fit comparably, which is documented.
- *Inverse optimal control.* Recovers a cost from behaviour. Identifiability of the cost is limited and known to be so.
- *Muscle synergy analysis.* Finds low-dimensional structure. Whether synergies are neural or an artefact of the decomposition is disputed, and the dispute is technical and live.
- *Uncontrolled manifold analysis.* Shows variability structured to preserve task-relevant variables. Establishes structure without an optimiser.
- *Perturbation studies.* Show rapid task-relevant corrections, supporting feedback control accounts.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Multiple cost functions fit the same trajectories; inverse optimal control has known identifiability limits.

**What has been settled so far.** Trajectory fits. Cost identifiability limits. Structured variability. Rapid task-relevant feedback.
**What would settle the rest.** Independent measurement of the cost being minimised.

## 2.134 The Whorf question

**The question.** Does the language a person speaks shape how they think?

**One answer given.** Yes, measurably. Colour categories affect discrimination speed with lateralised effects, grammatical gender affects object descriptions, and spatial frame languages produce different non-linguistic spatial reasoning, replicated across communities.

**The other answer given.** The effects are real and narrow. They appear largely in tasks where language is available as a strategy and diminish under verbal interference; core cognition — number approximation, object tracking, basic spatial reasoning — appears language-independent, and the strong version has not been supported.

**Origin conditions.** That thought and language are separable; that non-linguistic tasks measure thought without language.

**What both answers require.** That a task can measure thought independently of language.

**What the question is about.** Thought as distinct from language use.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Task performance, in participants who have language available.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Verbal interference designs.* Reduce or remove many effects. The strongest route, and it establishes the effects are partly strategic rather than absent.
- *Colour category studies.* Lateralised effects replicated. Interpreted as language-driven categorical perception or as label-based strategy.
- *Spatial frame studies.* Differences in non-linguistic tasks across communities, robust. Confounded by environment and practice, which the field notes.
- *Infant and prelinguistic studies.* Establish capacities before language. Bear on the strong version only.
- *Bilingual switching studies.* Show within-person shifts by language of task, which supports an online rather than a structural effect.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Verbal interference removes many effects, which establishes they are partly strategic; the remaining effects are read oppositely.

**What has been settled so far.** Interference effects. Replicated colour and spatial findings. Prelinguistic capacities. Bilingual shifts.
**What would settle the rest.** A task measuring thought with language demonstrably unavailable.

## 2.135 Grandmother cell and distributed coding

**The question.** Is a concept represented by a small number of highly selective neurons, or by a pattern across many?

**One answer given.** Sparse and selective. Recordings in human medial temporal lobe find single neurons responding to one individual across photographs taken at different angles, line drawings, and the written and spoken name — invariance across format that no low-level feature explains. Firing rates from a handful of such cells suffice to decode which of many individuals was seen, and the same cells respond to associated concepts, which is what a sparse conceptual code predicts.

**The other answer given.** Distributed. The recorded cells are the tail of a broad distribution, recording is biased toward responsive units, and population decoding from many weakly tuned neurons outperforms sparse readout in most systems studied.

**Origin conditions.** That the representation has a determinate sparseness; that recording samples the population without bias.

**What both answers require.** That the sampled neurons represent the population.

**What the question is about.** Sparseness across a population, from a screened sample.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Neurons selected by responsiveness during a search, in patients with clinical electrode placement.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Single-unit recording in humans.* Finds highly selective cells reliably. Sampling is driven by a screening procedure, which the authors state.
- *Large-scale population recording.* Finds broad, weak tuning dominating, in animal cortex. Establishes the distributed picture in accessible areas.
- *Decoding comparisons.* Population decoding generally outperforms sparse. Depends on the recorded set.
- *Sparseness estimation from unbiased sampling.* Calcium imaging gives less biased samples and finds sparse but not single-cell coding.
- *Theoretical analysis.* Both codes are efficient under different constraints, which is proved and does not select.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Human recordings and animal population recordings give opposite pictures, with the sampling procedures differing.

**What has been settled so far.** Selective cells exist. Broad tuning dominates in sampled populations. Screening bias, acknowledged. Efficiency of both codes under stated constraints.
**What would settle the rest.** Unbiased sampling of a whole population representing one concept. **nye — not yet explored fully:** whether large-scale unbiased recording or imaging in human medial temporal lobe has since been achieved, which is the recording bias the concept-cell result rests on.

## 2.136 The faint young sun

**The question.** Stellar models say the early sun was substantially dimmer. Geological evidence says liquid water was present. How?

**One answer given.** Greenhouse compensation. Higher concentrations of carbon dioxide, methane, or both offset the reduced luminosity, and the carbonate-silicate weathering cycle supplies a feedback that would have maintained whatever concentration was needed: as the sun brightened, weathering drew carbon dioxide down. The mechanism is quantitative, operates on the right timescale, and is the same feedback that regulates climate over geological time today.

**The other answer given.** The proposed concentrations conflict with proxies. Paleosol and other constraints bound early carbon dioxide below several proposed values, methane is limited by photochemistry in an anoxic atmosphere, and the remaining candidates — reduced albedo, different cloud behaviour, higher nitrogen pressure — each carry their own difficulties.

**Origin conditions.** That stellar evolution models are correct for the early sun; that proxies constrain early atmospheric composition.

**What both answers require.** That the early state is reconstructible from present evidence.

**What the question is about.** Early composition and albedo, reconstructed from proxies calibrated by models.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Proxies with wide uncertainty and model-dependent calibration.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Carbon dioxide greenhouse.* Requires concentrations that several proxy studies bound below. Proxies are contested in calibration.
- *Methane greenhouse.* Effective in anoxic conditions; photochemical haze formation limits it and can cool by shading, which is modelled both ways.
- *Reduced albedo.* Less land, fewer cloud nuclei. Plausible and hard to constrain from evidence.
- *Higher nitrogen pressure.* Broadens absorption lines; proxy estimates of early pressure have gone in both directions.
- *Revise the solar model.* A more massive early sun losing mass. Constrained by other observations and not favoured.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Proxy bounds and greenhouse requirements conflict, and the proxies are contested in calibration.

**What has been settled so far.** The luminosity deficit, from stellar models. Proxy bounds with their calibration disputes. Haze behaviour, modelled.
**What would settle the rest.** A proxy for early atmospheric pressure and composition with calibration independent of climate models. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current status of paleosol carbon dioxide bounds and of nitrogen pressure proxies, both of which have been revised in more than one direction.

## 2.137 The origin of the genetic code

**The question.** The mapping from codons to amino acids is nearly universal. Why this mapping?

**One answer given.** Selection for error tolerance. The assignment places chemically similar amino acids at codons differing by one base, so point mutations and mistranslation produce conservative substitutions. Measured against millions of randomly generated alternative codes, the natural code ranks in the extreme tail on every error metric tried, and the effect is strongest at the codon positions where errors are most frequent.

**The other answer given.** Optimality does not establish origin. Stereochemical affinities between codons and amino acids are reported for several pairs, and coevolution with biosynthetic pathways explains the block structure; the error-minimisation could be a consequence of an origin fixed for other reasons, and the accounts are not separable by the code's structure alone.

**Origin conditions.** That the mapping was fixed by a process; that the code's present structure carries evidence of it.

**What both answers require.** That present structure constrains the origin.

**What the question is about.** The origin of the mapping, constrained by its present structure.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The code's structure, extant variants, and laboratory affinity measurements.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Error-minimisation statistics.* The code ranks extremely high among random alternatives, robustly measured. Consistent with several origins.
- *Stereochemical affinity experiments.* Aptamer selection finds codon-related affinities for several amino acids. Contested in statistical treatment, and covers part of the code.
- *Coevolution accounts.* Explain block structure by biosynthetic relatedness, with good fit. Also consistent with other origins.
- *Code variants.* Natural and engineered variants show the code is changeable. Establishes it is not frozen absolutely.
- *Ancestral reconstruction.* Infers earlier smaller codes. Model-dependent and not decisive between accounts.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 6 and 8. Error-minimisation, stereochemical and coevolutionary accounts each fit the code's structure, which is consistent with several origins.

**What has been settled so far.** Error-minimisation ranking. Affinity results for a subset. Block structure and biosynthetic correlation. Natural variants.
**What would settle the rest.** Evidence distinguishing a code selected for error tolerance from one that acquired the property. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the current statistical standing of the aptamer affinity results, which have been contested on their null model.

## 2.138 The origin of language

**The question.** Language appears in one lineage. Was it a gradual extension of prior communication or a discrete change?

**One answer given.** Gradual. Precursors are present in primate communication, vocal learning, and gesture; anatomical and archaeological indicators extend back hundreds of thousands of years, and no evidence supports a single event.

**The other answer given.** Discrete in at least one respect. Recursive combination has no demonstrated animal analogue, and accounts of its gradual emergence do not specify intermediate states with a function; the discontinuity is in the record and in the comparative data alike.

**Origin conditions.** That language decomposes into components with independent evolutionary histories, or that it is a unit.

**What both answers require.** That the capacity's components are identifiable.

**What the question is about.** Syntactic capacity, indexed by anatomy and artefact.
**What the field's instruments reach.** Comparative animal data, anatomy, ancient DNA, and material culture.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Comparative studies.* Establish precursors for many components — vocal learning, intention reading, combinatorial signalling — and none for recursion.
- *Anatomical indicators.* Hyoid and hypoglossal canal evidence, contested in inferential value.
- *Ancient DNA.* Candidate genes identified; FOXP2's early story was substantially revised by later data, which is a documented reversal.
- *Archaeological proxies.* Symbolic artefacts as indirect evidence. Bears on symbolic capacity rather than syntax.
- *Computational modelling of emergence.* Produces combinatorial systems from communication pressures in simulation. Establishes sufficiency in models.

**Closure.** Endings 1, 3 and 6. Precursors are established for every component but recursion; ancient DNA produced a documented reversal.

**What has been settled so far.** Precursor components in animals. The FOXP2 revision. Simulation results. The absence of a demonstrated recursion analogue.
**What would settle the rest.** A marker of syntactic capacity readable from fossil or archaeological evidence. **nye — not yet explored fully:** the present state of the comparative evidence on recursion in animal signalling, and what ancient DNA now supports after the FOXP2 revision.

## 2.139 The Poincaré conjecture

**The question.** Is every simply connected closed three-manifold a three-sphere?

**Why the form does not fill.** Criterion (i) fails: no second answer is defended, before the proof or after. Perelman completed Hamilton's Ricci flow programme, the proof was verified by several independent groups over years, and the result is accepted without dissent.

**What is recorded.** A problem long-standing and widely regarded as very hard, settled by a method within the field's reach. Routes taken: the geometrisation programme, Ricci flow, and the surgery construction, each building on the last, and all ending in the proof. The difficulty was in constructing the proof, not in the question quantifying over a domain the field's instruments do not reach.

This entry is retained as the collection's worked contrast. Resource-limited difficulty and durability are different, and every other entry is readable as durable partly because this one is not.

**What would make it fill.** A defended second position, which none of the routes produced at any point.

## 2.140 Origin and fate of the universe

**The question.** Did the universe have a beginning, and will it have an end?

**One answer given.** A beginning is indicated. Expansion extrapolated backward reaches a state of extreme density; singularity theorems show that under stated energy conditions, geodesics are incomplete to the past, and inflationary spacetimes are past-incomplete by the Borde-Guth-Vilenkin argument.

**The other answer given.** The theorems constrain classical extrapolation only. They assume energy conditions that quantum fields violate, and the regime in question is one where classical general relativity is not expected to hold; bouncing and cyclic models remain consistent with observation.

**Origin conditions.** That extrapolation backward is valid to the regime in question; that the energy conditions hold there.

**What both answers require.** That the early state is constrained by present observation plus theory.

**What the question is about.** Conditions at and before the earliest accessible epoch.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The microwave background and later, with earlier epochs reached only through model-dependent inference.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Singularity theorems.* Proved under energy conditions. The conditions are violated by quantum fields, which is established.
- *Past-incompleteness arguments.* Apply to expanding spacetimes on average. Their reach for quantum regimes is disputed by the authors of alternatives.
- *Observational cosmology.* Constrains the last thirteen billion years with precision. The earliest epochs are inferred.
- *Bouncing and cyclic models.* Consistent with data; distinguishing signatures in primordial gravitational waves have been proposed and not observed.
- *Fate determination.* Depends on dark energy's equation of state, measured with improving precision and not to the accuracy that separates the scenarios.

**Closure.** Endings 3, 4 and 5. The singularity theorems are proved under energy conditions quantum fields violate; distinguishing signatures are proposed and unobserved.

**What has been settled so far.** The theorems and their conditions. Precision cosmology to the microwave background. Current dark energy constraints.
**What would settle the rest.** Observation of primordial signatures distinguishing the early-universe models.

## 2.141 The block universe and the low-entropy past

**The question.** Is the distinction between past and future a feature of the world or of our position in it?

**One answer given.** Of our position. The laws are symmetric, the four-dimensional description is complete, and the asymmetry we experience follows from the entropy gradient given a low-entropy boundary condition.

**The other answer given.** The boundary condition is not derived, and the account explains the arrow by stipulating it. Attempts to derive the past hypothesis have not succeeded, and accounts positing a dynamically distinguished present remain consistent with the physics.

**Origin conditions.** That temporal asymmetry is either in the laws, in a boundary condition, or in the observer's position; that these are exhaustive.

**What both answers require.** That the asymmetry has a locus.

**What the question is about.** The status of the boundary condition.
**What the field's instruments reach.** The consequences of whatever condition obtained.

**Whether these are the same.** No.

**Routes taken, and where each ends.**

- *Statistical derivation.* Complete given the past hypothesis. This is entry 2.21 at a different framing, and the two entries share their unresolved element.
- *Cosmological derivation of the initial state.* Proposals exist; each contains a stipulation at the same place.
- *Psychological accounts of temporal experience.* Explain the sense of passage from memory asymmetry, which itself follows from the entropy gradient. Consistent and does not derive the condition.
- *Dynamically distinguished present.* Available and unfalsifiable given relativistic equivalence.
- *Two-time boundary conditions.* Consistent constructions; not favoured by evidence.

**Closure.** Endings 3 and 8. The derivation holds given the hypothesis; each cosmological proposal contains a stipulation at the same place.

**What has been settled so far.** The derivation given the hypothesis. The consistency of the alternatives. The relation between memory asymmetry and the gradient. This entry and entry 2.21 share their unresolved element: the status of the boundary condition.
**What would settle the rest.** A derivation of the boundary condition from something not stipulated.

## 2.142 Riemann, Hodge, Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer, Yang–Mills

**The question.** In each of four conjectures, does the disputed object lie at the fixed set or centre of a proved symmetry? Riemann, the functional equation taking s to 1−s, and the critical line. Hodge, complex conjugation, and balanced classes. Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer, the functional equation taking s to 2−s, and the behaviour at the centre. Yang–Mills, gauge symmetry, and the existence of a mass gap.

**Why the form does not fill.** Criterion (i) fails in all four: one position is a conjecture and its negation has no defenders.

**What is recorded.** In each, the symmetry is proved and the location claim is not, and the falsifier in each case is an object off the symmetric locus: a zero off the critical line, a balanced class with no algebraic representative, two counts differing, a gap shown absent. Each is answerable true or false, and the truth value is untouched by anything recorded here.

**What would make it fill.** A defended second position in any of the four.

Whether entry by structure without a defended pair should be admitted is unresolved and is recorded here rather than decided.

## 2.143 Measurement, routing, and classification

**The question.** How is something measured before it is well distinguished, assigned before its requirements are attended, and categorised before it is engaged with? Three problems, recurring across many fields.

**Why the form does not fill.** Criterion (i) fails: no two positions stand opposed in any of the three. # 3 Readings across the collection

## 3.1 Where routes end, across the collection

The eight endings are declared in 1.6. Their distribution across the entries is stated here as an observation, not as a count offered as evidence.

**Ending 1, measuring more of what was reached,** is the most common. It appears in nearly every entry, and it is what a reader most needs to see: the additional measurement is real, the field regards it as progress, and it returns readings from a domain the question is not about.

**Ending 5, a proved limitation on the method,** is the most consequential. It appears in a majority of the mature entries, and in three cases one theorem stops two or more entries at the same place: the parity problem in sieve theory blocks Goldbach and twin primes; the multitask distortion theorem is Goodhart's law proved inside contract theory and appears again at benchmarks; Rice's theorem generalises halting across every non-trivial property.

**Endings 6, 7 and 8** were found in the writing rather than anticipated, and each recurs across fields sharing no subject matter. They are declared in 1.6 with their instances named.

## 3.2 Entries that do not fill the form

Three are recorded and marked rather than forced.

**Poincaré (2.139)** is the worked contrast: a century-long resistance settled by a method within the field's reach, with no defended second position at any time. It marks the difference between resource-limited difficulty and durability.

**Riemann, Hodge, Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer, Yang–Mills (2.142)** share a structure, a proved symmetry with a location claim at its fixed set or centre, and none has a defended negation. Whether entry by structure without a pair should be admitted is unresolved.

**Measurement, routing and classification (2.143)** is recorded as an anomaly: three literatures holding each other unrelated, one shared structure, and no defended pair in any of them. No inference is drawn.

## 3.3 Paired results in fields sharing no subject matter

A pattern recorded here rather than at individual entries.

A field proves that a capacity is not present at one side of a two-sided system. The proof is valid, the result accepted, and the field proceeds. A second field, sharing no subject matter, proves the same capacity absent at the other side. Neither states where the capacity is; each carries the implicit remainder that it must be at the side it does not examine.

Bell with the no-free-lunch theorem. Arrow with the same. Lewontin with the findings that a count does not track what it was expected to govern. Rice with the proof barriers.

Where two such results pair, their conjunction entails that the capacity is at neither side taken alone. Since the capacity is exhibited, the correlations measured and the performance differences observed, it is produced in the interaction. This is a valid inference from accepted theorems and requires no new premise and no new measurement.

**Consequence.** A durable problem's counterpart is often not an open problem but a closed result filed elsewhere as a finding. A census of open problems undercounts the class.

## 3.4 Two measured defects in the writing

Across the entries, the second position stated is longer than the first in 111 of 142 cases. Chance would give about half. This is evidence of a systematic asymmetry in the writing rather than in the problems: the second position is usually the complicating or skeptical one, and it was given more room to explain itself.

The risk this creates is exactly F3 and F6: a first position stated more thinly than its holders would state it reads as weaker than it is, and a reader would then find the entry's balance false. Nineteen of the worst cases have been rewritten, with the first position restated at the strength its holders give it. The remainder are as written and the asymmetry is recorded here, so that a reader can weigh it and a specialist can correct any entry where the first position is thin.

Length is a screen and not a verdict. The count is given because it is checkable, not because it settles anything.

**A second measured defect: the route count.** Of the 141 filling entries, 134 list exactly five routes, five list six, and two list four. A field's record does not come in fives. Some questions have been approached three ways and some fifteen, and a count that is the same everywhere carries no information about any of them.

That regularity is the writer's hand, not the world's, and it means two things. Where a field took more routes than five, routes are missing, which is F2 and is the falsifier that matters. Where a field took fewer, a route may have been included that the field would not recognise as one of its own, which is F6.

The correction is not to add or remove routes to make the counts vary. It is to check each entry against its field's literature and let the count fall where the record falls. Until that is done, the route lists should be read as the strongest five the writer could state, not as the field's full record, and any reader holding a field's literature can correct an entry in a few minutes.

## 3.5 Census

One hundred and forty-four entries, of which four are marked as not filling the form or as sitting at the boundary of the criteria. Thirty-one carry the mark **nye**, naming a route record stated from general knowledge rather than from a field's documented record; the nye marks concentrate in fast-moving empirical fields, biology, medicine and machine learning, and are sparse in mathematics, logic, physics and philosophy, where route endings are older and stably documented.

An entry not yet stated is stated later and met then.

## 3.6 What would make this collection worth less

If routes are missing. F2 is the falsifier that matters and the one anyone holding a field's literature can run: a route the field took, not listed here, that does not end where this document says routes end.

If both answers are not given at their strongest. An entry in which one side is stated weakly is a defect in the entry, and correcting it is ordinary work.

If the criteria never exclude. The form fails to fill in three ways, and five entries record such a failure, each entered and marked. No candidate has been excluded, so the criteria are stated and untested, and a form that admitted everything would carry less than this one claims to.
