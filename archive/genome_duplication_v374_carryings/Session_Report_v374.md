## Genome duplication

1. *(17 · 43)* Two goatsbeard species, *Tragopogon*, arose in eastern Washington within a century of their parents' arrival from Europe in the 1920s, each with twenty-four chromosomes, twice the parents'; from the first generation they lose and silence gene copies, and which copies go is repeatable and predicted (Pamela and Douglas Soltis, in Carrie Arnold, "Genome Duplication Is a Radical Evolutionary Gamble", Quanta Magazine, 2 September 2026, read whole).
2. *(23 · 41)* Diploid snowflake yeast under selection for larger multicellular size became tetraploid within fifty days, their cells larger and longer, tetraploidy continuing across nearly five thousand generations and conserved aneuploidy following (Tong and colleagues, Nature 639:691, 2025, at its abstract).
3. *(17 · 43)* The New Zealand mud snail *Potamopyrgus antipodarum* carries a whole-genome duplication after its split from *P. kaitunuparaoa*, its data best fit at a tetraploid model, 3,040 genes at two copies where relatives carry one (Jalinsky and colleagues, Genome Biology and Evolution 17(11), 2025, at its abstract); the duplication is under a million years old, about 60% of doubled genes already lost, and asexual females are triploid or tetraploid (the Quanta article).
4. *(17 · 23)* In yeast, extra copies of each gene hinder adapting to nystatin (Otto's group, cited at the Quanta article, not read).
5. *(23 · 41)* Multicellular yeast are found with a genome duplication and with the gene ACE2 lost, whose product the field names as separating daughter cells after division (Oud and colleagues, PNAS 2013, at its title).
6. *(17 · 53)* 132 ancient duplications across 470 flowering plants are reported clustering at the end-Cretaceous extinction, global cooling and the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum (Chen and colleagues, Cell 2026, at a news account); a reply names five conceptual and methodological issues against the association (Marcussen and Meseguer, bioRxiv 2026, at its abstract).

## Twinning, conceiving and pregnancy

7. *(37 · 43)* Identical twinning is three to four pairs per thousand births across the world, over time and at each maternal age; fraternal twinning ranges from under ten to about thirty per thousand deliveries across regions, rises with maternal age, runs in mothers' families, and is associated with variants near FSHB and SMAD3 (review, Human Reproduction 39(1), 2024, at passages).
8. *(17 · 37)* Identical twins carry a stable methylation signature from early development, 834 positions differing from fraternal twins (the same review).
9. *(23 · 37)* Identical twinning rises after assisted reproduction with blastocyst transfer and handling of the zona pellucida (Fertility and Sterility; Human Reproduction Update, at titles), and after ovulation induction alone, clomiphene included (Derom and colleagues, The Lancet 1987; Fertility and Sterility 2005, at titles).
10. *(23 · 37)* The timing of identical twins' parting names the membranes shared: early, each its own placenta and amnion; later, a shared placenta and two amnions, the most frequent; later again, a shared amnion; past about two weeks, conjoined; in vitro studies revise the early days (the field's classical account; Twin Research and Human Genetics, at its title).
11. *(29 · 37)* At a shared placenta, unbalanced vessel joinings run one twin's blood into the other, the twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome; coagulating the whole vascular equator reached fewer residual joinings than selective coagulation (The Lancet 2014, at its listing).
12. *(23 · 37)* Male fetal progenitor cells are found in maternal blood up to twenty-seven years after birth (Bianchi and colleagues, PNAS 93(2), 1996, at its title); marmoset fraternal twins continue chimeric through shared placental blood, germlines included (Ross, French and Ortí, PNAS 2007, at its title); a heifer born beside a bull twin is most often a sterile freemartin (the field's account).
13. *(37)* In side-by-side conjoined twins, one twin, most often the right-hand one, shows reversed or disordered left-right organ arrangement (Levin and colleagues, Nature 384:321, 1996, at its title); the popular mirror-image identical twins are met weakly at handedness studies (the field's account).
14. *(23 · 37)* A single early blastomere makes a whole small organism (Driesch); the nine-banded armadillo bears identical quadruplets from one blastocyst; the wasp *Copidosoma floridanum* develops broods of up to about three thousand clones from one egg, with sterile soldiers defending their siblings (the field's accounts).
15. *(37)* A twin continues no further in about 21–30% of multiple pregnancies, its tissue received by the other twin, the placenta or the mother (American Pregnancy Association); pine seeds cleave one zygote to several embryos and programmed cell death leaves one (Cell Death and Differentiation, at its title); sand tiger shark embryos from several fathers eat their siblings, two pups born, one per uterus (Chapman and colleagues, Biology Letters 2013, at its title).
16. *(43)* The world's twinning rate rose from 9.1 to 12.0 per thousand deliveries between 1980–1985 and 2010–2015, about 1.6 million pairs a year, Africa 17.1, North America 16.9, Europe 14.4, Asia 9.2, South America 9.3, the field accounting the rise to medically assisted reproduction about three times more than to later childbearing (Monden, Pison and Smits, Human Reproduction 36(6), 2021, at passages).
17. *(43)* In sub-Saharan Africa twins' first-month mortality is nearly five times singletons', about 1.7 times by twelve months, the difference near 2 per thousand by five years and gone after six (Monden and Smits, Lancet Global Health 2017; N-IUSSP summary, at passages).
18. *(37 · 43)* Mothers of twins in nineteenth-century Utah bore about two more children and lived longer after menopause (Robson and Smith 2011, at a news account); in pre-industrial Europe higher twinning propensity went with lower fertility (Rickard and colleagues, Nature Communications 2022, at its title); a 2024 paper names the last birth (Nature Communications, at its title).
19. *(23)* At sea urchins, starfish and frogs the egg membrane depolarizes within seconds of sperm fusion, a calcium-activated chloride current inverting the sign of its potential, and further sperm fuse at none; at the sand dollar about thirty seconds pass before the mechanical barrier rises (Jaffe, Nature 1976; Journal of General Physiology 2018 review, at passages).
20. *(7 · 23)* At mammals the sperm's phospholipase C zeta starts calcium oscillations in the egg; mice lacking it show weak or absent oscillations and polyspermy near 80% at higher sperm counts against 7.6% (Scientific Reports 2018, at passages); zinc sparks leave the egg, and the egg sheds Juno, the sperm's fusion partner (Nature 2014, at its title).
21. *(23 · 37)* One egg entered by two sperm, a tripolar spindle at the first division, and the embryo continuing as two gave twins sharing their mother's genome whole and their father's at about 78%, one placenta and two amnions (Gabbett and colleagues, New England Journal of Medicine 2019, at a news account).
22. *(17 · 23)* The egg completes its second meiotic division at sperm entry, releasing its second polar body; the two pronuclei continue apart, the paternal genome's protamines exchanged for histones and its DNA demethylated; at mouse zygotes the parental genomes continue on two spindles until the first division (Reichmann and colleagues, Science 2018, not fetched this session); the embryo's own genome opens at two cells in mice and about four to eight in humans (the field's account).
23. *(7 · 23)* A cytoplasmic oscillation continues in *Xenopus* eggs with the period of the division cycle even with no nucleus (Hara, Tydeman and Kirschner, PNAS 1980, at its title); the first twelve cleavages run fast and together until the midblastula transition, where cycles lengthen and part and the embryo's own transcription opens, at a ratio of nuclear material to cytoplasm (Newport and Kirschner, Cell 1982, at its title).
24. *(31 · 37)* The heart's own beat opens at about the third week after conception; the fetus secretes no melatonin, the mother's crossing the placenta; the infant's own day rhythms open over weeks to months, and the mother's milk carries melatonin at night (Journal of Physiological Anthropology 2022, at passages); mothers and three-month-old infants coordinate heart rhythms during face-to-face synchrony (Feldman and colleagues, 2011, at its title).

## Light and living rates

27. *(11 · 37)* Mimosa leaves continued their daily opening and closing in constant darkness (de Mairan, 1729); cyanobacterial KaiA, KaiB and KaiC with ATP in a test tube run KaiC's phosphorylation round at about a day (Nakajima and colleagues, Science 2005, not fetched this session); a blind Somalian cavefish runs its own rhythm near two days in darkness, entrained by feeding (Cavallari and colleagues, PLoS Biology 2011, not fetched this session).
28. *(below 5 · 11)* Photosystem II steps one state at each photon absorbed and releases oxygen at each fourth, oxygen from single flashes at the third flash and each fourth after (Joliot, 1969; Kok, 1970); phytochrome's red form turns to far-red-absorbing at red light and back at far-red, lettuce seeds germinating at the last flash's colour (Borthwick and colleagues, PNAS 1952).
29. *(37)* Arabidopsis uses its starch at night at a rate that runs out near dawn (Graf and colleagues, PNAS 2010, at its title).
30. *(37 · planet)* Hydrothermal vent mussels at 1,688 metres carry 12.4-hour tidal rhythms in 7.4% of transcripts, 2.6% daily, the vent's temperature and pressure oscillating at the tide's period; brought to a laboratory light-dark cycle, they turn daily (Mat and colleagues, Nature Communications 2020, at passages); vent fluid flow pulses with the tide; black smokers sound at hydrophones (PLoS One 2007, at its title).
31. *(below 5 · 23)* *Desulforudis audaxviator* lives alone at 1.5 to 3 kilometres, on hydrogen from water split by the radiation of uranium and thorium decay (encyclopedia account).
32. *(below 5 · 5)* In vacuum each colour of light meets c alike; each colour's frequency times its wavelength is c; a prism parts the colours at each colour's own index; atoms emit and absorb at the same lines; hydrogen's 21-centimetre line is the electron's spin turning from parallel to opposite the proton's (the field's accounts).
33. *(below 5 · 11 · 37)* Leaf pigments pass excitation toward the reaction centre, whose special pair of chlorophylls parts the charge across the thylakoid membrane; light beyond about 680 nm alone, or shorter red alone, gives less photosynthesis than the two together (Emerson, 1957); chlorophyll absorbs blue and red and reflects green, and fluoresces near 685 and 740 nm; a plant under neighbours' far-red-rich light elongates its stem (the field's accounts).
34. *(7 · 37)* A tree lays earlywood and latewood each year; potato tuber starch granules lay growth rings that continue in constant conditions (Pilling and Smith, Plant Physiology 2003, at passages); cereal endosperm starch rings follow day and night (Buttrose, 1960, not fetched this session).
35. *(37)* New leaves arrive at the shoot apex near 137.5 degrees from the last; a sunflower's seeds, each at about 137.5 degrees from the prior, open two families of visible spirals winding opposite ways at consecutive Fibonacci numbers (Vogel, 1979); the spiral's turn around the stem runs clockwise at some plants and counterclockwise at others (the field's account).
36. *(37)* A cucumber tendril, its tip gripping, coils into two helices of opposite hand joined at a perversion, net twist zero (Darwin, 1865; Gerbode and colleagues, Science 2012); a leaf folded along its midrib in the bud unfolds as it grows; a fern's fiddlehead uncoils as it grows.
37. *(7 · 11)* Living proteins are built of L-amino acids and nucleic acids of D-sugars, the double helix right-handed in its common form (the field's account).

## Two conceptions

38. *(37)* European brown hares conceive again at days 36 to 41 of a pregnancy, just before birth, at 54.4% of the pregnancies where it was possible in captivity and about 43% in the wild, early embryos in the oviduct beside fully grown fetuses, the second set implanting after the first litter is born, up to 35.4% more offspring a season (Roellig and colleagues, Nature Communications 2010, at passages).
39. *(37)* Two or more eggs of one cycle can be fertilized by sperm of one or different males, the egg viable 12 to 48 hours and sperm up to five days; heteropaternal at 2.4% of fraternal twins in paternity disputes; common in cats and dogs (encyclopedia account).
40. *(23 · 37)* Two zygotes can fuse early into one person, each cell line keeping its own zygote's genome, the tetragametic chimera (encyclopedia account; the Keegan and Fairchild cases); a woman with two blood types carried her twin brother's cells (1953, the same account).
41. *(17 · 23)* The fetal cells found in mothers of sons are found by the fetus's own Y chromosome, their own conception's genome (Bianchi and colleagues, 1996, at its title and method).

## A genome at each momentary

42. *(11 · 17)* DNA replicates semi-conservatively: each daughter double helix carries one strand of the parent and one strand made new against it (Meselson and Stahl, 1958, the field's account).
43. *(11 · 23)* In aneuploid yeast about 20% of the proteins from genes on an extra chromosome are attenuated, mostly subunits of multiprotein complexes, their mRNA and translation increased with the extra copy and the proteins degraded, accumulating within minutes when degradation is blocked; excess subunits continue only when joined to their complexes (Dephoure and colleagues, eLife 2014, at passages).

## Conjoined twins

44. *(37 · 43)* Conjoined twins are about 1 in 50,000 to 200,000 births, most live births female, about 3 to 1; about half are stillborn and a further third die within a day; thoracopagus about 28% with a heart shared, thoraco-omphalopagus about 18.5%, omphalopagus about 10% with separate hearts and about 82% survival, craniopagus about 6% (encyclopedia account).
45. *(29 · 37)* The union is always at homologous sites, the lateral halves of a structure of one embryo united to the opposing halves of the other, and only where surface ectoderm is absent (the heart's and septum transversum's primordia) or about to be removed (the neural tube, the oropharyngeal and cloacal membranes); the field now reads fusion of two monozygotic embryos more than an incomplete splitting of one (Spencer; "Two is a Crowd", Clinical Anatomy 2019, at passages).
46. *(37)* The Hensel twins, two heads on one body, each control one side, one arm and one leg each, and walk, swim and drive together; the craniopagus twins Krista and Tatiana Hogan share a bridge between their thalami and are reported to pass sensation between them (encyclopedia account).
47. *(37)* A singleton human body, its mouth, anus, two nostrils and four tear ducts opening into one connected passage, is a surface of seven holes at topology's count (the field's account).

## Genome size at its emanatings

57. *(17)* The maize genome's intergenic regions are packed with retrotransposons, many nested inside older ones; each element's two long terminal repeats (LTRs) are identical at insertion and diverge with time, dating it; all examined inserted within the last six million years and most within three, the genome growing from about 1,200 to about 2,400 megabases in about three million years (SanMiguel and colleagues, Nature Genetics 1998, at its summary).
58. *(17)* Recombination between an inserted retrovirus's two LTRs removes the internal region between them and leaves a single, solo LTR; solo LTRs are about 90% of human endogenous retrovirus insertions, some 3,173 near-intact sequences among about 770,551 loci (Frontiers in Genetics review 2024, at passages).
59. *(17 · 43)* In birds and mammals, large amounts of DNA gained through transposable elements and duplications go with DNA lost through deletion, genome size near constant, the field accounting gain and loss as its accordion (Kapusta, Suh and Feschotte, PNAS 2017, at its title, content not fetched this session).

## Stops conducting: ageing and disease

63. *(23 · 29)* A tree's ray parenchyma cells continue living for several years in the sapwood; toward the heartwood they vacuolate and make heartwood substances that pass into the tissues around; their nuclei condense and disappear at the sapwood and heartwood boundary, not all at once; heartwood carries no starch and conducts no water (Journal of Wood Science review 2024, at passages).
64. *(29 · 37)* Drought fills xylem vessels with air and their conducting stops; some grapevines refill vessels at root pressure; new xylem growth restores conducting; past a threshold of conductivity lost, angiosperm trees do not recover (Knipfer and colleagues, Plant, Cell and Environment 2015, and Urli and colleagues, Tree Physiology 2013, at their titles, contents not fetched this session).
65. *(11 · 31)* Connexin 43, the heart's main gap-junction protein, is reduced and moved from the intercalated discs to the cells' sides in ageing and diseased hearts, conduction slowing and arrhythmia following (Frontiers in Cardiovascular Medicine 2025 and Journal of Physiology 2025 reviews, at their titles).
66. *(29 · 31)* Rings cut from jellyfish and from turtle ventricle carried a circulating excitation (Mayer, 1906 to 1908); Mines in 1913 named reentry's conditions: an area of one-way block, a wave returning along its path to its origin, and interruption of the circuit at any point ending it (the field's review at PMC, at passages).
67. *(31 · built)* A defibrillating shock depolarizes the myocardium at once, refractory tissue included, and the circulating wavefronts end (Circulation Research 1996, at its title, and the field's account).
68. *(23 · 29)* Cancer cells lack the electrical coupling of the cells they come from, which the field names junctional communication (Loewenstein and Kanno, 1966); connexins are now read as conditional tumour suppressors, at some tumours furthering progression (Nature Reviews Cancer 2010, at passages).
69. *(23 · 37)* Ageing mice cleared of p16Ink4a-positive senescent cells lived longer at the median, with kidney, heart and fat function continuing (Baker and colleagues, Nature 2016, at its abstract).
70. *(23 · 37)* Old mice joined to young at one shared circulation had their muscle satellite cells' Notch activation, proliferation and regeneration restored, and their hepatocytes' proliferation (Conboy and colleagues, Nature 2005, at its abstract).
71. *(17 · 37)* The four Yamanaka factors expressed cyclically, two days on and five off, extended progeroid mice's lifespan and improved regeneration in old mice; expressed continuously they brought weight loss and high mortality within days; stronger cyclic expression brought teratomas (Ocampo and colleagues, Cell 2016, at passages).
72. *(23 · 37)* Hydra's mortality stays constant with age across years of observing, its cells dividing continuously and shed at its tentacles and foot, each animal's body replaced cell by cell at least sixty times in four years (Martínez, Experimental Gerontology 1998, and Schaible and colleagues, PNAS 2015, at their titles and a news account).
73. *(23 · 37)* A *Turritopsis* medusa under stress transforms back into a polyp, its cells transdifferentiating (Piraino and colleagues, Biological Bulletin 1996, at its title and the encyclopedia's account).
74. *(23 · 37)* A cold-sensitive strain of *Hydra oligactis* moved to cold shifts from budding to making gametes and ages, genes of stem cell maintenance turning down and its stem cells declining; a cold-resistant strain shifts at none and does not age (Tomczyk and colleagues, GeroScience 2020, at its abstract).
