- *Science explanation*: Nozawa and colleagues name phospholipase C zeta 1 "the long-sought sperm-borne oocyte activation factor" and attribute the entry of several sperm to delayed blocks at the zona and at the egg's surface, the surface block being the more critical in the living animal; Bianchi and colleagues link the rapid shedding of Juno to the egg-surface block to polyspermy.

### Twins of one egg and two sperm

- *Natural observation*: Twins of one chorion, a boy and a girl, were found by genotyping of amniotic fluid to be identical at their mother's genome and to have "chimerically shared 78% of their paternal genome"; among 968 dizygotic twin pairs screened no other such pair was found ([Gabbett, Laporte, Sekar, Nandini, McGrath, Sapkota, Jiang, Zhang, Burgess, Montgomery, Chiu and Fisk, 2019, New England Journal of Medicine](https://andrologyacademy.net/highlighted-papers/basic-andrology/63-molecular-support-for-heterogonesis-resulting-in-sesquizygotic-twinning), read at its abstract as a society's page gives it, and at the university's news account at https://www.qut.edu.au/news?id=140848; the journal's own page did not open).
- *Science explanation*: The writers name the pair sesquizygotic and propose "chimerism arising at the juncture of zygotic division, termed heterogonesis" as the likely first step; the news account has one egg fertilised by two sperm at once, three sets of chromosomes divided among groups of cells, and the groups then parting into two embryos; the spindle of three poles and the two amnions are as the field's account has it.

### Two parental genomes apart through the first division

- *Natural observation*: As the field's account has it, the egg completes its second meiotic division at sperm entry and releases its second polar body, the paternal genome's protamines are exchanged for histones and its DNA demethylated, and the embryo's own genome opens at two cells in mice and about four to eight in humans; in mouse zygotes two separate bipolar spindles form, one around each pronucleus and each gathering one parental genome, the two spindles align and the genomes lie side by side before the first anaphase, apart through the first mitosis, and the genomes mix rapidly in the later divisions imaged up to eight cells; spindles failing to align give two-cell embryos with one or two binucleated blastomeres ([Reichmann and colleagues, bioRxiv 198275, version 4](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/198275v4), read at its preprint's full text; its printing at Science in 2018 was not opened).
- *Science explanation*: The writers link the failure of the two spindles to align to errors seen in embryos of fertilization in a dish.

### An egg's cycle with no nucleus, and its transition

- *Natural observation*: The title of the first publishing reads "A cytoplasmic clock with the same period as the division cycle in Xenopus eggs" (Hara, Tydeman and Kirschner, 1980, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 77(1), read at its title alone in another paper's reference list, its own pages not opened), and the oscillation's continuing with no nucleus is as the field's account has it; the Xenopus embryo "undergoes 12 rapid synchronous cleavages followed by a period of slower asynchronous divisions", the midblastula transition ([Newport and Kirschner, 1982, Cell 30(3), 675–686](https://bionumbers.hms.harvard.edu/bionumber.aspx?id=101950), read at an excerpt of its abstract at a database's page).
- *Science explanation*: As the abstract's excerpt has it, the timing of the transition does not depend on the number of cell divisions, on the time from fertilization, or on a mechanism numbering DNA modifications; the placing of the transition at a ratio of nuclear material to cytoplasm and the opening of the embryo's own transcription at it are as the field's account has it.

### The conceived's beat and day opening later

- *Natural observation*: The heart's own beat opening at about the third week after conception is as the field's account has it, the review read naming no week; "The human fetus and neonate do not secrete melatonin, as synthesis begins post-natally", and the mother's melatonin "crosses the placenta rapidly and unaltered to provide photoperiod information"; 24-hour rhythms in heart rate have been found in sheep, monkey and human fetuses; infants are born with no overt rhythm in melatonin or cortisol, an infradian sleep-wake rhythm emerges by 5 weeks, sleep episodes consolidate at about 15 weeks, and by 6 to 9 months most infants sleep through the night; "night milk is comprised of increased levels of leptin, melatonin, and tryptophan" (Wong, Wright, Spencer, Vetter, Hicks, Jenni, LeBourgeois and colleagues, 2022, Journal of Physiological Anthropology 41, 22, read at its full text at https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40101-022-00294-0); mothers and their 3-month-old infants in face-to-face interaction coordinated heart rhythms within lags of less than one second, and the concordance rose during episodes of affect and vocal synchrony ([Feldman, Magori-Cohen, Galili, Singer and Louzoun, 2011, Infant Behavior and Development 34(4), 569–577](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/mother-and-infant-coordinate-heart-rhythms-through-episodes-of-in-3/), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: Feldman and colleagues argue that humans, as other mammals, can influence an attachment partner's physiological processes through coordinated "visuo-affective social signals"; the review has the mother's melatonin giving the fetus photoperiod information.

### Daily rhythms continuing with no day arriving

- *Natural observation*: As an encyclopedia's pages have it, de Mairan in 1729 placed plants in constant darkness and the daily opening and closing of their leaves continued, one page naming the plant Mimosa pudica and another the heliotrope, his colleague Marchant making the result known in Paris in 1729 (de Mairan, 1729, read at an encyclopedia's pages, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circadian_rhythm and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Jacques_d%27Ortous_de_Mairan); as the same encyclopedia has it, the cyanobacterial clock can be reconstituted in a test tube from the three proteins KaiA, KaiB and KaiC alone and sustains a 22-hour rhythm over several days upon the addition of ATP (Nakajima and colleagues, 2005, Science, not opened); the blind Somalian cavefish Phreatichthys andruzzii, isolated beneath the desert for an estimated 1.4 to 2.6 million years, has a clock that "is not regulated by light" and "retains a food-entrainable clock that oscillates with an infradian period", near 47 hours at 22 °C, 43 at 25 °C and 38 at 29 °C ([Cavallari, Frigato, Vallone, Fröhlich, Lopez-Olmeda, Foà, Berti, Sánchez-Vázquez, Bertolucci and Foulkes, 2011, PLoS Biology 9(9), e1001142](https://journals.plos.org/plosbiology/article?id=10.1371/journal.pbio.1001142), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: Cavallari and colleagues found frameshift mutations truncating two non-visual opsins, melanopsin (Opn4m2) and TMT-opsin, and argue that these opsins carry light to the clocks of fish tissues; de Mairan, as the encyclopedia has it, inferred no clock within the plant and concluded that the plants were able "to sense the Sun without ever seeing it".

### Oxygen at each fourth flash, and the last flash's colour

- *Natural observation*: Lettuce seeds given brief alternating flashes of red and far-red light "respond only to the final exposure", germinating after a last red flash and lying dormant after a last far-red one (Borthwick, Hendricks and colleagues, 1952, read at a textbook's page, https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/life11e/life11e_ch36_22.html; the paper itself, at the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, gave a browser check and was not opened); in isolated spinach chloroplasts given a series of short flashes after darkness, the oxygen yield of the third flash exceeds that of the fourth, and the yields repeat in a "cycle of four" whose swing damps over about three to four cycles ([Kok, Forbush and McGloin, 1970, Photochemistry and Photobiology 11, 457–475](https://www.life.illinois.edu/govindjee/Electronic%20Publications/Historical%20Papers/Kok_1970.pdf), read at its full text; Joliot, Barbieri and Chabaud, 1969, cited within it, not opened).
- *Science explanation*: Kok and colleagues fit a linear four-step scheme, S0 to S4, each flash adding one oxidizing equivalent at an independent trapping centre and S4 releasing oxygen and going back to S0, with about 45% of the centres at S0 and 55% at S1 after ten minutes of darkness and about 10% misses and 5% double hits at each flash, and they set this against Joliot's assumption that all centres go to the ground state in darkness; as the textbook has it, phytochrome's form Pr is converted to Pfr on absorbing red light, and Pfr is converted back to Pr on absorbing far-red light.

### Two colours together at a leaf

- *Natural observation*: As an encyclopedia's page has it, Emerson described in 1957 that light of wavelengths beyond 680 nanometres alone gives a low rate of photosynthesis and that, with light of shorter wavelength given together with it, the rate exceeds the sum of the two rates apart; as another of its pages has it, chlorophyll fluorescence is measured as a ratio of emission at 685 and 740 nanometres; chlorophyll's absorbing of blue and red and giving back of green, and a plant's elongating of its stem under far-red-rich light from plants beside it, are as the field's account has it ([Emerson, 1957, Science 125, 746](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emerson_effect), read at an encyclopedia's page that cites it; Emerson, Chalmers and Cederstrand, 1957, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 43, 133–143, named for the same effect at a letter by Govindjee, 2020, Journal of Plant Science Research 36, 1–4, read at https://www.life.illinois.edu/govindjee/recent_papers_files/1_Gov_On_Emerson%282020%29.pdf; neither of Emerson's papers was opened).
- *Science explanation*: As the encyclopedia has it, Emerson concluded that two photosystems work together, one driven by light of shorter and one by light of longer wavelength; Govindjee's letter has the effect leading to the scheme of two light reactions and two pigment systems, the Z-scheme; the passing of excitation toward a reaction centre's special pair of chlorophylls and the parting of charge across the thylakoid are as the field's account has it.

### A second conception late in a hare's pregnancy

- *Natural observation*: Captive European brown hares (Lepus europaeus) copulated again between days 36 and 41 of a pregnancy lasting 41.9 ± 0.7 days, and a second conception succeeded from day 37 to day 41 alone, artificial insemination at days 34 to 36 giving no pregnancy; it occurred in "54.4% (37 out of 68)" of the pregnancies at which fertilization before delivery was possible; among free-ranging females prepartum copulation and potential superfetation were found in 42.9%, three of seven late-pregnant females; early embryonic stages lay in the oviduct beside fully developed fetuses in the uterus, and "Subsequent implantation occurred after parturition"; the females could produce "up to 35.4% more offspring per breeding season", the expected output rising from 14.4 to 19.5 offspring a season ([Roellig, Goeritz, Fickel, Hermes, Hofer and Hildebrandt, 2010, Nature Communications 1, 78](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms1079), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: The writers name it superfetation, placed within their own scheme as superconception, and call it "most likely an evolutionary adaptation", one moving maternal care from after the birth to before it.

### Two fathers at one cycle; two zygotes one person

- *Natural observation*: Superfecundation is "the fertilization of two or more ova from the same menstrual cycle"; the egg is viable 12 to 48 hours after ovulation and sperm live up to five days within the female's body; in one study (Wenk and colleagues, 1992, Acta Geneticae Medicae et Gemellologiae, as the page cites it) twins of two fathers were 2.4% of dizygotic twins whose parents were in paternity suits, and the page has it "common in animals such as cats and dogs" (an encyclopedia's page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfecundation). A tetragametic chimera forms by "fertilizing two separate ova by two sperm, followed by aggregation of the two at the blastocyst or zygote stages", and "Each population of cells keeps its own character" (an encyclopedia's page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimera_(genetics)); Karen Keegan's tetragametic chimerism was identified in 2002 in preparation for a kidney transplant, tissue typing having suggested she was not the mother of two of her three children; Lydia Fairchild, 2002, was shown to carry two sets of DNA, one of which could have produced her children; a 1953 British Medical Journal report had a woman with blood of two types, which "resulted from her twin brother's cells living in her body" ([Wikipedia, "Human chimera"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_chimera), read at an encyclopedia's page).
- *Science explanation*: The field names the first superfecundation, and heteropaternal superfecundation for babies of different fathers, and names the second tetragametic chimerism; the 1953 woman's two blood types are given as her twin brother's cells living in her body.

### Each daughter helix, one old strand and one made

- *Natural observation*: Escherichia coli grown for several generations on heavy nitrogen (15N) and then moved to 14N gave, on a caesium chloride density gradient, DNA of one intermediate density after one replication and, after two replications, equal amounts at the intermediate density and at the 14N density; each of the two daughter double helices carries one strand of the parent and one strand newly made, and no daughter helix is of two old strands and none of two made strands ([Meselson and Stahl, 1958, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 44(7), 671–682](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meselson%E2%80%93Stahl_experiment), read at an encyclopedia's page; the journal's own page did not open).
- *Science explanation*: The field names the copying semiconservative, the scheme Watson and Crick proposed; the intermediate band after one replication excluded the conservative scheme and the two bands after two replications excluded the dispersive one; that the parent's two strands part and each is the template for one further strand, base by base, is as the field's account has it.

### Conjoined twins, their numbers and their sexes

### Two selves at one body, each its own side

- *Natural observation*: Abby and Brittany Hensel, born 1990, are "dicephalic parapagus twins", two heads on one torso; "Each twin controls one arm and one leg", "The sense of touch of each is restricted to her body half", and together they "walk, run, swim, play volleyball and the piano, and ride a bicycle normally", type and drive a car (an encyclopedia's page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abby_and_Brittany_Hensel). ### A snail's hand from its mother, reversed by hand

### One genome, two coats

- *Natural observation*: CC, born 22 December 2001 at College Station, Texas, and called "the first cloned pet", was a brown tabby and white domestic shorthair, and her genetic donor Rainbow was a "calico domestic longhair" (an encyclopedia's page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CC_(cat)); the publishing's own page says "This kitten's coat-coloration pattern is not a carbon copy of its genome donor's" ([Shin, Kraemer, Pryor, Liu, Rugila, Howe, Buck, Murphy, Lyons and Westhusin, 2002, Nature 415, 859](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature723), read at its abstract).

### A bamboo's clones flowering together across the world

- *Natural observation*: Phyllostachys bambusoides seeded en masse in China "In the years 919 and 1114" and in Japan "Sometime between 1716 and 1735, and again in 1844-1847", and in the late 1960s transplanted stocks in England, Alabama and Russia flowered again beside the Japanese parent stock, which Janzen reads as "a seeding cycle of about 120 years"; of other bamboos' periods he writes "These range from 3 to 120 years; most are between 15 and 60 years"; clones of the species from various parts of Japan planted in Chiba Prefectural Forest in 1923 all flowered in the 1960s; Argentinian Guadua trinii seedlings planted in an arboretum and in a city park flowered 30 years later in synchrony with each other and with their siblings at home; Chusquea abietifolia carried from Jamaica to England in the 1880s flowered in synchrony with its parent cohort; such bamboos are "usually 'monocarpic' or semelparous plants in that they seed once and die" ([Janzen, 1976, Annual Review of Ecology and Systematics 7, 347–391](https://gwern.net/doc/biology/1976-janzen.pdf), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: Janzen gives two: the timing is "set by an internal physiological calendar rather than an external weather cue", and he remarks that "only 1/365 as many physiological events need occur if years are counted"; the synchrony is maintained by seed predators, satiated by the mast crop and removing the seed set early or late, a hypothesis he calls "probably untestable in most contemporary habitats".

### A winter carried through a season's divisions

- *Natural observation*: The level of stable silencing of the floral repressor FLC "depends quantitatively on the length of the prior cold", through the fraction of cells switched to the silenced state (Angel, Song, Dean and Howard, 2011, Nature 476, 105–108, read at its abstract, https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10241); the silencing is "stable and mitotically heritable" (an encyclopedia's page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernalization); in Arabidopsis thaliana "FLC is epigenetically silenced by prolonged cold in a process called vernalization" and "the locus is reactivated before the completion of seed development", each generation needing the cold anew, and a mutation in the demethylase ELF6 impaired the reactivation and passed a partly vernalized state to the following generation ([Crevillén, Yang, Cui, Greeff, Trick, Qiu, Cao and Dean, 2014, Nature 515, 587–590](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature13722), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: The field names it vernalization, "the induction of a plant's flowering process by exposure to the prolonged cold of winter": cold brings Polycomb silencing of FLC with trimethylation of histone 3 at lysine 27, each cell silenced or not, and Angel and colleagues call it "the perception and memory of winter in plants"; removal of the silent chromatin marks at FLC in embryogenesis prevents inheritance of the vernalized state.

### A genome doubling in length by insertions

- *Natural observation*: Of 23 retrotransposons near the maize adh1 gene, 17 were dated, with two more from unlinked sites, by comparing the divergence of each element's long terminal repeats with the divergence of adh1 between maize and sorghum; "All retrotransposons examined have inserted within the last six million years", "most in the last three million years"; the writers suggest that the insertions "increased the size of the maize genome from approximately 1200 Mb to 2400 Mb" in the last three million years, and report a higher mutation rate in retrotransposons than in genes; the nesting of the elements is the subject of the paper's fifth reference, "Nested retrotransposons in the intergenic regions of the maize genome" ([SanMiguel, Gaut, Tikhonov, Nakajima and Bennetzen, 1998, Nature Genetics 20, 43–45](https://www.nature.com/articles/ng0998_43), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: The writers date each element by the divergence between its long terminal repeats and give the genome's growth to retrotransposon insertions as a suggestion; that an element's two repeats are identical at insertion is as the field's account has it.

### Solo repeats left by inserted retroviruses

- *Natural observation*: Solo long terminal repeats are given as "accounting for about 90% of all HERV insertions" (the review citing Subramanian and colleagues, 2011, and Thomas and colleagues, 2018); "There are 770,551 HERV loci in human genome" by RepeatMasker annotation, "only 3,173 relatively intact HERV sequences were identified" (the review citing Vargiu and colleagues, 2016), and human endogenous retroviruses are about 8% of the human genome ([Chen, Huang, Wang, Wang, Jia and Li, 2024, Frontiers in Genetics 15, 1358078](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fgene.2024.1358078/full), read at its full text).

### Genome length at gaining and losing

- *Natural observation*: Across the genomes of 10 mammal and 24 bird species spanning about 100 million years, the DNA gained through transposable-element expansion varied widely between lineages and was offset by DNA lost through deletion, mostly deletions larger than 10 kilobases, genome size varying little ([Kapusta, Suh and Feschotte, 2017, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 114, E1460–E1469](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5338432/), read at its abstract and significance statement); Laupala crickets, with a genome 11 times larger than Drosophila's, lose DNA more than 40 times more slowly ([Petrov, Sangster, Johnston, Hartl and Shaw, 2000, Science 287, 1060–1062](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.287.5455.1060?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: Kapusta and colleagues name it an "accordion" model of genome size evolution, gains by transposable-element expansion balanced by large segmental deletions, and propose that extensive DNA loss, more than low transposable-element activity, has left flying birds and bats with more compact genomes than their flightless relatives; Petrov and colleagues give the slower loss as consistent with their hypothesis that part of the variation in genome size comes from differing patterns of insertion and deletion mutations.

### Ploidy rising at speciation, and the largest genome

- *Natural observation*: 15% of angiosperm and 31% of fern speciation events are accompanied by a ploidy increase, estimates about four times higher than earlier ones, with 35% of species within genera polyploid (n = 1,506) ([Wood, Takebayashi, Barker, Mayrose, Greenspoon and Rieseberg, 2009, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106, 13875–13879](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1073/pnas.0811575106?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record); whole-genome duplication is rare among mammals, as the field's account has it; the monocot Paris japonica has a genome of 1C = 152.23 picograms by flow cytometry, the largest eukaryotic genome known at its publishing and 15% above the earlier largest estimate, widening the known range of genome size to about 2,400-fold among angiosperms and about 66,000-fold among eukaryotes, and, as the field's account has it, that is near 149 billion base pairs, about fifty times a human's, in an octoploid ([Pellicer, Fay and Leitch, 2010, Botanical Journal of the Linnean Society 164, 10–15](https://academic.oup.com/botlinnean/article/164/1/10/2418538), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: Wood and colleagues found no direct evidence that polyploid lines diversify faster and conclude that the wide occurrence of polyploidy does not come from raised diversification rates in polyploid lineages; the field names genome size's following no measure of organismic complexity "the C value paradox", the words of Petrov and colleagues' abstract (Science 287, 1060–1062, 2000); the rarity among mammals is left open, as the field's account has it.

### Two developmental paces from each cell's own speeds

- *Natural observation*: Presomitic-mesoderm-like cells induced from mouse and human pluripotent stem cells show oscillating expression of HES7, the core gene of the segmentation clock, with periods of 2–3 hours in mouse and 5–6 hours in human; swapping the HES7 loci between the two genomes did not much change the periods, and degradation and the delays in HES7 expression are slower in the human cells, as are reactions of several other genes and of another cell type ([Matsuda, Hayashi, García-Ojalvo, Yoshioka-Kobayashi, Kageyama, Yamanaka, Ikeya, Toguchida, Alev and Ebisuya, 2020, Science 369, 1450–1455](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.aba7668?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record); mouse and human embryonic stem cells differentiating to motor neurons in vitro differ twofold in pace, the human cells having a twofold greater protein stability and cell-cycle duration ([Rayón, Stamataki, Pérez-Carrasco, Garcia-Perez, Barrington, Melchionda, Exelby, Lázaro, Tybulewicz, Fisher and Briscoe, 2020, Science 369, eaba7667](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.aba7667?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at the journal's summary of it, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: Matsuda and colleagues place the period difference at cell-autonomous differences in the speeds of biochemical reactions, their title's "differential biochemical reaction speeds", and at no sequence difference at the HES7 loci, a mathematical model with the measured parameters accounting for the 2–3-fold difference; Rayón and colleagues found that neither the cells' sensitivity to signals nor the sequence of gene-regulatory elements explained the pace, which went with protein stability and cell-cycle duration.

### An elephant's many copies of one guarding gene

- *Natural observation*: African elephants have at least 20 copies (40 alleles) of TP53, 19 of them retrogenes with evidence of transcription, against 1 copy (2 alleles) in humans; elephants' cancer mortality is estimated at 4.81% (95% CI 3.14%–6.49%) against 11% to 25% in humans; after ionizing radiation elephant lymphocytes undergo apoptosis at 14.64% against 7.17% in healthy human controls and 2.71% in patients with Li-Fraumeni syndrome ([Abegglen, Caulin, Chan, Lee, Robinson, Campbell, Kiso, Schmitt, Waddell, Bhaskara, Jensen, Maley and Schiffman, 2015, JAMA 314(17), from page 1850](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1001/jama.2015.13134?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: None is given beside the observing; the writers say the lower-than-expected cancer rate is potentially related to the multiple copies of TP53, and that elephant cells show a greater apoptotic response to DNA damage than human cells.

### A bowhead whale's faithful repair

- *Natural observation*: The bowhead whale has a maximum lifespan above 200 years and a mass above 80,000 kilograms; its primary fibroblasts needed fewer oncogenic hits for malignant transformation than human fibroblasts; its cells show greater capacity and fidelity of DNA double-strand-break repair and lower mutation rates than cells of other mammals; the cold-inducible RNA-binding protein CIRBP is highly expressed in its fibroblasts and tissues, whale CIRBP raised non-homologous end joining and homologous recombination in human cells, and CIRBP overexpression in Drosophila lengthened lifespan and raised resistance to irradiation ([Firsanov, Zacher, Tian, Sformo and colleagues, Gorbunova last-named, 2025, Nature 648, 717–725](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09694-5), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: The writers conclude that the whale, in place of added tumour-suppressor genes, maintains genome integrity through enhanced DNA repair, a strategy that "does not eliminate damaged cells but faithfully repairs them", and that this may contribute to its long life and low cancer incidence.

### Cells' rates converging once out of the body

- *Natural observation*: Metabolic rate does not scale with body mass in cultured mammalian cells, as the title has it (Brown, Gratton and Stuart, 2007, American Journal of Physiology – Regulatory, Integrative and Comparative Physiology 292, R2115–R2121, read at its title and citation in Semantic Scholar's record, its abstract not opened); cellular metabolic rates in vivo scale as body mass to the power −1/4, and rates of cells in culture "converge to a single predicted value for all mammals regardless of size" ([West, Woodruff and Brown, 2002, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 99, supplement 1, 2473–2478](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1073/pnas.012579799?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: West and colleagues suggest that aerobic energy transformation at each level of biological organization is limited by the transport of materials through fractal-like networks, a single three-quarter-power law describing whole mammals, isolated cells, mitochondria and respiratory-complex molecules, and the convergence of cultured cells to one value being a prediction of that model.

### A receiving cortex leading its sender in phase

- *Natural observation*: In monkeys performing a cognitive task, a dominant influence from one area of sensorimotor cortex to another was found with either a negative or a positive time delay, recordings of Brovelli and colleagues, 2004, as the abstract of the first publishing has them; that publishing is a two-region model reproducing the experimental delay times and coherence spectra ([Matias, Gollo, Carelli, Bressler, Copelli and Mirasso, 2014, NeuroImage 99, 411–418](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/modeling-positive-granger-causality-and-negative-phase-lag-betwee/), read at its abstract, in a university's record); a later modelling publishing, at three scales from three-neuron motifs through spiking populations to neural mass models, has the phase difference between sender and receiver shrinking, and the pair passing from delayed to anticipated synchronization, as the receiver's own frequency, measured uncoupled, rises ([Dalla Porta, Matias, dos Santos, Alonso, Carelli, Copelli and Mirasso, 2019, Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience 13, 41](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnsys.2019.00041/full), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: Matias and colleagues model it as "Anticipated Synchronization", a regime of one-directional influence from sender to receiver in which the receiver leads the sender in time, stable in a sender-receiver pair with the receiver under negative delayed self-feedback, and suggest that the primate cortex may operate in this regime; Dalla Porta and colleagues propose faster internal dynamics of the receiver, set by its inhibitory loop and its external current, as the main mechanism.

### Heart cells at one beat, and its opening place

- *Natural observation*: Rat heart cells separated by trypsin and grown attached to glass in a liquid medium contract periodically like a whole beating heart, at up to 150 beats a minute, the rate changed by cardiac drugs and by metabolic substrates and inhibitors ([Harary and Farley, 1960, Science 131, 1674–1675](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.131.3414.1674?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record); as the field's account has it, such single cells beat each at its own rate and, growing into contact, beat together and organize into beating fibres (Harary and Farley, 1963, Experimental Cell Research 29, 451–465, read at its title, its abstract not opened); in the dog, with electrodes over the rostral, middle and caudal sinus node, the internodal pathways and the His bundle, the pacemaker lay within the sinoatrial node 87% of the control time, most often at the middle electrode; right sympathetic stimulation shifted it rostrally within the node, and right vagal and left sympathetic stimulation shifted it caudally within the node and to sites beyond the node ([Goldberg, 1975, American Journal of Physiology 229, 1116–1123](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1152/ajplegacy.1975.229.4.1116?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: None is given beside the observing; Goldberg adds that slight shifts of the pacemaker within the sinus node changed the pattern of atrial excitation.

### Labelled cells lit, and a learned fear again

- *Natural observation*: In mice, dentate gyrus neurons active during fear conditioning were labelled with channelrhodopsin-2 and later lit in a different context, and freezing rose only during the light; it did not rise in unconditioned mice labelled alike or in conditioned mice labelled with EYFP alone; the light was 473 nanometres, 15-millisecond pulses at 20 hertz, across a 12-minute session of four 3-minute epochs, off, on, off, on; the main group had virus and optical fibre at one side of the brain and a separate group at both; ten light-responsive dentate neurons from nine mice spiked reliably and precisely time-locked to the pulses' onset ([Liu, Ramirez, Pang, Puryear, Govindarajan, Deisseroth and Tonegawa, 2012, Nature 484, 381–385](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3331914/), read at its abstract and its full text). Under amnesia induced by a protein-synthesis inhibitor, the raised synaptic strength and dendritic spine density of consolidated engram cells were absent, and optogenetic activation of those cells still brought retrieval, with the engram cells' specific connectivity retained ([Ryan, Roy, Pignatelli, Arons and Tonegawa, 2015, Science 348, 1007–1013](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.aaa5542?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: Liu and colleagues give a sparse and specific ensemble contributing to a "memory engram" as sufficient for the recall; Ryan and colleagues name the cells engram cells and propose that a specific pattern of connectivity among them may be what stores the information, strengthened synapses contributing to retrieval.

### Which cells join an ensemble, and two ensembles linking

- *Natural observation*: In mice, changing CREB function in subsets of lateral amygdala neurons changed the probability of a neuron's recruitment into the fear trace ([Han, Kushner, Yiu, Cole, Matynia, Brown, Neve, Guzowski, Silva and Josselyn, 2007, Science 316, 457–460](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.1139438?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record); ablating the neurons overexpressing CREB after learning, by an inducible diphtheria-toxin strategy, blocked the fear's expression robustly and persistently, and ablating a like portion of random lateral amygdala neurons did not ([Han, Kushner, Yiu, Hsiang, Buch, Waisman, Bontempi, Neve, Frankland and Josselyn, 2009, Science 323, 1492–1496](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.1164139?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record); neurons are recruited to a trace by relative neuronal excitability immediately before training, as the title has it (Yiu, Mercaldo, Chen, Richards, Rashid, Hsiang, Pressey, Mahadevan, Tran, Kushner, Woodin, Frankland and Josselyn, 2014, Neuron 83, 722–735, read at its title, its abstract not opened). In mice the CA1 ensembles of two contexts learned within a day overlap more than those of two contexts a week apart; fear learned at one context transfers to the other within a day and not across a week; aged mice, with lower CA1 excitability, show no overlap and no transfer, and raising excitability and activating a shared CA1 ensemble during the two exposures rescued the linking ([Cai, Aharoni, Shuman and colleagues, Silva last-named, 2016, Nature 534, 115–118](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature17955), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: Han and colleagues name the first neuronal competition and selection, eligible neurons selected by their relative CREB activity at learning; Cai and colleagues name the "memory allocation hypothesis", learning raising neuronal excitability for a time and biasing a later learning toward the same ensemble, the overlapping ensembles linking the two.

### Co-activity again in sleep, and synapses' proteins replaced

- *Natural observation*: In three rats, hippocampal place cells that fired together at particular locations during spatial tasks fired together more in the slow-wave sleep after the tasks than in the sleep before them; cells inactive in behaviour, or active with non-overlapping spatial firing, showed no such rise, and the effect declined gradually across each sleep session after behaviour ([Wilson and McNaughton, 1994, Science 265, 676–679](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.8036517?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record); as the field's account has it, the molecules of synapses are replaced within days to weeks and learned behaviour lasts for years ([Crick, 1984, Nature 312, 101](https://www.nature.com/articles/312101a0), read at its title, its text not opened).
- *Science explanation*: Wilson and McNaughton suggest that the effect may result from synaptic changes during waking experience, information acquired in active behaviour being re-expressed in hippocampal circuits in sleep, consistent with some theories of consolidation; for Crick's page none could be read.

### Forgetting steep and then slow, and its rate changed

- *Natural observation*: Ebbinghaus's saving in relearning series of 13 syllables fell from about 58% after a third of an hour to 33.7% after one day, about a fourth after six days and about a fifth after 31 days, forgetting "very rapid at the beginning of the process and very slow at the end" ([Ebbinghaus, 1885, "Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology", chapter VII, translated by Ruger and Bussenius, 1913](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Ebbinghaus/memory7.htm), read at its full text, its tables being images and not read); learning spaced across sessions lasts longer than the same learning massed together, as the field's account has it; in Drosophila, inhibiting Rac slows the decay of early learning from a few hours to more than a day and blocks interference-induced forgetting, and raised Rac activity in mushroom body neurons speeds the decay, acquisition unchanged ([Shuai, Lu, Hu, Wang, Sun and Zhong, 2010, Cell 140, 579–589](https://repository.cshl.edu/id/eprint/15528/), read at its abstract, in its laboratory's repository).
- *Science explanation*: Ebbinghaus gives a logarithmic formula, b = 100k/((log t)^c + k) with k = 1.84 and c = 1.25, as a shorthand for his own results and no general law; Shuai and colleagues give the small G protein Rac as driving a forgetting mechanism under both passive decay and interference-induced forgetting, independent of the Rutabaga adenylyl cyclase pathway of acquisition, and propose its remodelling of the actin cytoskeleton as contributing to the erasure.

### RNA from a trained sea slug, injected

- *Natural observation*: RNA from the central nervous system of Aplysia given long-term sensitization training by tail shocks, injected into untrained animals, induced sensitization; the RNA-induced sensitization, like the trained one, required DNA methylation, the inhibitor RG-108 preventing it; in culture the RNA of trained animals raised the excitability of sensory neurons, and not of motor neurons, dissociated from naive animals ([Bédécarrats, Chen, Pearce, Cai and Glanzman, 2018, eNeuro 5(3), ENEURO.0038-18.2018](https://www.eneuro.org/content/5/3/ENEURO.0038-18.2018), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: The writers conclude that "RNA is sufficient to generate an engram" for long-term sensitization and give the results as support for a nonsynaptic, epigenetic model of storage.

### Spacers joining at one end of an array

- *Natural observation*: As the field's account has it, bacterial CRISPR arrays receive new spacers at the leader end, the array carrying past infections in their order, and spacers at the array's oldest end are lost across generations; after viral challenge, bacteria integrated new spacers derived from phage genomic sequences, and removing or adding particular spacers changed the cell's phage-resistance phenotype ([Barrangou, Fremaux, Deveau, Richards, Boyaval, Moineau, Romero and Horvath, 2007, Science 315, 1709–1712](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1126/science.1138140?select=title,publication_year,biblio,authorships,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract, in OpenAlex's record).
- *Science explanation*: The writers conclude that CRISPR, with its associated cas genes, provides resistance against phages, the specificity determined by sequence similarity between spacer and phage; the array as a record of past infections in their order is the field's account and is absent from the abstract.

### Yeast cells oscillating together above a density

- *Natural observation*: Vibrio fischeri luminesce only at high cell density, as the field's account has it; in suspensions of the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae the glycolytic oscillations of the population appear above a critical cell density and vanish below it, and the vanishing occurs "synchronously in all cells and not by desynchronization" ([De Monte, d'Ovidio, Danø and Sørensen, 2007, PNAS 104(47), 18377–18381](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2141785), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: The writers name the yeast's transition "dynamical quorum sensing", the cells coupled through acetaldehyde, a small molecule that they release and that diffuses between them; for Vibrio fischeri the field names quorum sensing through an autoinducer molecule the cells release and detect, as the field's account has it.

### Receptors made before an antigen arrives

- *Natural observation*: The adaptive immune system carries, before an antigen arrives, lymphocytes with receptors assembled at each cell, as the field's account has it; sequencing of T-cell receptor beta chain genes in healthy humans gave, as a lower bound, an estimated 60–120 million different beta chain gene sequences among the naive CD4 and CD8 T cells of young adults aged 20–35, a richness that declined modestly in adults aged 70–85 while clonal expansion among their naive cells rose ([Qi, Liu, Cheng, Glanville, Zhang, Lee, Olshen, Weyand, Boyd and Goronzy, 2014, PNAS 111(36), 13139–13144](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4246948/), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: The receptors are assembled by V(D)J recombination, gene segments joined anew at each cell, and an arriving antigen selects the clones that bind it, as the field's account has it; the writers account the rising clonality of aged naive cells to uneven homeostatic proliferation.

### Opposed colours at one cell, and a tunnelling electron

- *Natural observation*: Colour opponent cells of the retina and lateral geniculate nucleus respond with opposite polarities to opposed colours, red against green and blue against yellow, as the field's account has it (Hering; De Valois and colleagues, 1966, not opened); beside it, from measured electron transfer in biological and chemical systems, a protein is described as "a uniform electronic barrier to electron tunnelling", and a change of 20 ångströms in the distance between donor and acceptor within a protein changes the transfer rate about 10^12-fold ([Moser, Keske, Warncke, Farid and Dutton, 1992, Nature 355, 796–802](https://www.nature.com/articles/355796a0), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: The field names the first opponent processing, as the field's account has it; for the second the writers give distance, free energy and reorganization energy as sufficient to define the rate and the direction of biological electron transfer.

### A stem-cell deficiency beside a hydra's ageing

- *Natural observation*: In Hydra oligactis, the species with a cold-sensitive strain that ages after moving to cold, autophagy is deficient in the epithelial stem cells of the ageing animals, as the field's account has it (Tomczyk and colleagues, 2020, Development, not opened, its title alone read in a search's listing of https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6983715); about 1,800 Hydra polyps of twelve cohorts, each polyp in its own glass dish, were followed from March 2006 for almost ten years, 3.9 million Hydra-days in all, and mortality stayed constant with age at about 0.6 percent a year while reproduction did not decline with age ([Schaible, Scheuerlein, Dańko, Gampe, Martínez and Vaupel, 2015, PNAS, online 7 December 2015](https://www.demogr.mpg.de/en/news_press/news/press/forever_young_4396.htm), read at its institute's news account).
- *Science explanation*: Tomczyk's title names the first, "Deficient autophagy in epithelial stem cells drives aging in the freshwater cnidarian Hydra"; for the constant mortality the news account gives the polyp's many stem cells, the replacing of all its cells about each four weeks and the expelling of mutated cells, the animal one that "does not senesce by accumulating damages and mutations".

### A funeral practice ended, and a disease ending decades after

- *Natural observation*: Kuru spread among the Fore of Papua New Guinea through funerary cannibalism, about 200 people dying of it each year around 1957; the Fore largely stopped the practice around 1960, the incubation averages 10–13 years and is estimated at up to 50 years or more, and the last person with kuru died in 2009 (an encyclopedia's page, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuru_(disease)); bovine spongiform encephalopathy spread among cattle fed meat-and-bone meal made from cattle, as the field's account has it; among more than 3,000 people of the Eastern Highlands, 709 of them participants in mortuary feasts and 152 of those later dead of kuru, the prion protein variant G127V was common in the area of high incidence and absent from kuru patients and from unexposed populations (Mead, Whitfield, Poulter and colleagues, 2009, New England Journal of Medicine 361(21), 2056–2065, read at its abstract through https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1056/NEJMoa0809716?select=title,publication_year,biblio,primary_location,abstract_inverted_index); mice expressing human prion protein with wild-type and V127 forms together resisted kuru and classical CJD prions, and mice with V127 alone resisted all prion strains tested ([Asante, Smidak, Grimshaw and colleagues, 2015, Nature 522, 478–481](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature14510), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: A prion, the host's own protein PrP at another fold, converts the normal protein to that fold; Mead and colleagues conclude that the 127V variant is a resistance factor selected during the epidemic, and Asante and colleagues report that V127 protein is not converted to the prion form and inhibits, by its dose, the propagation of wild-type prions.

### A soil bacterium using L-glucose

- *Natural observation*: L-glucose, the opposite-handed form of D-glucose, is not transported by the D-glucose carrier and contributes virtually no energy in test diets, L-monosaccharides passing as laxatives (ScienceDirect's topic page, at passages); a soil bacterium was found using L-glucose (Shimizu and colleagues, 2012, not fetched in that session).
- *Science explanation*: The field has the D-glucose carrier and the body's enzymes coupling with one hand alone; for the bacterium none is given. No page of this publishing opened at this gathering; the two lines are as session v374's report has them.

### One zygote to several embryos, several embryos to fewer

- *Natural observation*: The nine-banded armadillo bears identical quadruplets from one blastocyst, and the wasp Copidosoma floridanum develops broods of up to about three thousand clones from one egg, with sterile soldiers, as the field's account has it; a twin vanishes in an estimated 21–30% of multifetal pregnancies, and "The fetal tissue is absorbed by the other twin, multiple, placenta or the mother" (American Pregnancy Association, read at its page, https://americanpregnancy.org/healthy-pregnancy/multiples/vanishing-twin-syndrome/); sand tiger shark embryos of several fathers eat their siblings and two pups are born, one in each uterus, as the field's account has it (Chapman, Wintner, Abercrombie, Ashe, Bernard, Shivji and Feldheim, 2013, Biology Letters 9, its title alone read, "The behavioural and genetic mating system of the sand tiger shark, Carcharias taurus, an intrauterine cannibal"); in seeds of the pine Pinus sylvestris several embryos arise from one zygote, one becomes dominant, and the cells of each subordinate embryo die in order from the base beside the suspensor toward the apex ([Filonova, von Arnold, Daniel and Bozhkov, 2002, Cell Death and Differentiation 9(10), 1057–1062](https://www.nature.com/articles/4401068), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: At the pine the writers report programmed cell death as the major mechanism that eliminates the subordinate embryos, the female gametophyte also undergoing it after the dominant embryo is selected; the rest are given with none.

### Twinning's two rates, at their publishing

- *Natural observation*: Identical twinning is 3–4 pairs in each thousand births, fairly similar across the world and over the years and stable with maternal age, and identical twins rarely cluster in families; twin birth rates of 2010–2015 ranged from under 10 in each thousand deliveries in poorer regions of Latin America and South-East Asia to up to 30 in several African countries, and fraternal twinning is familial and associated with maternal age and parity; in blood DNA of five cohorts, a total N of 5,723, methylation at 834 CpG positions differed between identical twins and fraternal twins, 497 positions lower and 337 higher in identical twins, with a mean correlation of 0.85 between samples drawn about five years apart ([van Dongen, Hubers and Boomsma, 2024, Human Reproduction 39(1), 35–42](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10767898/), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: Of identical twinning the writers give as a prevailing hypothesis that "MZ twinning occurs at random"; with fraternal twinning the review associates variants near FSHB and in SMAD3, from Mbarek and colleagues' 2016 study of 1,980 mothers of spontaneous fraternal twins against 12,953 controls, and a later meta-analysis adds GNRH1, FSHR, ZFPM1 and IPO8.

### Identical twinning rising after assisted conceiving

- *Natural observation*: A rise of identical twinning after ovulation induction alone, clomiphene among it, is as the field's account has it ("Increased monozygotic twinning rate after ovulation induction", 1987, The Lancet 329(8544), 1236–1238, given in the batch as Derom and colleagues, its title alone read; "Risk factors for monozygotic twinning after in vitro fertilization: a systematic review and meta-analysis", 2019, Fertility and Sterility 111(2), 302–317, its title alone read; a Fertility and Sterility paper of 2005, not found); across 42 articles on IVF and ICSI searched to March 2017, reported rates of identical twinning ranged from zero to 13.2%, and blastocyst transfer carried higher odds of it than cleavage-stage transfer, odds ratio 2.18 with a 95% interval of 1.93–2.48, while the evidence on handling of the zona pellucida was limited and conflicting (["Determinants of monozygotic twinning in ART: a systematic review and a meta-analysis", 2018, Human Reproduction Update 24(4), 468–483](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1093/humupd/dmy006?select=title,publication_year,biblio,primary_location,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: None is given beside the observing; the review's writers say the mechanisms of identical twinning after blastocyst transfer remain to be clarified; of twins sharing one chorion a looser inner cell mass separating at the blastocyst's forming or hatching is offered, as the field's account has it.

### The world's twinning rate, and twins' early deaths

- *Natural observation*: In sub-Saharan Africa, across 90 surveys of 30 countries with 1,685,110 singleton and 56,597 twin livebirths of 1995–2014, twins' neonatal mortality was 5.0 times singletons' (95% interval 4.5–5.6), and under-5 mortality in 2009–14 was 213.0 in each thousand twin livebirths against 63.5 for singletons, one-fifth of twins dying before age 5 (Monden and Smits, 2017, Lancet Global Health 5(7), e673–e679, read at its abstract through https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1016/S2214-109X(17)30197-3?select=title,publication_year,biblio,primary_location,abstract_inverted_index); the ratio is about 1.7 by twelve months of age and the difference is gone after six years, as the field's account has it (N-IUSSP summary, not opened); the world's twinning rate rose from 9.1 to 12.0 twin deliveries in each thousand deliveries between 1980–1985 and 2010–2015, about 1.66 million twin deliveries a year in the later period, with rates of 17.1 in Africa, 16.9 in North America, 14.8 in Oceania, 14.4 in Europe, 9.3 in South America and 9.2 in Asia ([Monden, Pison and Smits, 2021, Human Reproduction 36(6), 1666–1673](https://academic.oup.com/humrep/article/36/6/1666/6168974), read at its full text).
- *Science explanation*: The writers give the great increase of medically assisted reproduction as an important reason for the rise, later childbearing also contributing in high-income countries, and cite Pison and colleagues (2015) for the estimate that "the effect of MAR is on average about three times larger than the effect of delayed childbearing"; of twins' excess mortality Monden and Smits write that it "cannot be explained by common risk factors for under-5 mortality, including birthweight".

### Multicellular yeast with one separating gene lost

- *Natural observation*: Diploid snowflake yeast under selection for larger multicellular size became tetraploid within the first 50 days and remained tetraploid about 950 days more, some 5,000 generations, across 10 replicate populations (Tong, Datta, Cheng and colleagues, 2025, Nature 639, 691–699, read at its abstract, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08689-6); in an earlier publishing, laboratory evolution of a haploid Saccharomyces cerevisiae strain in bioreactor batch cultures, in two independent experiments of fewer than 500 generations, yielded multicellular, fast-sedimenting clusters; both evolved lines had a whole-genome duplication and each a different frameshift mutation "in a stretch of eight adenosines in ACE2", the two mutant alleles in the haploid parent gave only slow-sedimenting clusters of a few cells, and a diploid strain built with two mutant ace2 alleles gave the complete phenotype ([Oud, Guadalupe-Medina, Nijkamp, de Ridder, Pronk, van Maris and Daran, 2013, PNAS 110(45), E4223–E4231](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1073/pnas.1305949110?select=title,publication_year,biblio,primary_location,abstract_inverted_index), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: ACE2 "encodes a transcriptional regulator involved in cell cycle control and mother-daughter cell separation", and the writers conclude that whole-genome duplication and a frameshift mutation in ACE2 are together sufficient for the phenotype; of the snowflake yeast Tong and colleagues give the larger, longer tetraploid cells, forming larger clusters, as the immediate advantage that selection for size maintained.

### Water conducted through cells already dead

- *Natural observation*: A growing tree's sapwood is called living wood and moves water from the roots to the leaves, though its water-conducting cells are functionally dead; heartwood forms from it by "a genetically programmed process that occurs spontaneously", with "Some uncertainty" over whether the wood dies in the forming, and a tree "can thrive with its heart completely decayed" (an encyclopedia's page on wood, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood); the xylem's tracheary elements, its tracheids and vessel elements, "are dead by maturity and no longer have living contents", water passes through the emptied cells, and by 1891 Eduard Strasburger "had shown that the transport of water in plants did not require the xylem cells to be alive" ([Wikipedia, "Xylem"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xylem), read at an encyclopedia's page).
- *Science explanation*: None is given beside the dead cells' conducting; for the water's rise the encyclopedia gives the cohesion-tension theory, evaporation at the leaf cells' surfaces pulling the water upward, its molecules cohering by hydrogen bonds and adhering to the cell walls.

### The appendix arising many times and seldom lost

- *Natural observation*: Across 533 mammal species the caecal appendix "has evolved a minimum of 29 times, possibly as many as 41 times", with at most 12 losses and none of them unambiguously supported by the tree; presence of an appendix is correlated with a higher concentration of lymphoid tissue in the caecum and with the thickness of the caecal apex, and with none of the dietary variables ([Smith, Parker, Kotzé and Laurin, 2017, Comptes Rendus Palevol 16, 39-57](https://sciencepress.mnhn.fr/sites/default/files/articles/pdf/comptes-rendus-palevol2017v16f1a07.pdf), read at its full text). A biofilm of microbes, mucus and immune molecules lines the bowel and is most pronounced in the appendix (the Duke account below); that paper's reference list has a 2011 report titled "The appendix may protect against Clostridium difficile recurrence" (Im and colleagues, read at its title alone).
- *Science explanation*: The 2017 writers say the correlations support "the hypothesis of an adaptive immune function" for the caecum and appendix as one complex, and call the appendix "apparently a safe house for bacteria"; the safe-house proposal is that beneficial gut bacteria survive in the slender pouch, whose position makes it hard for material to enter while the bowel empties, and repopulate the gut after a severe diarrhoea, a deduction its writers call circumstantial ([Bollinger, Barbas, Bush, Lin and Parker, 2007, Journal of Theoretical Biology, online](https://corporate.dukehealth.org/news/appendix-isnt-useless-all-its-safe-house-bacteria), read at a university account).

### Embryos germinating within the fruit, no dormancy entered

- *Natural observation*: Fruits (siliques) of Arabidopsis thaliana of differing ages were cut from the plant and cultured on a solidified medium of mineral salts with vitamins, myo-inositol and 3 per cent sucrose; embryos at early and unripe stages completed their forming and then germinated within the fruit, ripe green embryos in green ovules germinated with no further growing, siliques with brown ovules and yellowish ripe embryos showed no such germinating, and abscisic acid added to the medium inhibited it ([Raghavan, 2002, Induction of vivipary in Arabidopsis by silique culture: implications for seed dormancy and germination, American Journal of Botany, from page 766, volume 89 as the field's account has it](https://manuscript.isc.ac/Inventory/10/33286.htm), read at its abstract in an index record, the publisher's page not opened).
- *Science explanation*: The germinating within the fruit is named vivipary; the abstract speaks of embryos that "have not become dormant at the time of culture of siliques", of silique culture circumventing "a cold treatment and light exposure normally required for germination", and of the abscisic acid finding as "Suggestive of a role for abscisic acid in preventing vivipary on the mother plant".

### A dry seed wetted: respiration within minutes

- *Natural observation*: In intact seeds of Arabidopsis given water, fluorescent biosensors showed adenosine triphosphate rising and oxygen being used within minutes of the wetting, and the mitochondria's matrix shifting from a more oxidised to a more reduced thiol condition; 412 redox-switched cysteine peptides were identified, and "The redox state across all Cys peptides was shifted toward reduction from 27.1% down to 13.0% oxidized thiol"; seeds lacking the most strongly responding redox proteins (glutathione reductase 2, NADPH-thioredoxin reductase a/b, thioredoxin-o1) showed higher respiration and disturbed dynamics of the citric acid cycle, and "Germination in aged seeds was strongly impaired" ([Nietzel and colleagues, the first writer's name as the field's account has it, 2020, Redox-mediated kick-start of mitochondrial energy metabolism drives resource-efficient seed germination, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 117(1), 741–751, doi:10.1073/pnas.1910501117](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31871212/), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: "We identify a global operation of thiol redox switches that is required for optimal usage of energy stores by the mitochondria to drive efficient germination"; the title names a "kick-start" of mitochondrial energy metabolism, the results of the seeds lacking the proteins are read as suggesting a less efficient use of the energy stores, and "Seeds preserve a far developed plant embryo in a quiescent state".

### Calcium risings and adenosine triphosphate at a fertilising egg

- *Natural observation*: In mouse eggs at fertilising, calcium was followed by a dye's fluorescence and adenosine triphosphate by the luminescence of firefly luciferase; "At fertilization an initial increase in ATP levels occurs with the first Ca2+ transient, with a second increase occurring about 1 h later", the estimate going "from a prefertilization concentration of 1.9 mM to a peak value of 3 mM", and "ATP levels returned to prefertilization values as the Ca2+ oscillations terminated"; "An increase in ATP also occurred with other stimuli that increase Ca2+", "it was blocked when Ca2+ oscillations were inhibited by BAPTA injection", and "an ATP increase was not seen when eggs were activated by cycloheximide" ([Campbell and Swann, 2006, Ca2+ oscillations stimulate an ATP increase during fertilization of mouse eggs, Developmental Biology 298(1), 225–233, doi:10.1016/j.ydbio.2006.06.032](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/482/), read at its abstract in the writers' university record, the publisher's page not opened).
- *Science explanation*: The calcium oscillations are said to be necessary and sufficient for the rise of adenosine triphosphate at fertilising, a rise the abstract gives as a transient one.

### Two parents' genomes apart in the earliest human embryo

- *Natural observation*: Single cells of human embryos ahead of implanting were sequenced by scCOOL-seq for "a genome-wide map of DNA methylation and chromatin accessibility at single-cell resolution"; "Unlike in mice, the chromatin of the paternal genome is already more open than that of the maternal genome" at "the mid-zygote stage in humans", and "this state is maintained until the 4-cell stage"; genes with high variations in DNA methylation and those with high variations in chromatin accessibility "tend to be two different sets"; "1,797 out of 5,155 (35%) widely open chromatin regions in promoters closed when transcription activity was inhibited" ([Li, Guo, Gao, Ren, Yuan, Yan, Li, Lian, Li, Hu, Gao, Wen, Tang and Qiao, 2018, Single-cell multi-omics sequencing of human early embryos, Nature Cell Biology 20, 847–858, doi:10.1038/s41556-018-0123-2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41556-018-0123-2), read at its abstract).
- *Science explanation*: The closing of the open promoter regions under inhibited transcription is given as "indicating a feedback mechanism between transcription and open chromatin maintenance".
